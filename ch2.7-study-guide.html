Section 7A — Intermolecular Forces (Spec 7.1–7.6)

7.1 London Forces (Induced Dipole–Dipole Forces)

Exam-ready definition:

London forces are weak intermolecular forces caused by the movement of electrons in an atom or molecule creating an instantaneous/temporary dipole, which induces a dipole in a neighbouring atom or molecule. The resulting attraction between the temporary dipole and the induced dipole is the London force. They exist between ALL atoms and molecules, whether polar or non-polar.

Key points to include for full marks: - Electrons are in constant motion → instantaneous/temporary dipole - This induces a dipole in an adjacent molecule - Attraction between temporary dipole and induced dipole - Present in ALL molecules (and atoms)

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Jan 2024 Q4 (1 mark) — Which has strongest London forces?
    MS: Straight-chain alkane with most electrons (CH₃(CH₂)₇CH₃). More electrons → larger electron cloud → more easily polarised → stronger London forces.

  2. Oct 2024 Q19(a)(i) (2 marks) — Describe how London forces arise.
    MS: Instantaneous/temporary dipole from asymmetrical electron distribution → induced dipoles in neighbouring molecules.

  3. Oct 2024 Q19(a)(ii) (1 mark) — Why HI has stronger London forces than HCl.
    MS: HI has more electrons.

  4. Jun 2022 Q16(d) (4 marks) — Explain HX boiling point trend.
    MS: (1) All HX have London forces; (2) London force strength increases with number of electrons down the group; (3) Only HF has hydrogen bonding; (4) Hydrogen bonding is much stronger than London forces.

Common mistakes: - Writing "van der Waals forces" instead of "London forces" (Edexcel uses "London forces") - Saying "more electrons = stronger forces" without mentioning polarisability - Confusing London forces with permanent dipole–dipole forces - Saying London forces are only in non-polar molecules (they exist in ALL molecules)

7.2 Permanent Dipole–Dipole Forces

Exam-ready definition:

Permanent dipole–dipole forces are intermolecular forces of attraction between the permanent dipoles of polar molecules. They arise because polar molecules have an uneven distribution of electron density, creating a permanent δ⁺ end and a permanent δ⁻ end. The δ⁺ end of one molecule attracts the δ⁻ end of another.

Key points: - Only exist in POLAR molecules - Caused by a permanent uneven distribution of electron density - Stronger than London forces but weaker than hydrogen bonding - PH₃ is an important example: non-polar P–H bonds (Δχ ≈ 0) but a polar molecule due to the lone pair

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Jun 2023 Q6 (1 mark) — Butane vs propanal b.p.
    MS: Propanal has a C=O bond that creates permanent dipole–dipole forces, which are stronger than the London forces in butane.

  2. Jan 2023 Q4 (1 mark) — Which has only London forces?
    MS: CF₄ is tetrahedral and symmetrical → no permanent dipole → London forces only. (HF has H-bonding; OF₂ and PF₃ are polar.)

  3. Oct 2023 Q4 (1 mark) — Table of IMF for BF₃, CH₄, NH₃, H₂S.
    MS: H₂S has London forces + permanent dipole–dipole, but NOT hydrogen bonding (S is not electronegative enough).

Common mistakes: - Confusing which molecules are polar vs non-polar (symmetrical molecules like CF₄, BF₃ are non-polar despite polar bonds) - Saying PH₃ has hydrogen bonding (P is NOT electronegative enough) - Forgetting that permanent dipole–dipole forces are additional to London forces (all molecules have London forces too)

7.3 Hydrogen Bonding

Exam-ready definition:

Hydrogen bonding is a strong type of intermolecular force that occurs when a hydrogen atom is covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (fluorine, oxygen, or nitrogen) and is attracted to a lone pair of electrons on a highly electronegative atom (F, O, or N) in a DIFFERENT molecule.

Key points for full marks: - H must be covalently bonded to F, O, or N (NOT to C, P, S, Cl) - H is attracted to a lone pair on F, O, or N in a different molecule - Strongest type of intermolecular force (but NOT a covalent bond) - Gives anomalous properties: high b.p. of H₂O, HF, NH₃; lower density of ice

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Jan 2024 Q1 (1 mark) — Which exhibits hydrogen bonding?
    MS: HF (F is highly electronegative, H bonded directly to F).

  2. Jan 2023 Q5 (1 mark) — Which forms hydrogen bonding?
    MS: (CH₃)₃COH — only option with O–H bond. N not bonded to H in option A; F not bonded to H in option B; C=O in option D has no O–H.

  3. Jan 2023 Q6 (1 mark) — HF b.p. anomaly.
    MS: HF > HI > HBr > HCl. HF highest due to H-bonding; then London forces dominate: HI > HBr > HCl.

  4. Jun 2023 Q20(b) (6 marks, QWC) — Compare IMF in H₂ and NH₃.
    MS indicative points: (1) Both form London forces; (2) Temporary dipole induces dipole in adjacent molecule; (3) Weaker attraction in H₂ (electron cloud less easily polarised); (4) NH₃ forms hydrogen bonds (N very electronegative); (5) H-bond between N lone pair and δ⁺H of different molecule; (6) NH₃ liquefies more easily.

  5. Jun 2023 Q7 (1 mark) — Which diagram shows H-bond between methanol and water?
    MS: H must be bonded to O (not C); O—H⋯O angle ~180°.

Common mistakes: - Saying H bonded to C can hydrogen bond (it CANNOT — Jan 2023 Q5 specifically tests this) - Confusing intramolecular hydrogen bonds with intermolecular ones - Saying H-bonding is a covalent bond or a type of covalent bonding - Forgetting to specify that the H is attracted to a LONE PAIR on the electronegative atom - Writing "H-bond between H and O" without specifying it is a lone pair on O

7.4–7.5 Predicting Physical Properties from IMF

Key definitions:

Boiling point is the temperature at which the vapour pressure of a liquid equals the external pressure. Stronger intermolecular forces → higher boiling point.

Solubility depends on whether the solute–solvent intermolecular forces are similar in strength to the solute–solute and solvent–solvent forces they replace ("like dissolves like").

Rules for predicting b.p.: 1. H-bonding > permanent dipole–dipole > London forces 2. More electrons → stronger London forces → higher b.p. (for molecules with same type of IMF) 3. Less branching → greater surface area → stronger London forces → higher b.p. 4. More H-bonding sites → stronger overall IMF → higher b.p.

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Jun 2024 Q4(a) (1 mark) — Structural isomer with highest b.p.
    MS: Least branched chain (greater surface area for London forces).

  2. Jun 2024 Q4(b) (1 mark) — Compound with highest b.p. (diol vs mono-alcohol).
    MS: Butane-1,4-diol — two H-bonding sites per molecule.

  3. Jan 2023 Q19(b)(ii) (2 marks) — Explain b.p. difference between two alcohols.
    MS: (The less branched one) has stronger London forces due to greater surface area / less branching. Must specify "London forces" and "greater surface area". MS explicitly says "Do not award just stronger intermolecular forces."

  4. Jan 2023 Q19(b)(iii) (3 marks) — Solubility in ethanol vs water.
    MS: (1) London forces between B and ethanol; (2) H-bonds between B and water; (3) Comparison — London forces B–EtOH stronger than/similar to those in B; OR H-bonds B–H₂O weaker than H-bonds in water.

  5. Oct 2023 Q19(d) (6 marks, QWC) — Why I₂ is more soluble in hexane than water.
    MS: (1) I₂ has only London forces; (2) Water forms H-bonds (strongest IMF); (3) Hexane has only London forces; (4) I₂–hexane London forces similar in strength to those broken → dissolves; (5) I₂ cannot break water H-bonds → does not dissolve.

  6. Jun 2023 Q22(c)(iii) (2 marks) — Why propane-1,2,3-triol is viscous.
    MS: Three OH groups → forms 3 H-bonds per molecule → strong IMF → viscous.

Common mistakes: - Writing "stronger intermolecular forces" without specifying which type (MS penalises this) - Forgetting that ALL molecules have London forces (even those with H-bonding or pd-pd forces) - Saying a molecule has "more H-bonds" when it has the same number of H-bonding sites but different branching - Confusing solubility reasoning: must compare forces formed vs forces broken, not just list forces

7.6 Ion-Dipole Interactions

Exam-ready definition:

Ion-dipole interactions are the attractive forces between an ion and the permanent dipole of a polar molecule. When an ionic compound dissolves in a polar solvent (such as water), the δ⁺ hydrogen atoms of water are attracted to negative ions (anions) and the δ⁻ oxygen atoms of water are attracted to positive ions (cations). These interactions help to separate the ions from the ionic lattice, enabling dissolution.

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Jun 2024 Q2 (1 mark) — NaCl dissolving in water — what IMF?
    MS: Ion-dipole interactions.

  2. Oct 2024 Q16(c)(i) (2 marks) — Draw ion-dipole interaction diagram (Mg²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ with water).
    MS: H atoms of water near SO₄²⁻; O atom of water near Mg²⁺. Must show δ⁺/δ⁻ on water at least once.

Common mistakes: - Confusing ion-dipole with hydrogen bonding - Not showing partial charges (δ⁺/δ⁻) on the water molecule - Putting the wrong end of water near the wrong ion

Section 8A — Redox Chemistry (Spec 8.1–8.10)

8.1 Oxidation and Reduction (Definitions)

Exam-ready definitions:

Oxidation is the LOSS of electrons OR an INCREASE in oxidation number.

Reduction is the GAIN of electrons OR a DECREASE in oxidation number.

Oxidising agent is a species that CAUSES oxidation by GAINING electrons (it is itself reduced). Its oxidation number DECREASES.

Reducing agent is a species that CAUSES reduction by LOSING electrons (it is itself oxidised). Its oxidation number INCREASES.

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Jun 2022 Q2 (1 mark) — Identify the oxidising agent.
    MS: The species that gains electrons / has its oxidation number decreased.

  2. Jan 2024 Q21(b)(i) (2 marks) — Ca + Cl₂: explain redox.
    MS: Ca oxidised 0→+2 AND Cl₂ reduced 0→−1. Both oxidation number changes required.

  3. Oct 2023 Q19(b) (2 marks) — Show MnO₂ + HCl is redox using oxidation numbers.
    MS: Mn: +4 → +2 (reduced); Cl: −1 → 0 (oxidised). Both changes must be shown.

Note: Jan 2023 Q18(a) on disproportionation of Cl₂ is listed under §8.9–8.10 (Disproportionation) below — see there for the full MS.

Common mistakes: - Writing "the oxidising agent loses electrons" (wrong — it GAINS electrons) - Confusing oxidation/oxidising agent (the agent does the OPPOSITE of what it's named) - Not showing BOTH oxidation and reduction changes when asked to "show it is redox" - Confusing the species being oxidised with the oxidising agent

8.2–8.5 Oxidation Numbers

Rules for assigning oxidation numbers:

Rule Statement
1 The oxidation number of an element in its standard state is 0 (e.g., Cl₂, Na, O₂)
2 For simple monatomic ions, the oxidation number equals the charge (e.g., Na⁺ = +1, O²⁻ = −2)
3 In compounds, oxygen is usually −2 (exception: −1 in peroxides like H₂O₂, Na₂O₂)
4 In compounds, hydrogen is usually +1 (exception: −1 in metal hydrides like NaH, CaH₂)
5 Fluorine is ALWAYS −1
6 The sum of all oxidation numbers in a neutral compound equals 0; in an ion, equals the charge

Roman numeral naming convention:

The Roman numeral in a compound name (e.g., chlorate(V), manganate(VII)) indicates the oxidation number of the specified element.

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Jan 2023 Q7 (1 mark) — Oxidation number of V in VO²⁺.
    MS: V = +4 (O is −2, overall +2: x + (−2) = +2 → x = +4).

  2. Jan 2023 Q8 (1 mark) — Formula of potassium manganate(VI).
    MS: K₂MnO₄ (Mn(VI): x − 8 = −2 → x = +6; K is +1, so K₂MnO₄).

  3. Jan 2024 Q6 (1 mark) — In which compound is an oxidation number −1?
    MS: CH₄ has no element at −1 (H = +1, C = −4). Correct options include HF (F = −1), NaH (H = −1), H₂O₂ (O = −1).

  4. Jun 2024 Q18(b)(ii) (1 mark) — Oxidation number of Cl in ClO₂.
    MS: Cl = +4 (2 × (−2) + x = 0 → x = +4).

  5. Jun 2022 Q4 (1 mark) — Name KClO₂ using oxidation state.
    MS: Potassium chlorate(III). Cl: K = +1, 2O = −4, so Cl = +3.

  6. Jun 2023 Q8 (1 mark) — Which pair has the same oxidation state?
    MS: Cr in K₂Cr₂O₇ and K₂CrO₄ — both have Cr = +6.

  7. Jun 2023 Q9 (1 mark) — Formula of barium(II) ferrate(VI).
    MS: BaFeO₄ (Ba²⁺ and FeO₄²⁻ with Fe = +6).

  8. Jun 2024 Q18(b)(i) (1 mark) — Roman numerals in chlorate(V) and chlorate(VII).
    MS: Oxidation number of chlorine (+5 and +7).

Common mistakes: - Forgetting the peroxide exception: O = −1 in H₂O₂, Na₂O₂ (tested in Jan 2024 Q6) - Forgetting the hydride exception: H = −1 in NaH, CaH₂ (tested in Jan 2024 Q6) - Assigning oxidation numbers to elements in their standard state as non-zero - Confusing oxidation number with charge (e.g., in CO₃²⁻, C is +4 not +2) - Not showing working for oxidation number calculations

8.6–8.8 Half-Equations and Balancing Redox Equations

Exam-ready definition:

A half-equation shows either the oxidation or reduction process separately, including electrons. To combine half-equations into a full redox equation: (1) balance all atoms except H and O; (2) balance O with H₂O; (3) balance H with H⁺ (in acid) or OH⁻ (in alkali); (4) balance charge with electrons; (5) multiply half-equations so electrons cancel; (6) add the half-equations and cancel common species.

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Oct 2023 Q6 (1 mark) — Balance O₂ in alkaline conditions.
    MS: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (n = 4 electrons).

  2. Jun 2022 Q16(b) (4 marks) — Explain why IO₃⁻ + 5I⁻ + 6H⁺ → 3I₂ + 3H₂O is NOT disproportionation.
    MS: I in IO₃⁻ = +5 (reduced to 0 in I₂); I in I⁻ = −1 (oxidised to 0 in I₂). Not disproportionation because two DIFFERENT species are oxidised and reduced — not the SAME species.

  3. Jun 2024 Q18(b)(iii) (2 marks) — Deduce oxidation number range for disproportionation.
    MS: Compound A disproportionates to give ClO₂ (Cl = +4, reduced) and HClO₄ (Cl = +7, oxidised); for disproportionation, Cl in compound A must lie strictly between these values, so the oxidation number is +5 or +6.

Note: Oct 2024 Q19(b)(i) on Cl₂ + H₂O disproportionation is listed under §8.9–8.10 (Disproportionation) below — see there for the full MS.

Common mistakes: - Not multiplying half-equations to make electrons equal before combining - Forgetting to add water to balance oxygen in acidic or alkaline conditions - Identifying comproportionation (reverse) as disproportionation - Not stating that the SAME element must be both oxidised AND reduced for disproportionation - Writing "redox" alone instead of "disproportionation" (explicitly ignored in Jun 2023 MS)

8.9–8.10 Disproportionation

Exam-ready definition:

Disproportionation is a redox reaction in which the SAME element is simultaneously oxidised AND reduced. The oxidation numbers of this element in the products are both higher and lower than its oxidation number in the reactant.

Key disproportionation reactions to know:

Reaction Element Oxidised to Reduced to
Cl₂ + H₂O → HClO + HCl Cl (0) Cl (+1) in HClO Cl (−1) in HCl
Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O Cl (0) Cl (+1) in NaClO Cl (−1) in NaCl
3NaClO → 2NaCl + NaClO₃ Cl (+1) Cl (+5) in NaClO₃ Cl (−1) in NaCl
2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ O (−1) O (0) in O₂ O (−2) in H₂O

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Jan 2023 Q18(a) (3 marks) — Disproportionation of Cl₂ forming Ca(ClO)₂.
    MS: Cl oxidised from 0 to +1 (in ClO⁻) and Cl reduced from 0 to −1 (in Cl⁻).

  2. Jun 2023 Q22(c)(i) (3 marks) — Disproportionation of Cl₂ in bleach.
    MS: Cl (0) → +1 in HClO/ClO⁻ (oxidised) and Cl (0) → −1 in HCl/Cl⁻ (reduced). Must state "disproportionation" not just "redox".

  3. Jan 2024 Q24(b)(iii) (2 marks) — Disproportionation of NaClO.
    MS: 3NaClO → 2NaCl + NaClO₃. Cl from +1 to −1 (reduced) and +1 to +5 (oxidised).

  4. Oct 2024 Q19(b)(i) (3 marks) — Cl₂ + H₂O disproportionation.
    MS: Same element (Cl) both oxidised (0 → +1 in HOCl) and reduced (0 → −1 in HCl).

  5. Oct 2023 Q7 (1 mark) — Identify disproportionation reaction.
    MS: S₂O₃²⁻ reaction where S goes from +2 to +4 and 0 simultaneously.

Common mistakes: - Confusing disproportionation with comproportionation (reverse process) - Not stating that the SAME element is both oxidised and reduced - Writing "redox" instead of "disproportionation" (explicitly penalised in Jun 2023 MS) - Not giving the correct oxidation numbers for each species - Not being able to balance disproportionation equations (e.g., 3ClO⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻)

Section 8B — Groups 1 and 2 (Spec 8.11–8.23)

8.11–8.13 Reactivity Trends

Exam-ready definitions:

Group 1 reactivity increases down the group because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and is more effectively shielded by more inner electron shells. This means LESS energy is needed to remove the outer electron (lower first ionisation energy), so the metals react more vigorously.

Group 2 reactivity increases down the group for the same reason: larger atomic radius and greater shielding lead to lower ionisation energies, making it easier to lose the two outer electrons.

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Jan 2024 Q21(d) (6 marks, QWC) — Ca vs Mg reactivity with water.
    MS: Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂; Ca reacts faster than Mg; Ca has lower ionisation energy; Ca(OH)₂ more soluble → darker blue / higher pH.

  2. Jan 2024 Q21(c)(i) (2 marks) — Observations of Mg(NO₃)₂ thermal decomposition.
    MS: Two from: brown gas (NO₂), bubbles, solid melts, indicator turns red.

  3. Jun 2023 Q10 (1 mark) — Why Na⁺ forms peroxide but Li⁺ does not.
    MS: Larger ionic radius of Na⁺ → lower charge density → polarises O₂²⁻ less → stabilises peroxide. Li⁺ has too high charge density.

Common mistakes: - Saying reactivity INCREASES up the group - Confusing the reason (must mention ionisation energy AND distance/shielding) - Forgetting that Group 2 reactions with cold water are slow (Mg barely reacts), but steam reactions are vigorous

8.14–8.17 Thermal Decomposition

Exam-ready definition:

Thermal decomposition is the breaking down of a compound into simpler substances when heated. For Group 2 compounds, thermal stability INCREASES down the group. This is because the M²⁺ ion gets larger down the group, decreasing its charge density and polarising power. The larger cation polarises the anion less, so less distortion of the electron cloud occurs and more energy is needed to break the bonds.

Polarising power explanation chain (high-value for QWC): 1. M²⁺ ionic radius increases down Group 2 2. Charge density (charge/size ratio) decreases 3. Polarising power decreases 4. The cation distorts/polarises the anion (e.g., CO₃²⁻, NO₃⁻) less 5. The O–C or O–N bonds within the anion are weakened less 6. More energy is required to decompose the compound 7. Thermal stability increases

Thermal decomposition products:

Compound Type Group 1 (general) Group 2 (general) Li / Be (exceptions)

8.18–8.20 Solubility Trends

Section 8C — Group 7 (Spec 8.24–8.28)

8.24–8.25 Group 7 Trends

Exam-ready definitions:

Reactivity as oxidising agents DECREASES down Group 7 because atomic radius increases, shielding increases, and the ability to attract electrons (electronegativity) decreases. This means the halogen molecules become less able to gain an electron and act as oxidising agents.

Displacement reactions: A more reactive halogen (higher up Group 7) will displace a less reactive halogen from its halide salt solution. E.g., Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂.

Trend summary:

Property Direction down Group 7
Atomic radius INCREASES
Electronegativity DECREASES
Reactivity (oxidising power) DECREASES
Boiling temperature INCREASES (more electrons → stronger London forces)
Bond enthalpy (H–X) DECREASES (bond gets weaker, longer)

Real exam examples with MS answers:

  1. Jan 2024 Q12 (1 mark) — Group 7 trends.
    MS: Boiling temp ↑, electronegativity ↓, oxidising power ↓ (all three simultaneously).

  2. Jun 2024 Q12 (1 mark) — Why b.p. increases down Group 7.
    MS: More electrons → stronger London forces.

  3. Jun 2023 Q8 (1 mark) — Which halogen displacement reaction would NOT occur?
    MS: F₂ cannot be displaced by At₂ (F₂ is the strongest oxidising agent).

  4. Jan 2024 Q7 (1 mark) — Which equation shows oxidation of a Group 7 element?
    MS: Br₂ + F₂ → 2BrF (Br oxidised from 0 to +1).

Common mistakes: - Saying reactivity INCREASES down Group 7 (it DECREASES as oxidising agents) - Confusing "reactivity" — halogens get LESS reactive down the group (opposite to Groups 1 and 2) - Explaining b.p. increase as "stronger covalent bonds" (it is intermolecular London forces)

Concept 1: Predicting Physical Properties from Intermolecular Forces

Key Principles

When predicting boiling points, melting points, or solubility, you must systematically consider:

Step 1: Identify the types of IMF present in each substance. - ALL substances have London forces - Polar molecules ALSO have permanent dipole–dipole forces - Molecules with H bonded to F, O, or N ALSO have hydrogen bonding - Ionic compounds have ion-dipole interactions with polar solvents

Step 2: Compare the strongest IMF in each substance. - Hydrogen bonding > permanent dipole–dipole > London forces - When the same type of IMF operates, compare: - Number of electrons (more electrons → stronger London forces) - Surface area / branching (less branching → greater surface area → stronger London forces) - Number of H-bonding sites (more OH/NH groups → more H-bonds per molecule)

Step 3: Apply to the property being asked about.

Worked Example 1.1: Boiling Point Comparison (Jan 2023 Q6 style)

Question: Arrange HF, HCl, HBr, and HI in order of increasing boiling temperature. Explain your answer.

Solution:

  1. All four HX molecules have London forces.
  2. Only HF has hydrogen bonding (H bonded to the most electronegative element, F).
  3. London force strength increases down the group (more electrons → more polarisable electron cloud): - HCl (18 electrons) < HBr (36 electrons) < HI (54 electrons)
  4. Hydrogen bonding is much stronger than London forces, so HF has the highest b.p. despite having the fewest electrons.

Answer: HCl < HBr < HI < HF

Mark scheme requirements (4 marks): - (1) All hydrogen halides have London forces - (2) London force strength increases with number of electrons (HCl to HI) - (3) Only HF has hydrogen bonding - (4) Hydrogen bonding is much stronger than London forces

Worked Example 1.2: Isomeric Alcohols (Jan 2023 Q19(b)(ii) style)

Question: Compound A (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂OH, pentan-1-ol) has a higher boiling point than compound B ((CH₃)₃CCH₂OH, 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol). Explain why.

Solution: - Both compounds have hydrogen bonding (both have one –OH group) - Both compounds have London forces - Since hydrogen bonding is the same in both, the difference is due to London forces - Compound A has a straight chain → greater surface area → stronger London forces - Compound B is branched → smaller surface area → weaker London forces

Exam-ready answer: Compound A has stronger London forces due to greater surface area / less branching.

MS note: "Do not award just stronger intermolecular forces" — you MUST specify London forces.

Worked Example 1.3: Solubility in Different Solvents (Jan 2023 Q19(b)(iii) style)

Question: Compound B (a branched alcohol) dissolves more readily in ethanol than in water. Explain why, considering intermolecular forces.

Solution: - When B dissolves in ethanol: London forces form between B and ethanol. These London forces are similar in strength to the London forces within ethanol and within B → favourable dissolution. - When B dissolves in water: hydrogen bonds form between B and water (through the –OH group). However, the hydrogen bonds within water are very strong, and the hydrogen bonds formed between B and water are weaker (B has only one –OH group, water has two H-bonds per molecule per direction) → less favourable. - In ethanol, the forces broken (London forces in ethanol + London/H-bonds in B) are more similar to the forces formed (London forces + H-bonds between B and ethanol).

Mark scheme (3 marks): - (1) London forces between B and ethanol - (2) H-bonds between B and water - (3) Comparison: London forces B–EtOH stronger than/similar to those in B; OR H-bonds B–H₂O weaker than H-bonds in water

Worked Example 1.4: Non-Polar Molecule Identification

Question: Which of the following has ONLY London forces: BF₃, CH₄, NH₃, or H₂S?

Solution: - BF₃: Trigonal planar, symmetrical → bond dipoles cancel → non-polar → London forces only ✓ - CH₄: Tetrahedral, symmetrical → non-polar → London forces only ✓ - NH₃: Trigonal pyramidal (lone pair) → polar molecule → London forces + permanent dipole–dipole + hydrogen bonding ✗ - H₂S: Bent (lone pairs) → polar molecule → London forces + permanent dipole–dipole ✗ (but NOT hydrogen bonding — S is not electronegative enough)

If asked to identify the compound with ONLY London forces, check for molecular symmetry. Answer: BF₃ or CH₄ (depends on the specific options in the question).

Worked Example 2.1: Oxidation Numbers in Simple Compounds

Question: Calculate the oxidation number of the specified element in each compound: (a) V in VO²⁺ (b) Cr in K₂Cr₂O₇ (c) Fe in BaFeO₄ (d) Cl in KClO₂

Solution:

(a) V in VO²⁺:
O = −2, overall charge = +2
x + (−2) = +2 → x = +4

(b) Cr in K₂Cr₂O₇:
K = +1, O = −2, overall = 0
2(+1) + 2x + 7(−2) = 0 → 2 + 2x − 14 = 0 → 2x = 12 → x = +6

(c) Fe in BaFeO₄:
Ba = +2, O = −2, overall = 0
+2 + x + 4(−2) = 0 → x − 6 = 0 → x = +6 (so this is ferrate(VI))

(d) Cl in KClO₂:
K = +1, O = −2, overall = 0
+1 + x + 2(−2) = 0 → x − 3 = 0 → x = +3 (so this is potassium chlorate(III))

Worked Example 2.2: Oxidation Numbers in Peroxides and Hydrides (Jan 2024 Q6 style)

Question: In which compound does an element have an oxidation number of −1? Options: HF, NaH, H₂O₂, CH₄

Solution: - HF: F is always −1 ✓ (but the question asks about "an element" — this is valid) - NaH: Na = +1, so H = −1 ✓ (metal hydride — H is −1) - H₂O₂: H = +1, so O = −1 ✓ (peroxide — O is −1) - CH₄: H = +1, so C = −4 ✗ (no element at −1)

Answer: CH₄ is the one that does NOT have an element at −1. The others do (F in HF, H in NaH, O in H₂O₂).

Worked Example 2.3: Disproportionation Identification (Oct 2023 Q7 style)

Question: Which of the following is a disproportionation reaction? - A: 3Cu + 8HNO₃ → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + 4H₂O - B: S₂O₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → S + SO₂ + H₂O - C: NH₄NO₂ → N₂ + 2H₂O - D: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂

Solution: Disproportionation = SAME element both oxidised AND reduced.

  • A: Cu is oxidised (0 → +2), N is reduced (+5 → +2). Different elements → NOT disproportionation.
  • B: In S₂O₃²⁻, S has oxidation state +2 (average). Products: S(0) and S in SO₂ (+4). Same element (S) both oxidised (+2 → +4) and reduced (+2 → 0) → DISPROPORTIONATION ✓
  • C: N in NH₄⁺ is −3, N in NO₂⁻ is +3. Both end up at 0. This is comproportionation (reverse of disproportionation).
  • D: In H₂O₂, O is −1. Products: O in H₂O is −2 (reduced), O in O₂ is 0 (oxidised). Same element → DISPROPORTIONATION ✓

Answer: B or D (depending on options — in Oct 2023, answer was B for S₂O₃²⁻).

Worked Example 2.4: Oxidation Number Range for Disproportionation (Jun 2024 Q18(b)(iii) style)

Question: ClO₂ (Cl = +4) can disproportionate to form HClO₄ (Cl = +7). What is the minimum oxidation number that compound A must have for disproportionation to occur between ClO₂ and HClO₄?

Solution: For disproportionation of element X, the intermediate oxidation number must be between the oxidation numbers in the products.

Cl in ClO₂ = +4
Cl in HClO₄ = +7

The disproportionating species must have an oxidation number between +4 and +7 (exclusive), so that some Cl atoms are oxidised (to +7) and some are reduced (to +4 or below).

Answer: The oxidation number of Cl in compound A must be +5 or +6 (between +4 and +7).

Concept 3: Balancing Redox Equations from Half-Equations

Method

  1. Identify what is oxidised and what is reduced (assign oxidation numbers)
  2. Write the two separate half-equations: - Balance atoms (except H and O) - Balance O by adding H₂O - Balance H by adding H⁺ (acidic) or OH⁻ (alkaline — add H₂O to the O-deficient side, then OH⁻ to both sides) - Balance charge by adding electrons (e⁻)
  3. Multiply the half-equations so that the electrons cancel
  4. Add the half-equations and cancel common species

Worked Example 3.1: MnO₂ + HCl (Oct 2023 Q19(b) style)

Question: Show that MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + Cl₂ + 2H₂O is a redox reaction using oxidation numbers.

Solution:

Assign oxidation numbers: - Mn in MnO₂: x + 2(−2) = 0 → Mn = +4 - Mn in MnCl₂: Mn = +2 (Cl is always −1) - Cl in HCl: Cl = −1 - Cl in Cl₂: Cl = 0

Identify changes: - Mn: +4 → +2 (decreased = reduced) — gains 2 electrons - Cl: −1 → 0 (increased = oxidised) — loses 1 electron per Cl atom

Half-equations: - Reduction: MnO₂ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 2H₂O - Oxidation: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

Combine: MnO₂ + 4H⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → Mn²⁺ + Cl₂ + 2H₂O

Worked Example 3.2: Disproportionation of ClO⁻ (Jan 2024 Q24(b)(iii) style)

Question: Write a balanced equation for the disproportionation of sodium chlorate(I), NaClO, to form NaCl and NaClO₃.

Solution:

Assign oxidation numbers: - Cl in ClO⁻: x + (−2) = −1 → Cl = +1 - Cl in Cl⁻: Cl = −1 - Cl in ClO₃⁻: x + 3(−2) = −1 → Cl = +5

Half-equations: - Reduction (+1 → −1): ClO⁻ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cl⁻ + H₂O - Oxidation (+1 → +5): ClO⁻ + 3H₂O → ClO₃⁻ + 6H⁺ + 4e⁻

Multiply to cancel electrons: - Reduction × 2: 2ClO⁻ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + 2H₂O - Oxidation × 1: ClO⁻ + 3H₂O → ClO₃⁻ + 6H⁺ + 4e⁻

Add and cancel (4e⁻ cancel): 3ClO⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻

Full ionic equation: 3ClO⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻

With spectator ions: 3NaClO → 2NaCl + NaClO₃

Worked Example 3.3: Half-Equation in Alkaline Conditions (Oct 2023 Q6 style)

Question: Write the balanced half-equation for the reduction of O₂ to OH⁻ in alkaline conditions. How many electrons are transferred?

Solution:

In alkaline conditions: 1. Balance O: O₂ + 2H₂O → 4OH⁻ (oxygen balanced, but charge not balanced) 2. Balance charge: Left side = 0, Right side = 4 × (−1) = −4 3. Add electrons to left: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻

Check: Left: charge = 0 + 0 + (−4) = −4. Right: charge = 4 × (−1) = −4. ✓

Answer: 4 electrons (n = 4)

Worked Example 3.4: Cl₂ + H₂O Disproportionation (Oct 2024 Q19(b)(i) style)

Question: Explain why the reaction Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HClO + HCl is a disproportionation reaction.

Solution:

Assign oxidation numbers: - Cl in Cl₂: 0 (element in standard state) - Cl in HClO: x + (−2) + (+1) = 0 → x = +1 (oxidised) - Cl in HCl: +1 + x = 0 → x = −1 (reduced)

Explanation: The SAME element (chlorine) is both oxidised (from 0 to +1 in HClO) AND reduced (from 0 to −1 in HCl). This is the definition of disproportionation.

Concept 4: Group 2 Thermal Decomposition — Polarising Power Mechanism

The Polarising Power Argument (Full Chain)

This is one of the most frequently tested extended explanations. You must present a linked chain of reasoning:

M²⁺ ionic radius increases down Group 2
        ↓
Charge density (charge/size ratio) decreases
        ↓
Polarising power decreases
        ↓
Cation distorts/polarises the anion less
        ↓
Bonds within the anion are weakened less
        ↓
More energy is needed to break the bonds
        ↓
Thermal stability INCREASES down the group

Worked Example 4.1: Explaining the Trend (Jan 2024 Q21(c)(ii) style)

Question: Mg(NO₃)₂ is less thermally stable than Ca(NO₃)₂. Explain why.

Solution:

Mg²⁺ has a smaller ionic radius than Ca²⁺ (higher up the group). This means Mg²⁺ has a higher charge density and greater polarising power. The Mg²⁺ ion more strongly polarises/distorts the electron cloud of the NO₃⁻ anion. This greater distortion weakens the N–O bonds within the nitrate ion more than Ca²⁺ would. Therefore, less energy is required to break the N–O bonds and decompose Mg(NO₃)₂, making it less thermally stable.

MS requirement (3 marks, 3 linked points): - Mg²⁺ smaller → higher charge density → more polarising - N–O bonds weakened more - Less energy to break → decomposes more easily

Worked Example 4.2: Thermal Decomposition Equations (Jan 2023 Q21 style)

Question: Write balanced equations for: (a) The thermal decomposition of sodium nitrate (b) The thermal decomposition of magnesium nitrate (c) The thermal decomposition of lithium nitrate

Solution:

(a) NaNO₃ (Group 1, general): 2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + O₂

(b) Mg(NO₃)₂ (Group 2, general): 2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂ + O₂

(c) LiNO₃ (Li exception — behaves like Group 2): 4LiNO₃ → 2Li₂O + 4NO₂ + O₂

Key point: LiNO₃ decomposes to the OXIDE (like Group 2), NOT to the nitrite (like other Group 1 nitrates). This is because Li⁺ is small with high charge density (similar to Mg²⁺).

Worked Example 4.3: Diagonal Relationship Li/Mg (Oct 2024 Q20(e) style)

Question: Explain why lithium and magnesium show similar chemical behaviour in their thermal decomposition of nitrates.

Solution:

Equations: - 4LiNO₃ → 2Li₂O + 4NO₂ + O₂ - 2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂ + O₂ - 2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + ½O₂ (for comparison)

Explanation: Li⁺ and Mg²⁺ have similar ionic radii (despite being in different groups). Both are smaller than Na⁺. The smaller ionic radius gives Li⁺ and Mg²⁺ greater charge density compared to Na⁺. This greater charge density means they polarise the nitrate anion more strongly, weakening the N–O bonds more. As a result, the nitrate ion fully decomposes (breaking N–O bonds completely to give the oxide and NO₂), rather than simply losing oxygen to give the nitrite.

MS indicative points (6 points, 4 marks max + 2 reasoning): - IP1: Mg has extra shell but Mg²⁺/Li⁺ similar size - IP2: Both Li and Mg nitrate equations give NO₂ - IP3: NaNO₃ → NaNO₂ + O₂ (no NO₂) - IP4: Li/Mg nitrates produce NO₂ but NaNO₃ does not - IP5: Mg²⁺/Li⁺ smaller than Na⁺ → greater charge density - IP6: Polarise nitrate more → weaken N–O bond

Question: When a sample of Mg(NO₃)₂ is heated, 1200 cm³ of gas is collected at room temperature and pressure. Calculate the mass of Mg(NO₃)₂ that decomposed.

Solution:

Step 1: Write the equation: 2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂ + O₂

Step 2: Calculate total moles of gas: n(gas) = V / 24000 = 1200 / 24000 = 0.0500 mol

Worked Example 5.1: Solubility of Ca(OH)₂ from Titration (Oct 2023 Q20(b)(i) style)

Question: 25.0 cm³ of a saturated solution of Ca(OH)₂ is titrated with 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ HCl. The average titre is 18.95 cm³. Calculate the solubility of Ca(OH)₂ in g dm⁻³.

Solution:

Step 1: Write the equation: Ca(OH)₂ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O

Step 2: Moles of HCl: n(HCl) = c × V = 0.0500 × (18.95/1000) = 9.475 × 10⁻⁴ mol

Step 3: Moles of Ca(OH)₂ (mole ratio 1:2): n(Ca(OH)₂) = 9.475 × 10⁻⁴ / 2 = 4.738 × 10⁻⁴ mol (in 25.0 cm³)

Step 5: Convert to mass: M(Ca(OH)₂) = 40.1 + 2 × (16.0 + 1.0) = 74.1 g mol⁻¹ Solubility = 0.01895 × 74.1 = 1.40 g dm⁻³

Question: In a titration, 10.00 cm³ of solution is measured using a pipette (±0.04 cm³) and the titre is 25.00 cm³ using a burette (±0.05 cm³). What is the total percentage uncertainty?

Worked Example 6.1: Non-Spontaneous Displacement (Jan 2023 Q11 style)

Question: Which of the following reactions would NOT be expected to occur? - A: Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ - B: Br₂ + 2KI → 2KBr + I₂ - C: I₂ + 2KBr → 2KI + Br₂ - D: 2F⁻ + At₂ → 2At⁻ + F₂

Worked Example 6.2: Concentrated H₂SO₄ with Halides (Oct 2023 Q8 style)

Question: Concentrated sulfuric acid is added to solid sodium bromide. What product(s) are formed?

Solution:

Two processes occur: 1. Acid-base reaction: NaBr + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HBr 2. Redox reaction: HBr reduces H₂SO₄: H₂SO₄ + 2HBr → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O

Overall products: Br₂ (red-brown fumes), SO₂ (colourless acidic gas), H₂O, and NaHSO₄

Key point: Br⁻ is a moderate reducing agent — it reduces H₂SO₄ to SO₂ (S goes from +6 to +4) but NOT further to S or H₂S. Compare with I⁻ which reduces H₂SO₄ further.

Worked Example 6.3: Electronegativity Trend Down Group 7 (Jun 2022 Q16(a) style)

Question: Explain why electronegativity decreases down Group 7.

Solution:

Going down Group 7, the atomic radius increases (more electron shells are added). Although the nuclear charge also increases, the additional inner electron shells provide increased shielding. The increased distance between the nucleus and the outer electrons, combined with the greater shielding, means the nucleus attracts bonding electrons less strongly. Therefore, electronegativity decreases.

Key points for marks: - Atomic radius increases / more electron shells - Increased shielding - Nuclear attraction for outer electrons decreases - Therefore electronegativity decreases

Worked Example 6.4: Group 7 B.p. Trend with Explanation (Jun 2024 Q12 style)

Question: Explain why the boiling temperatures of the halogens increase down Group 7.

Solution:

Going down Group 7, the number of electrons in each molecule increases (Cl₂ = 34e⁻, Br₂ = 70e⁻, I₂ = 106e⁻). More electrons means a larger electron cloud that is more easily polarised. The resulting instantaneous and induced dipoles are stronger, giving stronger London forces. Stronger London forces require more energy to overcome, so boiling temperatures increase.

MS answer (1 mark): More electrons → stronger London forces.

Note: This is about INTERMOLECULAR forces (London forces), NOT about the strength of the covalent bond within the halogen molecule (X–X bond enthalpy actually DECREASES down the group). The F–F bond (158 kJ/mol) is anomalously weak compared to Cl–Cl (242 kJ/mol), Br–Br (193 kJ/mol), and I–I (151 kJ/mol), because fluorine's small atomic radius brings lone-pair electrons close together, causing strong lone-pair repulsion that weakens the bond.

Worked Example 8.2: Why I₂ is More Soluble in Hexane than Water (Oct 2023 Q19(d) style)

Question: Iodine is much more soluble in hexane than in water. Explain this in terms of intermolecular forces.

Solution:

Iodine has only London forces between its molecules.

In hexane: - Hexane also has only London forces - The London forces between I₂ and hexane molecules are similar in strength to the London forces between hexane molecules and between I₂ molecules - Energy released forming I₂–hexane London forces ≈ energy needed to break hexane–hexane and I₂–I₂ London forces - Therefore, I₂ dissolves readily

In water: - Water has strong hydrogen bonding between its molecules - I₂ cannot form hydrogen bonds (no H bonded to F, O, or N) - The London forces formed between I₂ and water are MUCH weaker than the hydrogen bonds that must be broken in water - The energetics are unfavourable → I₂ is barely soluble

MS (6 marks, QWC): - IP1: I₂ has only London forces - IP2: Water forms H-bonds (strongest IMF) - IP3: Hexane has only London forces - IP4: I₂–hexane London forces similar in strength to those broken → dissolves - IP5: I₂ cannot break water H-bonds → does not dissolve

Concept 9: Redox in Context — Batteries and Bleach

Worked Example 9.1: Lithium Battery (Oct 2024 Q20(c) style)

Question: In a lithium-ion battery, the overall equation is: C₆Li + CoO₂ → LiCoO₂ + C₆

Describe, with reference to oxidation numbers, what happens to cobalt.

Solution:

In CoO₂: O = −2, so Co = +4 (overall charge 0: x + 2(−2) = 0 → x = +4) In LiCoO₂: Li = +1, O = −2, so Co: +1 + x + 2(−2) = 0 → x = +3

Co changes from +4 to +3 → oxidation number DECREASES → Co is reduced (gains electrons).

Answer: Cobalt is reduced; its oxidation number changes from +4 to +3.

Worked Example 9.2: Bleach + Acid Danger (Jan 2024 Q24(b)(ii) style)

Question: Household bleach contains NaOCl. Explain why mixing bleach with an acid-based toilet cleaner is dangerous, using equilibrium reasoning.

Solution:

Bleach contains ClO⁻ ions in equilibrium: Eq.1: HClO ⇌ H⁺ + ClO⁻

Adding acid (H⁺) shifts Eq.1 to the LEFT: H⁺ + ClO⁻ → HClO

The HClO then reacts with Cl⁻ and H⁺ (from the acid): Eq.2: HClO + Cl⁻ + H⁺ → Cl₂ + H₂O

Toxic chlorine gas (Cl₂) is released — this is the danger.

MS (3 marks): - Adding acid → Eq.1 shifts left (H⁺ + ClO⁻ → HClO) - Eq.2 shifts right (HClO + Cl⁻ + H⁺ → Cl₂ + H₂O) - Toxic Cl₂(g) released

Quick Reference: Common Calculation Templates

Template 1: Enthalpy from Calorimetry (q = mcΔT)

q = m × c × ΔT           (in joules)
ΔH = ±q / n              (in kJ mol⁻¹, don't forget sign and ÷1000)

Ion-Dipole Interactions (Spec 7.6–7.8)

A newer tested concept. Ion-dipole interactions occur between IONS and the permanent dipole of a polar molecule (e.g., ionic compounds dissolving in water). Not an intermolecular force (which is between molecules), but grouped with IMFs in the spec.

Paper Q Marks Key Content

Oct 2024 Q16(c)(i) 2 Draw ion-dipole diagram: δ⁺H of water near SO₄²⁻; δ⁻O of water near Mg²⁺; show partial charges

Key points: - Ion-dipole ≠ hydrogen bonding (hydrogen bonding is between molecules, not involving ions) - In diagrams: H atoms (δ⁺) orient toward ANIONS; O atom (δ⁻) orients toward CATIONS - Must show at least one δ⁺/δ⁻ on the water molecule for full marks (Oct 2024 MS requirement)

Full-Mark Answer Pattern: Boiling Point Comparison (2–4 marks)

Must specify the TYPE of intermolecular force AND link to structural feature:

"London forces / dispersion forces"
  → more electrons / larger surface area / less branching
  → stronger London forces → higher boiling point

"Hydrogen bonding"
  → H bonded to highly electronegative atom (O, N, F)
  → strongest type of IMF
  → anomalously high boiling point (e.g. HF, H₂O, NH₃)

"Permanent dipole-dipole forces"
  → polar molecule (C=O, C-Cl)
  → stronger than London forces but weaker than H-bonding

MARK SCHEME WATCH:
  • "Stronger intermolecular forces" alone is NOT enough (Jan 2023 Q19b(ii))
  • Must specify "London forces" + "greater surface area"
  • H-bonding: must mention lone pair + δ⁺H interaction
