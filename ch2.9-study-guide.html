Section 10A: General Principles of Organic Reactions

10.1 Classification of Organic Reactions

Substitution: A reaction where an atom or group of atoms in a molecule is replaced by a different atom or group.

Elimination: A reaction where a small molecule (e.g., H₂O, HCl, HBr) is removed from a larger molecule, resulting in the formation of a double bond.

Addition: A reaction where two or more molecules combine to form a single, larger molecule (typically across a C=C double bond).

Oxidation: A reaction involving the loss of electrons (or gain of oxygen / loss of hydrogen). In organic chemistry, typically identified by a change in functional group (e.g., alcohol → aldehyde/carboxylic acid/ketone).

Hydrolysis: A reaction in which a molecule is broken down by reaction with water. In the context of halogenoalkanes, hydrolysis is a substitution reaction with water (or aqueous hydroxide) to form an alcohol.

10.2 Nucleophile — Definition

Nucleophile: An electron-pair donor that attacks an electron-deficient (electrophilic) carbon atom.

Common Nucleophiles in WCH12

Nucleophile Source Product Type
OH⁻ NaOH(aq) or KOH(aq) Alcohol
CN⁻ KCN or NaCN in ethanol Nitrile (extends carbon chain)
NH₃ Ammonia (in ethanol, under pressure) Primary amine
H₂O Water (slow) Alcohol (hydrolysis)

Exam fact (Jun 2023 Q17): In aqueous conditions, water (H₂O) acts as the nucleophile, NOT Ag⁺, NO₃⁻, or the solvent ethanol (if water is present).

10.3 Bond Polarity and the C–X Bond

The carbon–halogen bond is polar because halogens (except iodine) are more electronegative than carbon. Iodine has the same electronegativity as carbon (2.5), so C–I is essentially non-polar. For the other halogens, this polarity creates a δ⁺ charge on carbon and a δ⁻ charge on the halogen.

This polarity makes the carbon atom electrophilic (electron-deficient) and therefore susceptible to attack by nucleophiles.

Electronegativity and Bond Polarity

Bond Electronegativity Difference Polarity Bond Strength (mean bond enthalpy)
C–F 1.50 (F = 4.0, C = 2.5) Most polar 485 kJ mol⁻¹ (strongest)
C–Cl 0.50 (Cl = 3.0) Polar 339 kJ mol⁻¹
C–Br 0.30 (Br = 2.8) Polar 276 kJ mol⁻¹
C–I 0.00 (I = 2.5) Non-polar 238 kJ mol⁻¹ (weakest)

Critical exam point: Bond STRENGTH (enthalpy) is the key factor determining hydrolysis rate, NOT bond polarity. The weaker the C–X bond, the lower the activation energy, the faster the reaction.

10.4 SN2 Mechanism — Definition

SN2 (substitution nucleophilic bimolecular): A one-step nucleophilic substitution mechanism in which the nucleophile attacks the electrophilic carbon from the opposite side to the leaving group, forming a transition state where the incoming nucleophile and outgoing leaving group are both partially bonded. The C–X bond breaks at the same time as the new C–Nu bond forms.

Key Features of SN2

Feature Detail
Steps One (concerted)
Stereocentre Inversion of configuration (Walden inversion) at chiral carbon
Kinetics Second order: rate = k[halogenoalkane][nucleophile]
Favoured by Primary halogenoalkanes, good nucleophiles, polar aprotic solvents
Transition state Trigonal bipyramidal (5 groups around carbon)

Section 10B: Halogenoalkanes

10.5 Nomenclature of Halogenoalkanes

Halogenoalkane naming rules: 1. Identify the longest carbon chain containing the –OH or halogen-bearing carbon 2. Number the chain to give the substituent the lowest possible locant 3. Use prefixes: fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo- 4. Alphabetical order for multiple different substituents (but ignore di-, tri- prefixes)

10.6 Classification of Halogenoalkanes: 1°, 2°, 3°

Primary (1°) halogenoalkane: The carbon bearing the halogen is attached to one (or zero) other carbon atoms.

Secondary (2°) halogenoalkane: The carbon bearing the halogen is attached to two other carbon atoms.

Tertiary (3°) halogenoalkane: The carbon bearing the halogen is attached to three other carbon atoms.

Classification Examples

10.12 Rate of Hydrolysis — Bond Enthalpy Factor

The rate of hydrolysis of halogenoalkanes depends primarily on the bond enthalpy of the C–X bond:

C–I (238 kJ mol⁻¹) < C–Br (276 kJ mol⁻¹) < C–Cl (339 kJ mol⁻¹) < C–F (485 kJ mol⁻¹)

Weaker bonds have lower activation energies, so reactions are faster.

Exam fact (Oct 2023 Q15): "Why does 1-chlorobutane react more slowly than 1-bromobutane?" Answer: The C–Cl bond is stronger than the C–Br bond → higher activation energy → slower reaction. Solubility and polarity are irrelevant to rate.

10.13 Rate of Hydrolysis — Structure Factor (1° vs 3°)

Structure Mechanism Relative Rate Reason
Primary SN2 Slowest among the three (for a given halogen) Least steric hindrance favours SN2, but the C–X bond must break simultaneously with nucleophile attack (no stable carbocation forms)
Secondary SN2/SN1 Intermediate Some steric hindrance
Tertiary SN1 Fastest Forms stable carbocation intermediate; C–X bond breaks easily

Real exam application (Oct 2024 Q17(b)(ii), Jun 2024 Q16(b)(iii)): "Give TWO reasons why 2-iodo-2-methylpropane + KOH is faster than 1-chlorobutane + KOH." 1. 2-iodo-2-methylpropane is tertiary (SN1, fast) vs 1-chlorobutane is primary (SN2, slow) 2. C–I bond is weaker than C–Cl bond (lower activation energy)

Both reasons are required for full marks.

10.14 Core Practical 5 — Comparing Rates of Hydrolysis

CP5: Investigate the rate of hydrolysis of halogenoalkanes using silver nitrate solution in ethanol. Measure the time taken for a precipitate to form.

Key points for the exam: - Same temperature for all tests (ensure fair test) - Same concentration and volume of AgNO₃ - Same concentration of halogenoalkane - Time is measured from mixing to first appearance of precipitate - Rate ∝ 1/time

Distillation vs Reflux — The Critical Distinction

Technique When Used Why Product
Distillation Primary alcohol → aldehyde Aldehyde has LOWER boiling point than the alcohol, so it distils off as it forms, preventing further oxidation Aldehyde
Reflux Primary alcohol → carboxylic acid, OR secondary alcohol → ketone Condenser returns vapour to the flask, allowing prolonged heating and complete reaction Carboxylic acid or ketone

Exam application (Oct 2023 Q14(a–b)): - Propan-2-ol under reflux, excess K₂Cr₂O₇ → propanone (ketone). IR: C=O at 1720–1700 cm⁻¹. - Propan-1-ol with distillation → propanal (aldehyde). IR: C=O at 1740–1720 cm⁻¹.

10.21 [O] as Oxidising Agent Shorthand

[O] represents an oxidising agent (typically acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ or acidified KMnO₄) in organic equations.

Counting [O] atoms needed (Jan 2024 Q19): - Primary alcohol → carboxylic acid: RCH₂OH + 2[O] → RCOOH + H₂O - Secondary alcohol → ketone: R₂CHOH + [O] → R₂C=O + H₂O - Tertiary alcohol: no reaction

Real exam (Jan 2024 Q19): A compound with 2 primary –OH groups and 1 secondary –OH requires: 2 × 2[O] + 1 × [O] = 5[O] total.

10.22 Core Practical 7 — Oxidation of Alcohols

CP7: Oxidise a primary alcohol to a carboxylic acid using acidified potassium dichromate(VI) under reflux.

Key points: - Reflux apparatus: round-bottom flask + condenser (water in at bottom, out at top) - Distillation apparatus for aldehyde: round-bottom flask + condenser + collecting flask (no return of vapour) - Colour change: orange (Cr₂O₇²⁻) → green (Cr³⁺) - Secondary alcohols also oxidise to ketones (same conditions, same colour change)

10.23 Reactions of Tertiary Alcohols with HCl

Exam fact (Jan 2024 Q18): "Which reagent(s) can convert a tertiary alcohol to a chloroalkane?" Answer: ALL THREE work: 1. PCl₅ (reacts with all alcohols) 2. Section 10D: Spectra

10.24 Mass Spectrometry — Key Definitions

Molecular ion peak (M⁺): The peak in the mass spectrum corresponding to the unchanged molecule that has lost one electron. Its m/z value equals the relative molecular mass of the compound.

Fragmentation: The breaking of the molecular ion into smaller pieces (fragment ions) in the mass spectrometer. Each fragment has a characteristic m/z value.

Base peak: The tallest peak in the mass spectrum (100% relative abundance), corresponding to the most stable fragment ion.

M+1 Peak

The M+1 peak is caused by the natural abundance of carbon-13 (¹³C, ~1.1% natural abundance). The ratio of M+1 to M⁺ peak height gives an indication of the number of carbon atoms in the molecule.

Common Fragment Ions

m/z Fragment Ion Source
15 CH₃⁺ Methyl group
17 OH⁺ Hydroxyl
29 C₂H₅⁺ or CHO⁺ Ethyl or aldehyde fragment
31 CH₂OH⁺ Primary alcohol (α-cleavage)
41 C₃H₅⁺ Propene-type fragment
43 CH₃CO⁺ or C₃H₇⁺ Acetyl (ketone/aldehyde) or propyl
45 COOH⁺ Carboxylic acid fragment
58 C₃H₆O⁺ Molecular ion of acetone

Real Exam Applications

Oct 2024 Q14(b): "Which compound gives peaks at m/z 31 (CH₂OH⁺) and m/z 60 (M⁺)?" Answer: Propan-1-ol (C₃H₈O, Mᵣ = 60). The m/z 31 fragment is characteristic of a primary alcohol (α-cleavage next to the –OH group).

Jan 2024 Q20: "Identify the compound from its mass spectrum." Answer: CH₃COCH₃ (acetone). M⁺ at m/z 58, fragments at 43 (CH₃CO⁺) and 15 (CH₃⁺).

Jan 2023 Q15: "Which compound would NOT show a prominent m/z 43 peak?" Answer: CH₃CH₂NHCH₃ — the nitrogen-containing compound fragments differently; there is no CH₃CO⁺ or (CH₃)₂CH⁺ fragment.

Oct 2023 Q21(a)(ii): "Molecular formula from mass spectrum." M⁺ at m/z 86. Empirical formula mass = 43. Molecular formula = C₄H₆O₂ (43 × 2 = 86).

Oct 2023 Q21(a)(iv): Fragment identification: - m/z 41 → C₃H₅⁺ (must include positive charge) - m/z 45 → COOH⁺ (carboxylic acid fragment)

Jun 2024 Q20(a)(ii): Mass spectrum of isoprene (C₅H₈): - m/z 68 = M⁺ (C₅H₈⁺) - m/z 53 = C₄H₅⁺ (loss of ·CH₃)

IR spectroscopy: A technique that measures the absorption of infrared radiation by molecular bonds. Different bonds absorb at characteristic wavenumbers (cm⁻¹), allowing identification of functional groups.

Real Exam Applications

Jan 2023 Q16: "Identify the compound with IR peaks at 3415 cm⁻¹ and 2250 cm⁻¹." Answer: H₂NCH₂CH₂C≡N (amino-nitrile). 3415 = N–H stretch; 2250 = C≡N stretch.

Oct 2024 Q15: "Which functional group gives broad O–H around 2500–3300 cm⁻¹ AND C=O around 1700 cm⁻¹?" Answer: Carboxylic acid.

Worked Example 1: SN2 Mechanism with Curly Arrows

1A: OH⁻ + 1-Bromopropane (Standard SN2)

Question (Oct 2023 Q18(c)(ii), Jun 2024 Q16(b)(ii)):

Complete the mechanism for the reaction of 1-bromopropane with aqueous NaOH. Show curly arrows and the products.

Step-by-step drawing:

          H   H                     H   H
          |   |                     |   |
    H—O:⁻  ··   H—C—C—C—H   →   H—O—C—C—C—H  +  :Br:⁻
              ··   |   |               |   |   |
                  H   H               H   H

          δ⁺                     δ⁻
        (on C)                 (on Br)

More detailed with all marking points:

Step 1: Attack

   Lone pair        δ⁺        δ⁻
   on O of OH⁻  →  C — Br
   (must show        ↑
   the lone pair)    |
                     Dipole arrows
                     (δ⁺ on C, δ⁻ on Br)

   Curly arrow: from C–Br bond to Brδ⁻

Step 2: Products

   H   H

1B: CN⁻ + 1-Bromopropane (Extending Carbon Chain)

Question (modelled on Oct 2024 Q17(c)(i)):

Write an equation and name the product for the reaction of 1-chloropentane with KCN in ethanol.

Equation:

CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂Cl + KCN → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CN + KCl
    (1-chloropentane)         (hexanenitrile)

Key points: - Product name: hexanenitrile (6 carbons — one more than the 5-carbon starting material, because CN adds a carbon) - Condition: in ethanol (not aqueous — aqueous conditions favour hydrolysis to alcohol)

Why this reaction is important (Oct 2024 Q17(c)(ii)):

The CN⁻ substitution extends the carbon chain by one carbon — this is its primary value in organic synthesis.

1C: N₃⁻ + 1-Bromopropane (Azide Substitution)

Question (Jan 2023 Q20(c)(ii)):

Complete the mechanism for the reaction of azide ion (N₃⁻) with 1-bromopropane.

Key marking points (3 marks): 1. Curly arrow from lone pair on terminal N to C (NOT from the negative charge symbol) 2. C–Br dipole shown (δ⁺ on C, δ⁻ on Br) 3. Curly arrow from C–Br bond to Br, forming Br⁻ 4. Organic product: CH₃CH₂CH₂N₃ (1-azidopropane) 5. Br⁻ shown as the leaving group

Specific mark scheme notes from Jan 2023: - "Half-headed (fishhook) arrows are penalised ONCE" — use full curly arrows for heterolytic fission - "The product must be neutral" — N₃ replaces Br on the carbon; the negative charge is on Br⁻, not the organic product

    H
    |

Worked Example 2: Alcohol Oxidation — Distillation vs Reflux

2A: Primary Alcohol → Aldehyde (Distillation)

Question (Oct 2023 Q14(b)):

Propan-1-ol is oxidised and the product is collected by distillation. What C=O IR absorption identifies the product?

Analysis: - Propan-1-ol (primary alcohol) is oxidised - Distillation means the product is removed as it forms → prevents further oxidation - Product: propanal (CH₃CH₂CHO) — an aldehyde - IR identification: C=O stretch at 1740–1720 cm⁻¹ (aldehyde range)

2B: Primary Alcohol → Carboxylic Acid (Reflux)

Question (Jan 2023 Q19(c)):

A primary alcohol C₆H₁₄O is reacted with acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ under reflux. What is the organic product?

Analysis: - Primary alcohol + excess oxidising agent + reflux = carboxylic acid - Product: carboxylic acid with the same carbon skeleton as the starting alcohol - Colour change: orange → green

2C: Secondary Alcohol → Ketone (Reflux)

Question (Oct 2023 Q14(a)):

Propan-2-ol is oxidised under reflux with excess acidified K₂Cr₂O₇. What C=O IR absorption identifies the product?

Analysis: - Propan-2-ol (secondary alcohol) → ketone (propanone, CH₃COCH₃) - Secondary alcohols always give ketones regardless of conditions - IR: C=O stretch at 1720–1700 cm⁻¹ (ketone range)

Distinguishing aldehyde from ketone IR:

Product C=O Range (cm⁻¹) Additional IR Feature
Aldehyde 1740–1720 C–H stretch at 2775–2700 cm⁻¹ (aldehyde C–H)
Ketone 1720–1700 No distinctive C–H feature

2D: Tertiary Alcohol — No Oxidation

Question (Jun 2024 Q20(c), QWC, 6 marks):

Three students suggest different tests to identify the –OH group in menthol (secondary), linalool (tertiary), and citronellol (primary). Criticise their suggestions.

Analysis of each test:

Test 1: Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ - Menthol (secondary): Orange → green ✓ (oxidised to ketone) - Linalool (tertiary): No colour change ✗ (tertiary alcohols are NOT oxidised) - Citronellol (primary): Orange → green ✓ (oxidised) - Problem: Cannot identify –OH in tertiary alcohol using this test

Test 2: Acidified KMnO₄ - All three give purple → colourless - Problem: KMnO₄ also reacts with C=C double bonds (linalool and citronellol both contain C=C). Not specific for –OH.

Test 3: PCl₅ - All three give misty white fumes (HCl produced) - Advantage: Reacts with ALL alcohol types (primary, secondary, AND tertiary) - Best test: PCl₅ is the most universal test for –OH identification

Mark scheme structure (6 marks): - 4 marks for indicative content (IPs 1–6 above, max 4 from 6) - 2 marks for logical structure and reasoning - Incorrect chemistry (e.g., saying tertiary alcohol IS oxidised) deducts from reasoning marks

2E: Incomplete Combustion of Alcohols

Question (Jan 2024 Q17):

Which equation represents the incomplete combustion of butan-1-ol? A. C₄H₉OH + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O (complete) B. C₄H₉OH + 4O₂ → 4CO + 5H₂O (incomplete — CO not CO₂) C. Unbalanced D. Unbalanced

Answer: B — Incomplete combustion produces CO (carbon monoxide) instead of CO₂.

General principle: Incomplete combustion occurs when there is insufficient oxygen. Products include CO and/or C (soot).

Worked Example 3: IR Spectrum Analysis

3A: Identifying a Carboxylic Acid from IR

Question (Oct 2024 Q15):

An IR spectrum shows a very broad absorption around 2500–3300 cm⁻¹ and a strong absorption around 1700 cm⁻¹. Which functional group?

Answer: Carboxylic acid (–COOH)

Reasoning: 1. Broad O–H stretch (2500–3300 cm⁻¹) — much broader than alcohol O–H due to hydrogen bonding 2. C=O stretch (~1700 cm⁻¹) — confirms carbonyl 3. The combination of very broad O–H + C=O is diagnostic for carboxylic acid

Aldehyde No O–H 1740–1720 Additional C–H at 2775–2700

Ester No O–H 1750–1730 C–O stretch at 1300–1000

3B: Identifying Functional Groups from Multiple Peaks

Question (Jan 2023 Q16):

An IR spectrum shows absorptions at 3415 cm⁻¹ and 2250 cm⁻¹. Which compound? A. H₂NCH₂CH₂C≡N B. HOCH₂CH₂COOH C. CH₃COCH₂NH₂ D. CH₃CH₂OH

Answer: A (H₂NCH₂CH₂C≡N)

Step-by-step analysis: 1. 3415 cm⁻¹ → N–H stretch (in the 3550–3200 range). This could be an amine or an alcohol. 2. 2250 cm⁻¹ → C≡N stretch (in the 2260–2215 range). This is a nitrile. 3. Compound must contain BOTH N–H AND C≡N. 4. A has both (amine + nitrile). B has O–H and C=O (no nitrile). C has N–H and C=O (no nitrile). D has only O–H.

Worked Example 4: Mass Spectrometry Fragmentation

4A: Identifying a Compound from M⁺ and Fragment Peaks

Question (Jan 2024 Q20):

A mass spectrum shows a molecular ion peak at m/z 58 and prominent fragment peaks at m/z 43 and m/z 15. Which compound?

Answer: CH₃COCH₃ (acetone/propanone)

Step-by-step analysis:

  1. M⁺ at m/z 58: Relative molecular mass = 58. Check candidate formulae: - CH₃COCH₃ (C₃H₆O): 3(12) + 6(1) + 16 = 58 ✓ - C₃H₈O (propanol): 36 + 8 + 16 = 60 ✗ (doesn't match) - C₂H₆O₂: 24 + 6 + 32 = 62 ✗

  2. Fragment at m/z 43: - CH₃CO⁺ (acetyl ion, characteristic of ketones/aldehydes): 12 + 3 + 12 + 16 = 43 ✓ - OR C₃H₇⁺: 36 + 7 = 43 (also possible, but less consistent with other data)

  3. Fragment at m/z 15: - CH₃⁺: 12 + 3 = 15 ✓

  4. Combined: M⁺ 58 + fragment 43 (CH₃CO⁺) + fragment 15 (CH₃⁺) = consistent with propanone (the molecule fragments by breaking the C–C bond adjacent to the carbonyl).

4B: Fragment Ion Identification

Question (Oct 2023 Q21(a)(iv)):

The mass spectrum of compound X (C₄H₆O₂) shows prominent peaks at m/z 41 and m/z 45. Complete the table with the identity of these fragment ions.

The empirical formula of compound X is C₂H₃O (empirical mass = 43). The molecular ion peak is at m/z 86. Deduce the molecular formula.

Exam fact (cross-paper analysis): The M+1 peak is caused by carbon-13 (¹³C). The ratio of M+1 height to M⁺ height is approximately:

Worked Example 5: Rate of Hydrolysis Comparisons

5A: Bond Enthalpy Comparison

Question (Oct 2023 Q15):

1-Chlorobutane reacts more slowly than 1-bromobutane with aqueous NaOH. Why?

Full-mark answer:

The C–Cl bond (339 kJ mol⁻¹) is stronger than the C–Br bond (276 kJ mol⁻¹). A stronger bond requires more energy to break, giving a higher activation energy. Therefore, fewer molecules have energy ≥ Eₐ, and the rate of reaction is slower.

What NOT to say: - "C–Cl is less polar" — polarity doesn't determine rate in SN2 - "Cl is less electronegative" — wrong (Cl is MORE electronegative than Br) - "Chloride is a worse leaving group" — this is true but not the explanation expected at IAL level

5B: Structure Comparison (1° vs 3°)

Question (Oct 2024 Q17(b)(ii), Jun 2024 Q16(b)(iii)):

Give TWO reasons why 2-iodo-2-methylpropane reacts faster with KOH than 1-chlorobutane.

Full 2-mark answer: 1. 2-iodo-2-methylpropane is a tertiary halogenoalkane (reacts via SN1 mechanism, which is fast) whereas 1-chlorobutane is primary (reacts via SN2, which is slower) 2. The C–I bond is weaker than the C–Cl bond (lower bond enthalpy), so the activation energy is lower

Mark scheme note: Both reasons must be given for 2 marks. One reason only = 1 mark.

5C: Complete Rate Ranking

Question (Jan 2023 Q13, MCQ):

Which compound gives the fastest precipitate with AgNO₃ in ethanol? A. (CH₃)₃CI (tertiary iodoalkane) B. CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Cl (primary chloroalkane) C. (CH₃)₂CHBr (secondary bromoalkane) D. CH₃CH₂CH₂Br (primary bromoalkane)

Answer: A — (CH₃)₃CI

Reasoning: TWO factors both maximise the rate: 1. Tertiary structure → SN1 mechanism (fastest) 2. Iodine halogen → weakest C–X bond (lowest Eₐ)

Both factors compound to give the fastest reaction. Option C has only one favourable factor (Br), and option B has neither (primary + Cl = slowest).

Question (Jan 2023 Q14):

How many alkene products (including stereoisomers) are formed when CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₂CH₂CH₃ undergoes elimination?

Worked Example 8: Combined Organic Analysis (Mass Spec + IR + Tests)

Question (modelled on Oct 2023 Q21(a)):

An unknown compound X has the following properties: - Contains C, H, and O only - 1.92 g of X on complete combustion gives 1.08 g CO₂ and 0.132 g H₂O - Bromine water is decolourised - Na₂CO₃ solution produces effervescence - Mass spectrum: M⁺ at m/z 86, prominent fragments at m/z 41 and m/z 45 - No geometric (E/Z) isomerism

Deduce the structure of X.

Step 1: Empirical formula from combustion data

n(C) = 1.08/12 = 0.0900 mol
n(H) = 0.132/1 = 0.132 mol
Mass O = 1.92 − (0.0900×12) − 0.132 = 1.92 − 1.080 − 0.132 = 0.708 g
n(O) = 0.708/16 = 0.0443 mol

Ratio: 0.0900 : 0.132 : 0.0443 = 2.03 : 2.98 : 1 ≈ **2 : 3 : 1**
Empirical formula = **C₂H₃O** (mass = 43)

Step 2: Molecular formula - M⁺ at m/z 86 - 86 / 43 = 2 → Molecular formula = C₄H₆O₂

Step 3: Functional group tests - Bromine water decolourised → C=C (alkene) present - Na₂CO₃ effervescence → –COOH (carboxylic acid) present

Step 4: Structure determination - C₄H₆O₂ with C=C and COOH, and no E/Z isomerism - 2-Methylpropenoic acid (CH₂=C(CH₃)COOH) — the C=C has two identical H atoms on one carbon (CH₂=), so no E/Z isomerism is possible. This satisfies all constraints.

IR would show: Broad O–H (2500–3300), C=O (~1700), C=C (~1650)

Mass spec fragments: m/z 45 = COOH⁺, m/z 41 = C₃H₅⁺

Full-Mark Answer Pattern: SN2 Mechanism (3–4 marks)

MARKING POINTS (all must be present):

1. CURVED ARROW from LONE PAIR on nucleophile to δ⁺ carbon
   → Must originate from a visible lone pair (not from the negative charge symbol)
   → Full-headed curly arrow (NOT fishhook/half-arrow — those are for radicals)

2. DIPOLE on C–X bond shown (δ⁺ on C, δ− on X)
   → Or simply show C–X with arrow indicating polarity

3. CURVED ARROW from C–X bond to X
   → Both electrons of the bond go to the leaving group

4. ORGANIC PRODUCT drawn correctly
   → Must show the nucleophile attached, X replaced

5. LEAVING GROUP as anion (e.g. Br⁻, Cl⁻)

6. (For NH₃ reactions only) DEPROTONATION STEP
   → Second NH₃ removes H⁺ from the ammonium intermediate
   → Curly arrow from N lone pair → H; curly arrow from N–H → N⁺

PENALTIES (from mark schemes):
  • Fishhook arrows penalised ONCE
  • Missing lone pair on nucleophile penalised ONCE
  • K⁺ (spectator ion) should be ignored or noted separately

  Reagent: NaOH(aq) / KOH(aq) / NH₃(ethanol, pressure) / KCN(ethanol)

  Conditions: HEAT (usually)

  Reagent: KOH(ethanol) / NaOH(ethanol)
  Solvent: ETHANOLIC (NOT aqueous)
  Conditions: HEAT

  → Do NOT say "reflux" — gas escapes through condenser

Full-Mark Answer Pattern: Reactivity Comparison (2 marks)

Must give TWO distinct reasons from DIFFERENT factors:

STRUCTURAL factor:
  • Tertiary > secondary > primary (SN1 more stable carbocation)
  • SN2 favoured by primary; SN1 by tertiary

HALOGEN factor:
  • C–I < C–Br < C–Cl < C–F in bond enthalpy
  • Weaker bond = lower activation energy = faster reaction

Example answer:
  "A is tertiary so undergoes SN1 which is faster than SN2 in B which is primary.
   Also, C–I bond is weaker than C–Cl bond so requires less energy to break."

Paper Q Marks Nucleophile Halogenoalkane Key Details
Jun 2023 Q19(d)(i) 2 OH⁻ tertiary halogenoalkane SN1 mechanism: C–Br bond breaks first forming carbocation (trigonal planar), then OH⁻ attacks from either side 
SN1 vs SN2:
  • SN1: two-step, rate depends ONLY on halogenoalkane concentration
  • SN2: single-step, rate depends on BOTH halogenoalkane and nucleophile concentration
  • SN1 favoured by: tertiary halogenoalkanes (stable carbocation)
  • SN2 favoured by: primary halogenoalkanes (less steric hindrance)

Alcohol oxidation summary to memorise:

PRIMARY alcohol:
  Mild oxidation (distillation):  RCH₂OH → RCHO  (aldehyde)
  Strong oxidation (reflux):      RCH₂OH → RCOOH  (carboxylic acid)
  Reagent: acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ / K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄
  Colour change: orange → green

SECONDARY alcohol:
  Oxidation (any):               R₂CHOH → R₂C=O  (ketone)
  Reagent: same K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄
  Colour change: orange → green

TERTIARY alcohol:
  NOT oxidised by K₂Cr₂O₇
  NO colour change
  (This is the key distinguishing feature!)

3.2 QWC Alcohol Identification — Jun 2024 Q20(c) (6 marks)

This is the most detailed alcohol test question across all 7 papers.

Three students' suggestions for identifying –OH in:
  • Menthol (secondary alcohol)
  • Linalool (tertiary alcohol, also has C=C)
  • Citronellol (primary alcohol, also has C=C)

Student 1 — K₂Cr₂O₇:
  ✓ Works for menthol (secondary → orange→green)
  ✓ Works for citronellol (primary → orange→green)
  ✗ Does NOT work for linalool (tertiary — no colour change)
  → NOT a valid test for ALL three

Student 2 — KMnO₄:
  ✗ Not specific for –OH
  ✗ Also reacts with C=C in linalool and citronellol
  → Even primary alcohol + C=C gives colour change — not diagnostic

Student 3 — PCl₅:
  ✓ Produces misty fumes of HCl with ALL alcohols (primary, secondary, tertiary)
  ✓ Reacts with –OH group specifically (not C=C)
  → BEST test — distinguishes –OH from C=C

Marking: 4 indicative content marks + 2 structure/reasoning marks

E/Z isomerism from elimination:

If the halogenoalkane has an unsymmetrical structure, elimination can give:
  • Multiple alkene products (depending on which H is eliminated)
  • E and Z isomers for each alkene with different substituents

Example (Jan 2023 Q14): CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₂CH₂CH₃
  → hex-2-ene (E and Z) + hex-3-ene (E and Z) = 4 products

Symmetrical halogenoalkanes → fewer products
Branching near the halogen → fewer possible elimination sites
