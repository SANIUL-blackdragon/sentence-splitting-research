WPH12 Extension — Past Papers Index & Study Framework

Scope: Vocabulary for sourcing, sitting, marking and learning from real Edexcel International A-level (IAL) Physics Unit 2 (WPH12/01) examination papers. This extension is the Unit 2 counterpart of the WPH11 ext-past-papers-index set: same framework, adapted for the Waves and Electricity content of Unit 2.

Source: Pearson Edexcel IAL Physics specification (admin & assessment guidance), the 13 WPH12/01 paper+mark-scheme pairs held in output/past-papers/physics/wph12/, and the cross-paper statistics already compiled in ch2.3-definitions.md, ch2.4-definitions.md, ch2.3-exam-connection.md, ch2.4-exam-connection.md and ext-uncertainty-exam-connections.md (WPH11 version, methodology applies here too).

Purpose: Exam-ready definitions for every term a student needs in order to find, file, sit, mark and learn from WPH12 past papers. Real Pearson papers are copyrighted and must be sourced externally — this file is an index/framework, not a reproduction of any paper.

1. Paper Identity & Code Terms

1.1 Paper Code (WPH12/01)

Pearson Edexcel ref: Unit 2: Waves and Electricity — written examination.

Definition: The unique four-character + two-digit code that identifies a specific Pearson Edexcel IAL Physics Unit 2 paper. WPH12 denotes Physics Unit 2 (Waves and Electricity). The /01 suffix denotes the standard (January or May/June or October) sitting variant. Other variants (/02, /R) exist for re-sit or regional alternatives but /01 is the version almost universally sat.

WPH12 Exam context: Every paper in the repo is filed as WPH12_<session>_qp.pdf and WPH12_<session>_ms.pdf. Students must verify they are sitting/printed the /01 variant when practising — the alternative variants test the same spec but with different question wording. WPH12/01 is paired with WPH11/01 (Unit 1) to form the AS-level Physics aggregation; both must be sat before progressing to A2 (WPH14/15/16).

1.2 Unit 2 (Waves and Electricity)

Pearson Edexcel ref: Specification §2.3 (spec points 33–63) + §2.4 (spec points 64–80) — 48 outcomes total.

Definition: The second AS-level unit of the IAL Physics qualification, covering 31 waves-and-light outcomes (wave properties, longitudinal/transverse, superposition, stationary waves, refraction, diffraction, polarisation, wave-particle duality, photoelectric effect, atomic line spectra) and 17 electricity outcomes (current, p.d., resistance, resistivity, Kirchhoff's laws, series/parallel, power, internal resistance, potential dividers, V-I graphs, sensor circuits). Assessed by a single 1 h 30 min written paper worth 80 raw marks.

WPH12 Exam context: A student cannot pass WPH12 by knowing only waves OR only electricity — every paper tests both strands. Cross-paper statistics in ch2.3-definitions.md and ch2.4-definitions.md show ~56 marks of waves content and ~29 marks of electricity content per paper on average, summing (with cross-cutting uncertainty and practical skills marks) to the full 80. The waves strand is therefore roughly twice as heavy as the electricity strand — a fact that must drive revision time allocation.

1.3 Sitting

Pearson Edexcel ref: Assessment administration calendar.

Definition: A single examination event on a specific date. The IAL Physics calendar normally offers three sittings per year: January, May/June and October. Each sitting produces its own paper and mark scheme. WPH12/01 has been sat at every available sitting since the qualification was introduced (January 2019 was the first WPH12 sitting of the 2018 specification).

WPH12 Exam context: "How many sittings of WPH12/01 are available from Jan 2019 to Jan 2026?" → 20 sittings (Jan 2019, Jun 2019, Oct 2019, Jan 2020, Oct 2020, Jan 2021, Oct 2021, Jan 2022, Jun 2022, Oct 2022, Jan 2023, Jun 2023, Oct 2023, Jan 2024, Jun 2024, Oct 2024, Jan 2025, Jun 2025, Oct 2025, Jan 2026). Jun 2020 was cancelled globally due to COVID-19 — there is no Jun 2020 paper. May/June 2021 was replaced with adapted assessment / centre-assessed grades in many regions for IAL Physics and is excluded from the standard 20-sitting count (see §3.5 below).

1.4 Session

Pearson Edexcel ref: Results release windows.

Definition: Used interchangeably with "sitting" but more formally denotes the administrative window (registration → exam → results) tied to a sitting. The three sessions per year are: January session (results in March), May/June session (results in August), October session (results in January). A paper is identified by its session — e.g. "the January 2024 paper" or "Jan 2024 WPH12/01".

WPH12 Exam context: Students should aim to sit the real WPH12/01 paper in the session that aligns with their school's exam calendar. Most international schools enter students for the May/June session; January and October are commonly used for re-sits. The October session is particularly popular for IAL Physics because the May/June results (released in August) allow time for re-sit preparation.

2. Paper Structure Terms

2.1 Section A

Pearson Edexcel ref: WPH12/01 paper front cover — "Section A".

Definition: The first section of the WPH12/01 paper, worth 40 marks, consisting of ~10 short-answer multiple-choice questions (MCQs, 1 mark each, ~10 marks total) plus structured short-answer questions testing breadth across the spec. Questions are typically 1–4 marks each, with little scaffolding.

WPH12 Exam context: Section A rewards rapid recall and procedural fluency. Cross-paper statistics (see ch2.3-exam-connection.md and ch2.4-exam-connection.md) show every Section A contains at least one MCQ on wave properties (1 mark, e.g. Oct 2022 Q1 — wave speed quantities, Jan 2022 Q2 — de Broglie expression, Jun 2022 Q2 — what cannot be found from a displacement–time graph), at least one MCQ on circuits (1 mark, e.g. phase/path difference, drift velocity), and at least one structured short-answer on resistivity or photoelectric effect. Time guidance: complete Section A in ~50 minutes (≈1.25 min per mark).

2.2 Section B

Pearson Edexcel ref: WPH12/01 paper front cover — "Section B".

Definition: The second section of the WPH12/01 paper, worth 40 marks, consisting of 2–3 extended-response questions of 10–16 marks each. Questions are context-rich (a real-world scenario — a CD diffraction grating, an optical fibre with cladding, a strain gauge, a solar cell, a stationary wave in a closed tube) and require sustained multi-step reasoning, often with a Quality of Written Communication (QWC) mark.

WPH12 Exam context: Section B is where most students lose marks. The cross-paper analysis shows every Section B contains one practical/data question (CP5 standing wave, CP6 diffraction grating, CP7 resistivity, OR CP8 internal resistance) worth 12–18 marks, including 4–8 uncertainty marks (see ext-uncertainty-definitions.md). QWC questions in WPH12 typically occur in Section B and are worth 6 marks each (e.g. Oct 2022 Q15a(ii) beats, Jun 2025 Q17a stationary wave formation, Oct 2023 Q17a wave-particle duality). Time guidance: complete Section B in ~40 minutes (≈1 min per mark, leaving 10 minutes checking).

2.3 Multiple-Choice Question (MCQ)

Pearson Edexcel ref: Section A format.

Definition: A question with 4 lettered options (A–D) where only one is correct. MCQs in WPH12 are worth 1 mark each, with ~10 MCQs per paper in Section A. There is no negative marking — students should never leave an MCQ blank.

WPH12 Exam context: The existing ch2.3 and ch2.4 exam-connection files cite 30+ distinct MCQs across the 13 in-repo papers. Recurring MCQ patterns include: wave-property identification (Oct 2022 Q1, Jan 2022 Q2, Jun 2022 Q2, Oct 2023 Q8, Jan 2023 Q2), displacement-zero trap (Oct 2022 Q4, Jan 2022 Q13), diffraction-vs-refraction (Oct 2022 Q8), phase/path difference (Jan 2022 Q9, Jun 2025 Q7, Jan 2025 Q4, Jan 2025 Q7), distance between adjacent nodes (Oct 2023 Q6), longest-wavelength trap (Oct 2022 Q2). MCQs are 10–13 marks per paper (~13% of total) and disproportionately reward fluency over deep reasoning.

2.4 Quality of Written Communication (QWC) Question

Pearson Edexcel ref: QWC guidance — Appendix to specification.

Definition: A question (or part-question) explicitly marked for the structure, clarity and spelling of the written response in addition to physics content. QWC questions are worth 6 marks total: up to 4 marks for Indicative Content (IC) physics points and up to 2 marks for the structure (coherent linkage with connective words such as "because", "therefore", "so").

WPH12 Exam context: Approximately one to two QWC questions per WPH12 paper. The ch2.4 exam-connection file documents 4 Ch 2.4 QWC questions across 13 papers (Oct 2019 Q14a — NTC thermistor; Jun 2022 Q15a — Kirchhoff's laws; Jun 2023 Q14 — LDR circuit power; Jan 2025 Q16b — LDR + internal resistance), and the ch2.3 exam-connection file documents a similar density of Ch 2.3 QWC questions (Oct 2022 Q15a(ii) beats; Jan 2023 Q15a interference at harbour; Jun 2025 Q17a stationary wave; Oct 2023 Q17a wave-particle duality; Jun 2025 Q19b wave-particle comparison). Topics are split roughly 50/50 between waves (beats, harbour interference, stationary waves, wave-particle duality) and electricity (Kirchhoff, LDR, thermistor circuits).

3. Marking & Grading Terms

3.1 Raw Mark

Pearson Edexcel ref: Assessment reporting.

Definition: The actual mark a candidate scores on the paper out of the maximum (80 for WPH12/01). Raw marks are what the examiner writes on the script and what the mark scheme totals. Raw marks are NOT the same as uniform marks (UMS) or grades.

WPH12 Exam context: When a student completes a past paper for practice, the only meaningful score is the raw mark out of 80. To predict a grade, the student must compare the raw mark to the published grade boundary for that specific paper (see §3.4 below).

3.2 Uniform Mark Scale (UMS)

Pearson Edexcel ref: IAL uniform mark conversion.

Definition: A scaled mark that adjusts for variation in paper difficulty across sittings, so that the same UMS corresponds to the same grade in every session. For WPH12 (an AS unit), UMS ranges 0–120 (where 96 = A, 84 = B, 72 = C, 60 = D, 48 = E). The conversion from raw → UMS is published by Pearson with the grade boundaries.

WPH12 Exam context: UMS matters for the final AS/A-level aggregate, NOT for raw practice scoring. A student scoring 60/80 raw on a "hard" WPH12 paper may receive a higher UMS than the same raw score on an "easy" paper. Students should track raw marks during practice and use UMS only when comparing their school's predicted grade.

3.3 Grade Boundary

Pearson Edexcel ref: Grade boundary documents — published per session.

Definition: The minimum raw mark required to achieve a given grade on a specific paper in a specific session. Grade boundaries are NOT fixed — they are set by Pearson after each session based on statistical analysis of candidate performance and paper difficulty. Boundaries are published ~6 weeks after results day, alongside the mark scheme.

WPH12 Exam context: For WPH12/01 the typical raw-mark ranges are:

  • A boundary: ~75–80% (60–64 / 80) — note: A is awarded at A2 level on the aggregate, not on the AS unit itself, but Pearson publishes an indicative A* raw mark for AS units to support tracking.
  • A boundary: ~65–70% (52–56 / 80)
  • B boundary: ~55–60% (44–48 / 80)
  • C boundary: ~50–55% (40–44 / 80)
  • D boundary: ~45–50% (36–40 / 80)
  • E boundary: ~40–45% (32–36 / 80)

These ranges vary by ±3 marks between sessions. The WPH12 boundaries tend to sit 0–2 marks lower than the WPH11 boundaries at the A and A* levels because WPH12 has a slightly larger Section B practical/uncertainty load that students find harder. Always consult the official boundary document for the specific paper being sat.

3.4 A* Boundary

Pearson Edexcel ref: A* awarding rules — IAL specification.

Definition: The minimum raw mark (or UMS) required to achieve the A grade. For IAL Physics, A is awarded at A2 level based on: (a) an A grade overall across all 6 units, AND (b) ≥90% UMS averaged across the A2 units (WPH14, WPH15, WPH16). The AS units (WPH11, WPH12, WPH13) do NOT carry A* on their own — they cap at A.

WPH12 Exam context: Pearson still publishes an indicative A raw boundary for AS units so students can track progress toward the final A target. For WPH12/01 this indicative boundary typically sits at 60–64/80 (75–80%). Treating this indicative boundary as a target during practice is a sound strategy. A student targeting A overall should aim for ≥64/80 on WPH12/01 to keep the A achievable on the A2 aggregate.

3.5 COVID-Adjusted Grading

Pearson Edexcel ref: Special arrangements 2020/2021.

Definition: The modified grading process applied to the Oct 2020, Jan 2021 and (where sat) Jun 2021 sessions in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. Pearson reduced content (some spec points were examined less heavily), widened grade boundaries, and in some regions replaced the exam with centre-assessed grades. These adjustments make the 2020–2021 sessions atypical and statistically NOT comparable to pre-2020 or post-2021 sessions.

WPH12 Exam context: The WPH12 repo does NOT contain the COVID-affected sessions (Oct 2020, Jan 2021, Jun 2021) — they are missing from output/past-papers/physics/wph12/. The cross-paper statistics in ch2.3-definitions.md and ch2.4-definitions.md describe "13 papers" — all of which are non-COVID papers. Students should:

  • USE COVID papers for content/topic practice (the physics is unchanged), if they can be sourced externally.
  • EXCLUDE COVID papers from grade-boundary trend analysis (boundaries were inflated by ~3–5 marks at the A boundary).
  • EXCLUDE COVID papers from "which topics are most heavily tested" statistics (topic weighting was modified).

4. Examiner Feedback Terms

4.1 Mark Scheme (MS)

Pearson Edexcel ref: Mark scheme document — published per paper.

Definition: The official document, released by Pearson alongside results (~6 weeks after the exam), that specifies exactly how each question is marked. For each question it lists: the correct answer(s), the marks available, the acceptable alternative answers, and the mark scheme wording (the exact phrasing that must appear in a response to score each mark). Mark schemes also include "guidance" notes clarifying what does NOT score.

WPH12 Exam context: Mark schemes are pedantic. The recurring examiner-report theme (across both ch2.3 and ch2.4) is "students gave the right idea but the wrong phrasing". For 2-mark questions, the mark scheme typically has 3 acceptable points and the candidate needs any 2 — students should learn to identify all acceptable points, not just one. The ch2.3 and ch2.4 exam-connection files document the exact mark-scheme wording for ~80 distinct question types across the 13 in-repo papers.

4.2 Examiner Report (ER)

Pearson Edexcel ref: Principal Examiner report — published per paper.

Definition: The official narrative document, published alongside the mark scheme, in which the Principal Examiner for WPH12 reports on candidate performance for that specific sitting. For each question the report describes: the average mark scored, common errors, misconceptions observed, examples of strong responses, and advice for future candidates. Examiner reports are the single most valuable study resource after the papers themselves.

WPH12 Exam context: Examiner reports are the source of every "common error" cited in the WPH12 study guides (e.g. "students omitted 'perpendicular to the direction of travel' in the polarisation definition" appears in the Jun 2025 examiner report — see ch2.3-definitions.md §7; "students used diameter instead of radius in A = πr²" appears in 4+ examiner reports — see ch2.4-exam-connection.md §1). Reading the examiner report for a paper immediately after self-marking the paper doubles the learning value of the practice session.

4.3 Mark Scheme Wording (Indicative Content)

Pearson Edexcel ref: Mark scheme notation — underlined phrases.

Definition: The exact phrases listed in a mark scheme that an examiner will accept for a mark. In the published mark schemes, indicative content is often underlined to show the keywords the examiner is searching for. Synonyms may or may not be accepted — the mark scheme lists the accepted variants.

WPH12 Exam context: Examples from the existing study guides:

  • Polarisation (Jun 2025 Q12b(i), 3 marks): must include "perpendicular to the direction of travel/propagation" — omitting this loses a mark (ch2.3-definitions.md §7).
  • Resistivity definition (Jun 2024 Q14a(i), 1 mark): "a property of the material, independent of its dimensions" — saying "resistance per unit length" does NOT score (ch2.4-exam-connection.md §1).
  • QWC structure marks (all WPH12 QWC questions): must use connective language ("because", "therefore", "so", "which means"); listing isolated physics points scores 0/2 for structure even if the physics content is correct (ch2.3-exam-connection.md §3, ch2.4-exam-connection.md QWC section).
  • Photoelectric effect one-to-one interaction: "a single photon interacts with a single electron" — paraphrasing as "photons hit electrons" does NOT score (ch2.3-definitions.md §12).

4.4 Principal Examiner

Pearson Edexcel ref: Senior examiner role.

Definition: The senior examiner responsible for setting the paper, training the team of assistant examiners, writing the mark scheme, and authoring the examiner report. The Principal Examiner's name appears on the front of the mark scheme. The same Principal Examiner often oversees WPH12 across multiple sittings, which is why certain question styles recur.

WPH12 Exam context: When a particular phrasing or question style appears repeatedly across sittings (e.g. the "show that" question with intermediate values required — appearing in 13/13 papers; the d-vs-1/d diffraction grating trap — appearing in Oct 2022 Q18c, Jun 2023 and Jan 2025 Q14b(i)), it reflects the Principal Examiner's preference. Students who study the last 5 sittings are effectively studying the current Principal Examiner's style.

5. Practice & Study Terms

5.1 Past Paper

Pearson Edexcel ref: Pearson publishes past papers on its website ~6 weeks after results, freely downloadable (registration required) for student practice.

Definition: A real WPH12/01 examination paper from a previous sitting, used by students for timed practice and revision. "Past paper" collectively refers to the question paper (QP), mark scheme (MS), and examiner report (ER) for a single sitting. Pearson retains past papers on its website indefinitely.

WPH12 Exam context: The existing WPH12 study-guide ERQ files (ch2.3-exam-readiness-questions.md, ch2.4-exam-readiness-questions.md) are practice questions written by the team, NOT real Pearson papers. The complete index of every WPH12/01 paper from Jan 2019 → Jan 2026 (20 sittings, of which 13 are in the repo) is in ext-past-papers-index-exam-connections.md §1.

5.2 Question Paper (QP)

Pearson Edexcel ref: The actual exam booklet.

Definition: The physical (or PDF) examination paper as the candidate receives it: cover sheet with paper code, instructions, formula sheet (Appendix 8 of the spec), Section A questions, Section B questions, and any data/figure pages. For WPH12/01 the QP is typically 24–32 pages long, slightly longer than WPH11 because the waves content often requires more figures (wave diagrams, ray diagrams, energy-level diagrams, circuit diagrams).

WPH12 Exam context: When sitting a past paper for practice, the student should print the QP (not just read on screen) and write answers on the paper as in the real exam. Screen-based practice does not build the handwriting speed or spatial awareness required for the real exam. The formula sheet on the front of the WPH12/01 paper includes the wave equation v = fλ, Snell's law n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, the diffraction grating equation nλ = d sin θ, the photoelectric equation hf = φ + ½mv²_max, the de Broglie equation λ = h/p, the resistivity equation R = ρL/A, the drift velocity equation I = nAve, and the power equations P = IV = I²R = V²/R — students must know which equation to use without looking it up.

5.3 Timed Practice

Pearson Edexcel ref: Exam-condition simulation.

Definition: Completing a past paper under examination conditions: 1 hour 30 minutes (90 minutes) total, no breaks, no textbook, no notes, no phone, only an approved calculator and the Pearson data sheet. The student must finish within 90 minutes, even if some questions are unanswered, to build time discipline.

WPH12 Exam context: Untimed practice inflates scores by 8–12 marks (a 10–15% overestimate) because students linger on hard questions. The real paper rewards disciplined time allocation — see ext-past-papers-index-concepts.md §2 for the timed-practice method.

5.4 Self-Marking

Pearson Edexcel ref: Practical study method.

Definition: The student marks their own completed past paper using the official mark scheme, awarding marks strictly per the mark scheme wording (no half-marks, no "I had the right idea"). Self-marking forces the student to confront the gap between their answer and the mark scheme wording — this is where most learning happens.

WPH12 Exam context: Self-marking is most effective when done immediately after the timed practice session, while the student remembers their reasoning. Marking delayed by a day or more loses ~50% of the learning value. See ext-past-papers-index-concepts.md §3 for the mark-scheme analysis technique, including the three-layer mark-scheme read and the phrase-bank construction method.

WPH12 Extension — Past Papers Index & Study Framework (Concepts)

Scope: How to use real WPH12/01 past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports effectively — the working method that turns "I have 13 papers in the repo" into "I am exam-ready." This file connects the definitions in ext-past-papers-index-definitions.md into a structured practice routine.

Source: Pearson Edexcel IAL Physics admin guidance + the 13 WPH12/01 papers held in output/past-papers/physics/wph12/ + the cross-paper statistics already compiled in ch2.3-definitions.md, ch2.4-definitions.md, ch2.3-exam-connection.md and ch2.4-exam-connection.md.

1. Why Past Papers Are the Highest-Yield Activity

Every mark available in WPH12/01 has been earned by some combination of (a) physics content knowledge (waves + electricity), (b) procedural fluency in standard calculations (Snell's law, diffraction grating, resistivity, internal resistance, drift velocity), (c) exam technique (timing, command-word response, mark-scheme phrasing) and (d) practical skills (uncertainty, graphing, evaluation, CP5/CP6/CP7/CP8 method).

The existing study guides (ch2.3-, ch2.4-, ext-uncertainty-, ext-cp4-8-methods-) cover (a), (b) and (d). They cannot, by themselves, develop (c) — exam technique. Exam technique is a learned skill that requires repeated exposure to real papers under timed conditions, followed by careful self-marking and study of the examiner report.

A student who has worked through 8–10 past papers (timed + self-marked + examiner report read) typically scores 10–15 raw marks higher than an equally-prepared student who has only completed practice questions from the study guides. This is the difference between a B and an A*.

For WPH12 specifically, exam-technique matters MORE than for WPH11 because:

  • The waves content includes high-mark QWC questions (6 marks each) where structure/connective language is decisive.
  • The electricity content includes 3–4 mark "show that" calculations where every intermediate value must be shown.
  • The cross-strand practical questions (CP5–CP8) require fluency in BOTH content strands simultaneously (e.g. a CP6 diffraction grating question combines wave physics with measurement uncertainty).

2. The Timed Practice Method

2.1 Setup — Replicating Exam Conditions

The goal of timed practice is to reproduce the cognitive and physiological conditions of the real exam, so the brain builds the right automaticity.

Required conditions:

  1. Time: 1 hour 30 minutes (90 minutes) total. Use a countdown timer visible at all times. No pausing.
  2. No aids: Only an approved calculator (the one to be used in the real exam), the Pearson data sheet (or Appendix 8 of the spec), pen, pencil, ruler, protractor (for ray diagrams), eraser. No textbook, no notes, no phone, no internet.
  3. Environment: A quiet room, sitting at a desk, door closed. No music. No drinks break.
  4. Materials: Print the question paper (do not read on screen). Write answers on the printed paper as in the real exam. Use the same pen/pencil you will use in the exam. Have spare pens/pencils within reach.
  5. Mental state: Sit the paper at the same time of day as the real exam (most IAL papers are sat mid-morning, ~09:00–10:30 local time). Do not eat a heavy meal immediately before.

2.2 Time Allocation Within the 90 Minutes

Section Marks Suggested time Pace
Section A (MCQs + structured short answer) 40 50 min 1.25 min/mark
Section B (extended response) 40 35 min 0.9 min/mark
Checking / buffer — 5 min —
Total 80 90 min —

Practical tip: Set the timer to 50 minutes for Section A. When the timer rings, move to Section B regardless of whether Section A is complete. This forces the discipline of not over-investing in any single MCQ. The remaining 40 minutes is for Section B + checking.

2.3 In-the-Moment Decision Rules

These are the rules that separate A/A* students from B students under time pressure:

  1. MCQ rule: If you cannot solve an MCQ in 90 seconds, circle the question, mark your best guess in pencil, and move on. Return only if time remains at the end. Never leave an MCQ blank — there is no negative marking.
  2. "Show that" rule: A "show that" question gives the answer. Use the given answer as a check — work backwards if forward working stalls. The mark is for the working, not the answer. Example: Jan 2025 Q17c(i) "show that f² = T/(4L²μ)" — write v = fλ, then λ = 4L (closed tube fundamental), then v = √(T/μ), then substitute and rearrange to f² = T/(4L²μ). Every step is shown.
  3. "State" rule: A "state" question wants one phrase, no working. Do not write a paragraph. If you cannot answer in one line, you are overcomplicating it.
  4. "Calculate" rule: Show every step. Even if the calculator gives the answer in one keystroke, write the substituted formula first (e.g. nλ = d sin θ → 2 × 550 × 10⁻⁹ = (1/300 000) × sin θ → sin θ = 0.330 → θ = 19.3°), then the result with units. Method marks are awarded even if the arithmetic is wrong.
  5. Stuck rule: If a question has not yielded a useful equation after 60 seconds of thinking, move to the next question. Return with fresh eyes later — most "stuck" moments resolve themselves after a 2-minute break on a different question.
  6. Last 5 minutes rule: Use the final 5 minutes to (a) fill in any blank MCQs with a best guess, (b) check that every numerical answer has units, (c) check that every calculated answer has appropriate sig figs (see ext-uncertainty-concepts.md §7), (d) verify any ray-diagram angles are measured from the normal (not from the surface — the angle-from-surface trap appears in 7+ papers per ch2.3-definitions.md §6).

3. The Mark Scheme Analysis Technique

3.1 Why Self-Marking Must Be Ruthless

The single most common mistake in self-marking is generosity — awarding marks for "having the right idea" when the mark scheme requires a specific phrase or numerical value. This generosity inflates the practice score by 5–10 marks and gives a false sense of readiness.

Rule: Mark as the examiner would mark. If the answer does not contain the mark scheme wording (or an accepted alternative listed in the mark scheme), score 0 for that mark — even if you "knew what you meant."

3.2 The Three-Layer Mark Scheme Read

For every question, work through the mark scheme in three layers:

Layer 1 — The mark allocation. Identify how many marks are available and what each mark is for. A 3-mark "calculate" question typically has: 1 method mark (correct equation/substitution), 1 processing mark (correct arithmetic), 1 answer mark (correct value with units and sig figs). Knowing this structure tells you what to write even when you cannot solve the question fully.

Layer 2 — The indicative content. Underlined phrases in the mark scheme are the keywords the examiner is searching for. For 2-mark "explain" or "describe" questions, the mark scheme typically lists 3 acceptable points and the candidate scores 2 for any 2 — but only if the exact phrasing (or an accepted variant) is present. Students should rewrite their answer using the mark scheme wording to internalise the phrasing.

Layer 3 — The "guidance" notes. Mark schemes include "guidance" or "do not accept" notes specifying what does NOT score. Examples from the existing WPH12 study guides:

  • Oct 2022 Q13b(i): mark scheme explicitly rejects using diameter directly in A = πr² (ch2.4-exam-connection.md §1).
  • Jun 2025 Q12b(i): polarisation definition must include "perpendicular to the direction of travel/propagation" — omitting this loses a mark (ch2.3-definitions.md §7).
  • All WPH12 QWC questions: isolated bullet points with no connective language score 0/2 for structure even if every physics point is correct (ch2.4-exam-connection.md QWC section).

3.3 Building a "Phrase Bank"

After marking each paper, extract every mark scheme phrase that you did NOT use in your answer (or used a paraphrase of). Write these phrases into a dedicated notebook (or text file). Review the phrase bank weekly. After 5 papers the phrase bank will contain ~80 phrases — these are the exact wordings that score.

Example phrase-bank entries from the existing WPH12 study guides:

4. The Examiner Report Study Method

4.1 Reading the Examiner Report in Three Passes

The examiner report is typically 6–10 pages long. Read it in three passes:

Pass 1 — Overview (5 min): Read the opening section (the Principal Examiner's general comments on the sitting). This identifies the overall difficulty of the paper, the average mark, and the Principal Examiner's headline concerns. Use this to contextualise your own score — if the report says "this paper was found harder than usual by candidates" and you scored 60/80, your performance is stronger than 60/80 would suggest in a typical year.

Pass 2 — Question-by-question (30 min): For every question you attempted, read the examiner's commentary on that question. Note:

  • The average mark scored by candidates (the report often says "average 1.2/3" or "this question was poorly answered").
  • The specific errors candidates made (the report names them — these are your future errors to avoid).
  • The "what separated high-scoring candidates" commentary (these are the moves you should replicate).

Pass 3 — Action extraction (10 min): Convert every reported error into a personal action item. For example, if the report says "students forgot to halve the diameter before using A = πr²", write on a sticky note: "Circle every diameter; halve it before any area calculation." Stick this note on the inside cover of your formula sheet for the next paper.

4.2 The "Two Papers, Same Topic" Comparison

The most powerful use of examiner reports is to compare how the same topic was tested across two sittings. For example, the diffraction-grating question appears in 7+ of the 13 in-repo papers — see ch2.3-exam-connection.md §8. Reading the examiner reports for these papers in sequence shows the evolving pattern of:

  • Which step students most often missed (the d-vs-1/d trap — reported in Oct 2022 Q18c, Jun 2023, Jan 2025 Q14b(i)).
  • Which phrasing the examiner rewarded in the most recent sitting (which predicts the next sitting).
  • Whether the "show that" variant or the "calculate" variant was used (which predicts the next question type).

After 5 such comparisons the student can predict the mark scheme for the next sitting's diffraction-grating question with ~70% accuracy.

Similarly for resistivity: the resistivity question appears in 10 of 13 in-repo papers — see ch2.4-exam-connection.md §1. The recurring patterns (graphical method, gradient interpretation, diameter→radius conversion, identify-the-material variant) become visible after reading 3+ examiner reports.

7. Which Papers to Prioritise

7.1 The "Most Recent 5" Rule

The Principal Examiner for WPH12 typically rotates every 3–5 years, and within a Principal Examiner's tenure the question style is stable. The most recent 5 sittings are therefore the best predictor of the next sitting's style.

For a student preparing for the January 2026 sitting, the priority papers are:

  1. Jun 2025 (most recent large-cohort sitting in the repo — most representative of current style).
  2. Jan 2025 (most recent January sitting — same session style as the upcoming exam).
  3. Oct 2024 (most recent October sitting).
  4. May/Jun 2024 (one year before the target).
  5. Jan 2024 (one year before the most recent January sitting).

These 5 papers should be sat first, under timed conditions, in the final 8 weeks before the exam (see §9 below).

7.2 The "Same Session, Last Year" Rule

When preparing for a specific session (e.g. January), the same session one year prior (e.g. previous January) is disproportionately valuable because:

  • January papers tend to share structural features (specific Section B question types) that recur.
  • The Principal Examiner often reuses question "shells" (different numbers, same scenario) within the same session across consecutive years.

For January 2026 preparation: sit Jan 2025, Jan 2024, Jan 2023, Jan 2022 in sequence. Compare the four papers' Section B questions side-by-side — the pattern of recurring scenarios becomes visible.

7.3 The "Skip the COVID" Rule for the First Practice Papers

For the first 2–3 timed practice papers (when the student is still building exam technique), use non-COVID papers exclusively. COVID papers' content reductions make them slightly easier, which can mask technique weaknesses. Reserve COVID papers (if sourced externally) for later in the practice sequence when the student is already confident.

8. Tracking Progress Across Papers

8.1 The Progress Spreadsheet

Maintain a single spreadsheet (or markdown table) with one row per paper sat and the following columns:

Date sat Session Raw mark (/80) A boundary for that paper Margin vs A Section A mark (/40) Section B mark (/40) Uncertainty marks lost Waves marks lost Electricity marks lost QWC marks lost Notes

Fill in the spreadsheet immediately after self-marking each paper. After 5 entries the patterns become visible:

  • A consistent negative margin vs A → too weak on content; revisit ch2.3- or ch2.4-.
  • Consistently strong Section A but weak Section B → exam-technique issue; the issue is multi-step reasoning, not content.
  • Consistently weak Section A but strong Section B → fluency issue; the issue is rapid recall, not deep reasoning.
  • High "uncertainty marks lost" → work through ext-uncertainty-* end to end.
  • High "waves marks lost" → review ch2.3-* and the wave-specific MCQ traps in ch2.3-exam-connection.md.
  • High "electricity marks lost" → review ch2.4-*, especially resistivity (§1) and internal resistance (§3) which are the highest-yield Ch 2.4 topics.
  • High "QWC marks lost" → drill the connective-language structure pattern in ext-circuits-qwc-* and the QWC examples in ch2.3-exam-connection.md.

8.2 The Weekly Review

Once per week, review the spreadsheet and answer three questions:

  1. Which mark category did I lose the most marks in this week? → That is next week's study focus.
  2. Which paper showed my highest margin vs A? → That paper's strengths should be reproduced.
  3. Am I on track for my target grade? → If margin-vs-target is consistently negative, increase study intensity.

8.3 The Pre-Exam Confidence Check

In the final week before the exam, sit the most recent available paper under exam conditions and self-mark. The result of this paper is the most accurate single predictor of exam-day performance. If the result is at or above target, the student is ready. If below target, focus the final 3 days on the highest-impact weakness (typically uncertainty, resistivity, or a specific waves topic).

9.5 The "Always-Tested" Sub-Topics

Within the Critical/High topics, the following sub-topics appear in EVERY paper and are worth ~1–4 marks each:

  • One wave-property MCQ (v = fλ or "what is constant when light enters glass" — frequency — see ch2.3-exam-connection.md §1).
  • One refraction/Snell's law calculation (2–4 marks) — angle-from-surface trap on at least one of these.
  • One diffraction-grating calculation (3–5 marks) — d-vs-1/d conversion trap.
  • One photoelectric-effect calculation (3–5 marks) — eV ↔ joules conversion, threshold frequency.
  • One resistivity calculation OR resistivity graphical question (2–5 marks) — diameter→radius conversion.
  • One V-I graph or sensor-circuit question (2–4 marks) — thermistor or LDR.
  • One CP5/CP6/CP7/CP8 practical-method question (4–8 marks) — apparatus, method, uncertainty.
  • One "suggest an improvement" question (1–2 marks).

These eight sub-topics together account for ~25–35 marks per paper — almost half the total. They are the highest-yield targets for the final 2 weeks of revision.

7. Final Notes & Limitations

  • Real Pearson past paper content. This file is an INDEX/FRAMEWORK only. Real WPH12/01 papers are copyrighted by Pearson Education and must be sourced externally from the official Pearson qualification page or a school exam officer.
  • Exact grade boundary values. All boundary values in this file are indicative ranges based on typical WPH12/01 patterns. Exact values vary by session and are published by Pearson alongside results. The ranges used (A* 60–64, A 52–56, B 44–48) match those specified in the task brief.
