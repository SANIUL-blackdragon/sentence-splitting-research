Q1 QFR §64 1 mark · ~1 min
Define electric current.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge (the charge passing a point per unit time).
Marking Points
  • B1 Rate of flow of (electric) charge
NOT Accepted

"Flow of electrons" alone (describes electron flow, not the definition of current — ions can also carry current); "movement of charge" without "rate of"; "what flows in a wire".

⚠️ Common Error

Describing the mechanism (electrons moving) instead of giving the formal definition. The MS for Jan 2020 Q1(a) required "rate of flow of charge" specifically.

Q2 QFR §65 1 mark · ~1 min
Define potential difference.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: The energy transferred (work done) per unit charge between two points in a circuit.
Marking Points
  • B1 Energy transferred per unit charge (accept "work done per unit charge")
NOT Accepted

"The push of electricity"; "the voltage across a component" (circular definition); "energy per second" (that is power, not p.d.).

⚠️ Common Error

Confusing potential difference with current or power. "Energy per second" is a power definition, not a p.d. definition.

Q3 QFR §67a 1 mark · ~1 min
State Kirchhoff's first law.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: At any junction (node) in an electrical circuit, the sum of the currents entering the junction equals the sum of the currents leaving the junction.
Marking Points
  • B1 Sum of currents entering a junction = sum of currents leaving (accept "algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero")
NOT Accepted

"Voltage is the same in parallel" (that is a consequence, not the law); "Current is conserved" without mentioning a junction (too vague — the MS for Jun 2024 Q9 required explicit mention of the junction).

⚠️ Common Error

Stating Kirchhoff's second law instead. Adding "the current splits equally" (it does not necessarily split equally).

Q4 QFR §69 1 mark · ~1 min
Define electromotive force (e.m.f.).
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: The electromotive force (e.m.f.) of a source is the energy transferred per unit charge by the source when charge flows through it.
Marking Points
  • B1 Energy (transferred/converted/work done) per unit charge (by/through the source)
NOT Accepted

"The force that pushes electrons" (e.m.f. is NOT a force); "the voltage of a cell" (voltage is a p.d.); omitting "per unit charge"; "the p.d. across a cell" (terminal p.d. ≠ e.m.f. when current flows).

⚠️ Common Error

The word "force" in "electromotive force" misleads students into thinking it is a force. E.m.f. is an energy-per-charge quantity (measured in volts), not a force (measured in newtons).

Q5 TDC §69 1 mark · ~1 min
State the equation relating terminal potential difference, e.m.f., current, and internal resistance.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: V = ε − Ir
Marking Points
  • B1 V = ε − Ir (accept ℰ or E for e.m.f. symbol)
NOT Accepted

V = ε + Ir (wrong sign — would mean terminal p.d. exceeds e.m.f., which is physically impossible); V = IR (this is Ohm's law, not the e.m.f. equation).

⚠️ Common Error

Writing V = ε + Ir. This is the single most common sign error in Ch 2.4. The lost volts (Ir) must be subtracted from the e.m.f. to give the terminal p.d.

Q6 EXP §64 3 marks · ~3 min
Explain, in terms of the motion of conduction electrons and lattice ions, why a metal wire has resistance.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: When a p.d. is applied across a metal wire, the free electrons are accelerated by the electric field and gain kinetic energy. However, the electrons repeatedly collide with the vibrating lattice ions. At each collision, the electrons transfer energy to the lattice (heating the wire) and lose their directed velocity. This repeated scattering randomises the electron motion and limits the mean drift velocity, which manifests macroscopically as resistance.
Marking Points
  • M1 Electrons are accelerated by the electric field / gain kinetic energy
  • M1 Electrons collide with (vibrating) lattice ions
  • A1 Collisions randomise electron velocity / transfer energy to the lattice / limit the drift velocity
NOT Accepted

"Electrons get stuck" or "electrons are repelled by atoms" — must reference collisions with lattice ions specifically. "Opposition to current flow" is a restatement, not a mechanism.

⚠️ Common Error

Describing resistance as "opposition to current flow" without any microscopic mechanism. The MS for Jun 2019 Q4(b) explicitly required the collision mechanism.

Q7 WTE §67a 2 marks · ~3 min
Explain how Kirchhoff's first law is a consequence of the conservation of charge.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: Conservation of charge states that electric charge cannot be created or destroyed. At a junction, the charge flowing into the junction per unit time must equal the charge flowing out per unit time. Since current is defined as the rate of flow of charge (I = ΔQ/Δt), and the time interval is the same for all branches, the sum of currents entering must equal the sum of currents leaving.
Marking Points
  • E1 Charge cannot be created or destroyed / is conserved
  • E2 Therefore charge per unit time flowing in = charge per unit time flowing out at a junction, and since I = ΔQ/Δt, the sum of currents in equals sum of currents out
NOT Accepted

"Charge is conserved so current is conserved" (too vague — must explicitly link charge conservation to current via the rate definition).

⚠️ Common Error

Stating Kirchhoff's first law without explaining why it follows from charge conservation. The key step is connecting charge (Q) to current (I) via I = ΔQ/Δt.

Q8 DIA §70 4 marks · ~5 min
Sketch separate I–V characteristic graphs for each of the following components. In each case, label both axes and indicate the key features of the graph.
(a) A metallic conductor at constant temperature (ohmic conductor).
(b) A filament lamp.
(c) A silicon diode.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: Three I–V graphs sketched (V on x-axis, I on y-axis): (a) straight line through origin; (b) symmetric curve through origin with decreasing gradient; (c) zero current until ~0.6 V forward bias, then sharp rise.

Part (a) — Ohmic conductor: A straight line through the origin with constant gradient (I is directly proportional to V).

Part (b) — Filament lamp: A curve through the origin with decreasing gradient as |V| increases (the graph becomes less steep). The curve is symmetric in the first and third quadrants (same shape for positive and negative V).

Part (c) — Silicon diode: Approximately zero current for negative p.d. and for positive p.d. below ~0.6 V. Above approximately 0.6 V in the forward-biased direction, the current increases sharply.

Marking Points
  • B1 Part (a): Straight line through the origin; both axes labelled
  • B1 Part (b): Curve through the origin with decreasing gradient; symmetric about the origin
  • B1 Part (c): Approximately zero current for negative V and for positive V < 0.6 V
  • B1 Part (c): Sharp increase in current above threshold of ~0.6 V in forward direction
NOT Accepted

Drawing the diode curve in both quadrants (diodes conduct in only one direction). Drawing the filament lamp as a straight line. Omitting axis labels.

⚠️ Common Error

For the diode, drawing a curve that rises gradually rather than showing a sharp threshold at ~0.6 V. The MS requires the near-zero current region and the sharp transition to be clearly distinguishable.

Q9 WTE §70, §79 4 marks · ~5 min
A student is asked to explain why the resistance of a metal wire increases with temperature while the resistance of an NTC thermistor decreases with temperature. Their answer is: "The resistance changes because of temperature. In both cases the atoms vibrate more, which changes the resistance." Explain what is wrong with this answer and write a correct explanation for each component.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: The student's answer is wrong because it applies the same mechanism (lattice vibrations) to both components. "Lattice vibrations" is the mechanism for metals only — it does not explain thermistor behaviour.

Metal: As temperature increases, the lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude. This increases the frequency of collisions between conduction electrons and lattice ions, increasing the scattering of electrons and therefore increasing the resistance.

NTC thermistor: As temperature increases, more charge carriers are released (promoted) into the conduction band. This increases the number density of charge carriers (n). Since resistance is inversely related to the number of charge carriers, the resistance decreases.

Marking Points
  • C1 Identifies the error: the student applies the same mechanism to both; "lattice vibrations" is not the mechanism for semiconductors
  • C2 Metal: increased lattice ion vibrations → increased electron scattering → increased resistance
  • C3 Thermistor: increased temperature → more charge carriers released into conduction band → increased n → decreased resistance
  • C4 Clear contrast between the two different mechanisms
NOT Accepted

"Both have increased vibrations so both resistances increase"; "The thermistor has more energy so resistance decreases" (no mechanism); "Electrons move faster in the thermistor" (it is charge carrier number that increases, not their speed).

⚠️ Common Error

This is the #1 exam trap in Ch 2.4, appearing in 4 of 13 past papers. The MS for Jan 2022 and Oct 2023 specifically penalised students who used "lattice vibrations" to explain thermistor behaviour. The metal mechanism (vibrations) and semiconductor mechanism (charge carrier release) are fundamentally different.

Q10 DER §65 3 marks · ~4 min
The volt is defined as 1 joule per coulomb and the ampere as 1 coulomb per second. Show that 1 V = 1 W A⁻¹, where W represents watts. (Bald answer = 0 marks — every algebraic step must be shown.)
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: 1 V = 1 W A⁻¹ (shown via unit substitution: 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹ → 1 W s C⁻¹ → 1 W A⁻¹).

Starting from the definition of the volt:

1 V = 1 J C⁻¹

Since 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹, we can write 1 J = 1 W s. Substituting:

1 V = (1 W s) × C⁻¹ = 1 W s C⁻¹

Since 1 A = 1 C s⁻¹, therefore 1 s C⁻¹ = 1 A⁻¹. Substituting:

1 V = 1 W × A⁻¹ = 1 W A⁻¹ ✓

Marking Points
  • M1 Starts from 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹
  • M1 Expresses joules in terms of watts and seconds: 1 J = 1 W s (from 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹)
  • A1 Substitutes and uses 1 A = 1 C s⁻¹ to show 1 V = 1 W A⁻¹
NOT Accepted

Writing "1 V = 1 W A⁻¹" directly without derivation (bald answer = 0 marks). Skipping intermediate steps.

⚠️ Common Error

Students often write the final answer immediately, losing all 3 marks. Every algebraic manipulation must be shown for "show that" questions. This is a unit-derivation question, not a recall question.

Q11 CAL §73 3 marks · ~4 min
A copper wire of cross-sectional area 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ m² carries a current of 2.4 A. The number density of free electrons in copper is 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. Calculate the drift velocity of the free electrons in the wire.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: v = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹ (≈ 0.12 mm s⁻¹).

v = I/(nAe) = 2.4 / (8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 2.4 / (2.04 × 10⁴) = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹

Marking Points
  • M1 Correct rearrangement and substitution into v = I/(nAe)
  • M1 Correct evaluation of the denominator: nAe = 2.04 × 10⁴ (allow 2.0–2.1 × 10⁴)
  • A1 v = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹ (allow 1.15–1.20 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹)
NOT Accepted

Answer without unit; answer with incorrect magnitude (e.g. 1.2 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹ — this is faster than light!); using diameter instead of cross-sectional area.

⚠️ Common Error

Getting the magnitude completely wrong. Drift velocity in metals is always very small (~10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹), because the number density of free electrons is enormous. If your answer comes out much larger than 1 mm s⁻¹, check your calculation.

Q12 CAL §77 3 marks · ~3 min
An LED has a potential difference of 2.1 V across it and a current of 18 mA through it. Calculate the power dissipated by the LED.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: P = 0.038 W (or 38 mW).

P = VI = 2.1 × 0.018 = 0.0378 W = 0.038 W (or 38 mW)

Marking Points
  • M1 Conversion of 18 mA to 0.018 A
  • M1 Correct substitution into P = VI
  • A1 0.038 W or 38 mW (accept 0.0378 W for 3 s.f.)
NOT Accepted

37.8 W (failed to convert mA to A — this is 1000× too large); 2.1 × 18 = 37.8 "W" without unit conversion; answers without units.

⚠️ Common Error

The mA-to-A conversion trap. Multiplying 2.1 × 18 directly gives 37.8, which is wrong by a factor of 1000. Always convert milliamperes to amperes before substituting into P = VI.

Q13 RAT §71 3 marks · ~4 min
Wire P has length l, diameter d, and resistivity ρ. Wire Q has length 2l, diameter d, and is made of the same material. Wire R has length l, diameter 2d, and is made of the same material. State the ratio of resistances Rₚ : Rᵩ : Rᵣ.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: Rₚ : Rᵩ : Rᵣ = 4 : 8 : 1.

Working:

  • R = ρl/A, so Rₚ = ρl/A where A = πd²/4
  • Wire Q: length 2l, same A → Rᵩ = ρ(2l)/A = 2Rₚ
  • Wire R: same length l, diameter 2d → Aᵣ = π(2d)²/4 = 4A → Rᵣ = ρl/(4A) = Rₚ/4
  • Expressing as integer ratio: Rₚ : Rᵩ : Rᵣ = Rₚ : 2Rₚ : Rₚ/4 = 4 : 8 : 1
Marking Points
  • M1 Correct reasoning for Q: doubling length doubles resistance (Rᵩ = 2Rₚ)
  • M1 Correct reasoning for R: doubling diameter quadruples area, so resistance is quartered (Rᵣ = Rₚ/4)
  • A1 Correct final ratio: 4 : 8 : 1
NOT Accepted

1 : 2 : 0.25 (not integer ratio); 4 : 2 : 1 (wrong — confused the effect of doubling diameter); 1 : 2 : 4 (inverted the diameter effect).

⚠️ Common Error

Thinking that doubling the diameter doubles the area (instead of quadrupling it, since A ∝ d²). This gives the ratio 4 : 8 : 2 instead of 4 : 8 : 1. Remember: area depends on the square of the diameter.

Q14 GRD §71 4 marks · ~5 min
A student investigates how the resistance R of a manganin wire varies with its length l. The wire has a constant cross-sectional area of 5.1 × 10⁻⁷ m². The student measures R for different lengths and plots a graph of R against l. The graph is a straight line through the origin with a gradient of 6.7 Ω m⁻¹. Use the graph to determine the resistivity of manganin.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: ρ = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m.

From R = ρl/A, rearranging gives R = (ρ/A) × l. This is of the form y = mx (straight line through origin) where the gradient m = ρ/A.

Therefore: ρ = gradient × A = 6.7 Ω m⁻¹ × 5.1 × 10⁻⁷ m² = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m

Marking Points
  • M1 States or implies R = ρl/A, so R = (ρ/A) × l (straight line with gradient = ρ/A)
  • M1 Identifies gradient of R vs l graph = ρ/A
  • M1 Correct substitution: ρ = 6.7 × 5.1 × 10⁻⁷
  • A1 ρ = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m with correct units (accept 3.42 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m)
NOT Accepted

Using ρ = gradient/A (inverted fraction); using gradient = ρ × A (wrong relationship); omitting units; using m instead of m² for area giving ρ = 3.4 × 10⁻³ Ω m.

⚠️ Common Error

Confusing which variable goes on which axis, then writing gradient = A/ρ instead of ρ/A. Remember: if R is on the y-axis and l is on the x-axis, then gradient = ΔR/Δl = ρ/A, so ρ = gradient × A.

Q15 CAL §69 3 marks · ~4 min
A cell of e.m.f. 9.0 V and internal resistance 1.5 Ω is connected to an external resistor of 10.5 Ω. Calculate (a) the current in the circuit, and (b) the terminal p.d. across the cell.
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: (a) I = 0.75 A; (b) V = 7.9 V.

(a) I = ε/(R + r) = 9.0 V / (10.5 Ω + 1.5 Ω) = 9.0 / 12.0 = 0.75 A

(b) V = IR = 0.75 A × 10.5 Ω = 7.9 V

(or V = ε − Ir = 9.0 − 0.75 × 1.5 = 7.875 ≈ 7.9 V)

Marking Points
  • M1 I = ε/(R + r) = 9.0/12.0 = 0.75 A (method mark for correct equation and total resistance)
  • A1 I = 0.75 A
  • A1 V = 7.9 V (accept 7.88 V from V = ε − Ir route; ECF from incorrect current)
NOT Accepted

Using I = ε/R (ignoring internal resistance) giving I = 0.86 A; using V = ε (ignoring internal resistance) giving V = 9.0 V; answers without units.

⚠️ Common Error

Forgetting to include the internal resistance in the total resistance. The denominator must be (R + r), not just R. Also check: terminal p.d. must be less than e.m.f. when current flows — if you get V > ε, something is wrong.

Q16 DER §67b, §68 4 marks · ~5 min
Starting from Kirchhoff's first and second laws and the definition of resistance R = V/I, derive the formula for the total resistance of two resistors R₁ and R₂ connected in series: Rₜ = R₁ + R₂. (Bald answer = 0 marks — every step must be shown.)
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: Rₜ = R₁ + R₂ (derived from Kirchhoff's first law, Kirchhoff's second law, and V = IR).

By Kirchhoff's first law (conservation of charge), the same current I flows through both resistors since there is no junction between them.

By Kirchhoff's second law (conservation of energy), the sum of p.d.s equals the e.m.f.:

Vₜ = V₁ + V₂

Substituting V = IR for each component:

IRₜ = IR₁ + IR₂

Factoring out I:

IRₜ = I(R₁ + R₂)

Dividing both sides by I:

Rₜ = R₁ + R₂ ✓

Marking Points
  • M1 Same current I through both resistors (Kirchhoff's first law)
  • M1 Vₜ = V₁ + V₂ (Kirchhoff's second law)
  • M1 Substitution: IRₜ = IR₁ + IR₂, then factorising: IRₜ = I(R₁ + R₂)
  • A1 Divides by I to obtain Rₜ = R₁ + R₂
NOT Accepted

Stating the formula without derivation; starting from V = IR alone without citing Kirchhoff's laws; using numerical examples instead of algebraic proof; writing V₁ = I₁R₁ with different currents (current is the same in series).

⚠️ Common Error

Not explicitly citing Kirchhoff's laws as the starting point. The question specifically asks you to start from these laws. Another error is writing different subscripts for current (I₁, I₂) — in series, the current is the same throughout.

Q17 QWC §67a, §67b 6 marks · ~8 min
A circuit contains a battery of e.m.f. ε connected to three resistors: resistor P (4.0 Ω) in series with a parallel combination of resistor Q (6.0 Ω) and resistor R (12.0 Ω). A student measures the current leaving the battery as 1.50 A. Using Kirchhoff's laws, explain how the current distributes in this circuit and how the potential difference is shared between the components. Refer to each resistor by its label (P, Q, R) throughout your answer. (QWC: split rubric — 4 IC marks + 2 structure marks.)
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: K1 at junction: Iᵩ = 1.00 A, Iᵣ = 0.50 A (verified: 1.00 + 0.50 = 1.50 A). K2 around loop: Vₚ = Vᵩ = Vᵣ = 6.0 V; ε = 12.0 V.

Indicative Content — 4 marks:

MarkPoint required
IC1Kirchhoff's first law applied: current of 1.50 A enters the junction and splits. Using the current divider rule: current through Q = 1.50 × 12.0/(6.0 + 12.0) = 1.00 A; current through R = 1.50 × 6.0/(6.0 + 12.0) = 0.50 A. Verified: 1.00 + 0.50 = 1.50 A ✓ (must use labels Q and R)
IC2Physical principle of K1: conservation of charge — charge cannot accumulate at a junction
IC3Kirchhoff's second law: Vₚ = 1.50 × 4.0 = 6.0 V; Vᵩ = 1.00 × 6.0 = 6.0 V; Vᵣ = 0.50 × 12.0 = 6.0 V. Sum of p.d.s: 6.0 + 6.0 = 12.0 V = ε (must use labels P, Q, R)
IC4Physical principle of K2: conservation of energy — p.d. is the same across parallel components Q and R (both 6.0 V)

Structure, Precision and Linkage — 2 marks:

MarkCriterion
SP1Each resistor is referred to by its specific label (P, Q, R) throughout the answer — generic statements without labels lose IC marks
SP2Clear logical structure: K1 stated, applied to the specific junction, values calculated and verified; then K2 stated, applied to the specific loop, values calculated and verified; both laws explicitly linked to their physical conservation principles
NOT Accepted

"Current is conserved at junctions" without naming which junction or which resistors; "Energy is conserved" without referencing p.d. and e.m.f. specifically; "The voltages add up" without using the specific resistor labels and calculated values; any answer that does not consistently use the labels P, Q, R.

⚠️ Common Error

The MS for Jun 2022 Q15(a) was very strict: generic Kirchhoff's law statements without application to the specific resistors named in the question lost marks. Students must use the labels P, Q, R in every calculation and explanation. Writing continuous prose with logical connectives (therefore, since, because) is required for QWC marks.

Q18 QWC §71 6 marks · ~8 min
The resistance of a tungsten filament lamp increases significantly when it is operating compared to when it is cold. A student uses the cold resistance value in the equation R = ρl/A to calculate the resistivity of tungsten at its operating temperature. The student's calculated value is much lower than the accepted value at the operating temperature. Explain, using a causal chain of reasoning, why the student's calculated resistivity is incorrect. (QWC: split rubric — 4 IC marks + 2 structure marks.)
Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: Operating temperature raises tungsten resistivity (lattice-ion vibration mechanism), so R_operating ≫ R_cold. Using R_cold in ρ = RA/l gives a ρ value much lower than the true ρ at the operating temperature.

Indicative Content — 4 marks:

MarkPoint required
IC1When the filament lamp operates, the temperature of the tungsten filament increases significantly because electrical energy is converted to thermal energy in the filament
IC2Because the temperature increases, the resistivity of tungsten increases (since resistivity of metals increases with temperature due to greater lattice ion vibration)
IC3This means the resistance at operating temperature is much greater than the cold resistance. The student uses the cold resistance in ρ = RA/l, and since R_cold ≪ R_operating, the calculated ρ will be much lower than the true ρ at the operating temperature
IC4To obtain the correct resistivity at the operating temperature, the student must use the resistance at that temperature (e.g. read from the I-V characteristic at the operating current), not the cold resistance

Structure — 2 marks:

MarkCriterion
S1Coherent, sustained causal chain using connective language throughout ("because," "therefore," "which means," "so," "as a result")
S2Each step logically follows from the previous one; no isolated or disconnected statements; the chain runs continuously from cause to final conclusion
NOT Accepted

Simple list of facts without causal connections; "The resistance changes" without explaining why (missing the temperature-resistivity mechanism for metals); bullet points (loses structure marks); stating the answer without tracing through the causal chain.

⚠️ Common Error

Writing bullet-pointed facts without connective language loses both structure marks. The key physics point that many students miss is that resistivity of metals increases with temperature (lattice vibration mechanism), and this must be stated explicitly. Using the cold resistance gives a value that is wrong because ρ at the operating temperature is genuinely higher, not because of a measurement error.

Q19 GRD §71 8 marks · ~12 min
A student investigates the resistivity of a uniform constantan wire. She measures the resistance R of wires of the same length (1.00 m) but different diameters d. Her results are:
d / mmR / Ω
0.402.20
0.501.40
0.600.97
0.800.55
1.000.35

(a) Explain why a graph of R against 1/d² should be plotted to determine the resistivity, rather than R against d. State the relationship you would expect. (3 marks)
(b) Calculate the additional column values of 1/d² (in m⁻²). Plot R (y-axis) against 1/d² (x-axis). Determine the gradient of your graph and use it to calculate the resistivity of constantan. (5 marks)

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer: (a) R = (4ρl/π)(1/d²) → straight line through origin; (b) ρ ≈ 2.8 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m.

Part (a) — 3 marks:

Since A = πd²/4, we have R = ρl/A = 4ρl/(πd²). This is of the form R = (4ρl/π) × (1/d²), which is a straight line through the origin with gradient = 4ρl/π. A graph of R against d would be a curve (R ∝ 1/d²), making it impossible to determine a gradient. Plotting R against 1/d² linearises the relationship, allowing the gradient to be found and ρ calculated.

Part (b) — 5 marks:

Converting d from mm to m and calculating 1/d²:

d / mmd / m1/d² / 10⁶ m⁻²R / Ω
0.404.0 × 10⁻⁴6.252.20
0.505.0 × 10⁻⁴4.001.40
0.606.0 × 10⁻⁴2.780.97
0.808.0 × 10⁻⁴1.560.55
1.001.0 × 10⁻³1.000.35

Plotting R (y-axis) against 1/d² (x-axis) gives a straight line through the origin.

Gradient = ΔR/Δ(1/d²) using a large triangle on the line of best fit:

gradient = (2.20 − 0.35) / [(6.25 − 1.00) × 10⁶] = 1.85 / (5.25 × 10⁶) = 3.52 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m²

Since gradient = 4ρl/π:

ρ = gradient × π/(4l) = 3.52 × 10⁻⁷ × π/(4 × 1.00) = 2.77 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m ≈ 2.8 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m

Marking Points
Part (a)
  • M1 Derivation or statement of relationship: R = (4ρl/π)(1/d²), i.e. R ∝ 1/d²
  • E1 Explanation that R against d gives a curve while R against 1/d² gives a straight line through the origin
  • E2 Gradient of linearised graph = 4ρl/π, allowing ρ to be determined directly
Part (b)
  • C1 Correct conversion of d from mm to m before calculating 1/d² (critical step)
  • C2 Correct calculation of 1/d² values (at least 2 checked values correct)
  • P1 Suitable axes: R on y-axis, 1/d² on x-axis; points plotted correctly; line of best fit through origin
  • M2 Gradient calculated correctly from the line of best fit
  • A1 ρ = gradient × π/(4l) = 2.8 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m (accept 2.7–2.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m)
NOT Accepted

Plotting R against d (non-linear — defeats the purpose of linearisation); using d in mm without converting to metres for 1/d² (gives answer off by factor of 10⁶); using A = πd² (forgetting the /4) — A = πd²/4, so gradient = 4ρl/π, NOT ρl/π; forgetting to convert mm to m.

⚠️ Common Error #1

Calculating A = πd² (without /4) and then getting the wrong gradient expression. The cross-sectional area is A = π(d/2)² = πd²/4. Forgetting the factor of 4 gives ρ that is too large by a factor of 4.

⚠️ Common Error #2

Using d in mm directly when calculating 1/d². Since 1/d² has units of m⁻², the diameter must be in metres. Using mm gives 1/d² values that are 10⁶ times too large, making the gradient 10⁶ times too small and ρ 10⁶ times too small. Always convert mm → m first.
