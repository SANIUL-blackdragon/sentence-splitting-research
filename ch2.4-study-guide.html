1. Electric Current and Potential Difference (Spec 64–65)

Electric Current (Spec 64)

TermExam-ready definitionSpec pointExam data
Electric current (I)The rate of flow of electric charge. Current is defined as the charge passing a point per unit time: I = ΔQ/Δt.6415 marks, 4 sessions
Charge (Q)A fundamental property of matter; in circuits, the quantity of electricity transported. Measured in coulombs (C). One coulomb is the charge transported by a current of one ampere in one second: Q = It.64—
Coulomb (C)The SI unit of electric charge. Defined as the charge that passes a point in a circuit when a current of 1 ampere flows for 1 second (1 C = 1 A s).64—
Ampere (A)The SI unit of electric current. One ampere is one coulomb of charge passing a point per second (1 A = 1 C s⁻¹).64—
Rate of flow of chargeThe amount of charge passing a given point in a conductor per unit time; this is the definition of electric current.64—
Conventional currentThe flow of charge from positive to negative terminal of a cell; the direction taken as the direction of current in a circuit, opposite to the direction of electron flow.64—
Electron flowThe actual movement of electrons in a metal conductor, from negative to positive terminal, opposite in direction to conventional current.64—
ΔQ/ΔtThe defining equation for electric current: the change in charge (ΔQ) divided by the change in time (Δt). Units: amperes (A).64—
Charge conservation at a pointThe principle that charge cannot accumulate at any point in a circuit — the current entering any point must equal the current leaving it.64—
I (symbol)Symbol for electric current. In the equation I = ΔQ/Δt, I is measured in amperes (A).64—
Q (symbol)Symbol for electric charge. In I = ΔQ/Δt, Q is measured in coulombs (C).64—
t (symbol)Symbol for time. In I = ΔQ/Δt, t is measured in seconds (s).64—

Potential Difference (Spec 65)

TermExam-ready definitionSpec pointExam data
Potential difference (p.d., V)The energy transferred (work done) per unit charge between two points in a circuit: V = W/Q. It is the energy lost per coulomb of charge passing through a component.6514 marks, 5 sessions
VoltageA common name for potential difference; measured in volts (V).65—
Volt (V)The SI unit of potential difference. One volt is one joule of energy transferred per coulomb of charge (1 V = 1 J C⁻¹).65—
Energy transferred (W)The work done by the charge as it moves through a component; measured in joules (J). In V = W/Q, W is the energy transferred by charge Q moving through a p.d. V.65—
Work done per unit chargeAn equivalent definition of potential difference: the work done (energy transferred) when one coulomb of charge moves between two points.65—
V = W/QThe defining equation for potential difference: p.d. = energy transferred ÷ charge. Units: V (volts) = J (joules) ÷ C (coulombs).65—
p.d. across a componentThe energy transferred per unit charge as charge passes through that specific component; measured using a voltmeter connected in parallel across the component.65—
V (symbol)Symbol for potential difference (voltage). In V = W/Q, V is measured in volts (V).65—
W (symbol)Symbol for energy transferred or work done. In V = W/Q, W is measured in joules (J).65—

2. Resistance and Ohm's Law (Spec 66)

TermExam-ready definitionSpec pointExam data
Resistance (R)A measure of the opposition to current flow in a conductor, defined as the ratio of potential difference across a component to the current through it: R = V/I.6620 marks, 8 sessions
Ohm (Ω)The SI unit of resistance. One ohm is the resistance of a component when a p.d. of 1 volt drives a current of 1 ampere through it (1 Ω = 1 V A⁻¹).66—
R = V/IThe defining equation for resistance. Resistance is the gradient of a V-I graph (or the reciprocal of the gradient of an I-V graph).66—
Ohm's lawThe principle that, for an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, the current through the conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it.66—
Ohmic conductorA conductor for which the current is directly proportional to the potential difference across it at constant temperature, i.e. it obeys Ohm's law and has a constant resistance. Its I-V graph is a straight line through the origin.66—
Constant resistanceThe property of an ohmic conductor: the ratio V/I remains constant for all values of current and p.d. (at constant temperature).66—
Directly proportional (I ∝ V)The relationship described by Ohm's law: doubling the p.d. across an ohmic conductor doubles the current through it.66—
Constant temperature conditionThe necessary condition for Ohm's law to hold; if temperature changes, resistance may change and the I-V relationship is no longer linear.66—
Non-ohmic conductorA conductor or component for which current is NOT directly proportional to p.d., meaning its resistance changes with current or p.d. Examples: filament lamp, thermistor, diode.66—
Ω (symbol)The Greek letter omega, symbol for the ohm, the SI unit of resistance.66—

3. Kirchhoff's Laws / Current and P.D. Distribution (Spec 67a, 67b)

Current Distribution — Kirchhoff's First Law (Spec 67a)

TermExam-ready definitionSpec pointExam data
Kirchhoff's first lawAt any junction in an electrical circuit, the sum of the currents entering the junction equals the sum of the currents leaving the junction. This is a consequence of the conservation of charge.67a9 marks, 3 sessions
Conservation of chargeThe principle that electric charge cannot be created or destroyed; at any point in a circuit, the total charge flowing in equals the total charge flowing out. This is the physical basis for Kirchhoff's first law.67a—
Junction (in a circuit)A point in a circuit where two or more conductors meet and current can divide or combine. At a junction, Kirchhoff's first law applies: ΣI_in = ΣI_out.67a—
Current distributionThe way current divides among parallel branches of a circuit, governed by Kirchhoff's first law. The current in each branch depends on the resistance of that branch (lower resistance → higher current).67a—
ΣI_in = ΣI_outThe mathematical statement of Kirchhoff's first law: the algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero.67a—
NodeAn alternative term for a junction in a circuit; a point where circuit elements connect.67a—
Superposition of currents at a junctionThe principle that the total current at a junction is the algebraic sum of all individual currents entering and leaving; no current is lost or gained at a junction.67a—

P.D. Distribution — Kirchhoff's Second Law (Spec 67b)

TermExam-ready definitionSpec pointExam data
Kirchhoff's second lawIn any closed loop of an electrical circuit, the sum of the potential differences around the loop is equal to the sum of the electromotive forces in that loop. This is a consequence of the conservation of energy.67b7 marks, 2 sessions
Conservation of energy (in circuits)The principle that the total energy supplied by sources of e.m.f. in a closed loop equals the total energy transferred to components in that loop. This is the physical basis for Kirchhoff's second law.67b—
Closed loopA complete conducting path around a circuit that starts and ends at the same point, passing through one or more components and/or sources of e.m.f.67b—
P.D. distributionThe way potential differences are shared among components in a series circuit, governed by Kirchhoff's second law. The sum of p.d.s across all components in series equals the total e.m.f. of the source.67b—
ΣV = ΣεThe mathematical statement of Kirchhoff's second law: the sum of p.d.s around any closed loop equals the sum of e.m.f.s in that loop.67b—
Energy per unit chargeA way of understanding Kirchhoff's second law: the e.m.f. supplies a certain amount of energy per coulomb, and the p.d.s across components in the loop account for all of that energy per coulomb being transferred.67b—

4. Series and Parallel Circuits (Spec 68)

Series Circuits (Spec 68)

TermExam-ready definitionSpec pointExam data
Series circuitA circuit in which components are connected one after another along a single path, so the same current flows through each component.6823 marks, 7 sessions
Current in seriesThe current is the same through all components connected in series (a consequence of Kirchhoff's first law — there are no junctions).68—
P.D. in seriesThe total p.d. across components in series equals the sum of the individual p.d.s: V_total = V₁ + V₂ + ... (a consequence of Kirchhoff's second law).68—
Combined resistance in seriesThe total resistance of resistors in series is the sum of the individual resistances: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + ...68—
R_series = R₁ + R₂ + ...The formula for combining resistances in series. Adding more resistors in series always increases the total resistance.68—

Parallel Circuits (Spec 68)

TermExam-ready definitionSpec pointExam data
Parallel circuitA circuit in which two or more components are connected across the same two points (junctions), so the p.d. across each branch is the same.68—
P.D. in parallelThe potential difference is the same across each branch of a parallel circuit (all branches share the same two junctions).68—
Current in parallelThe total current entering a parallel arrangement equals the sum of the currents in each branch: I_total = I₁ + I₂ + ... (a consequence of Kirchhoff's first law).68—
Combined resistance in parallelThe reciprocal of the total resistance equals the sum of the reciprocals of the individual resistances: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...68—
1/R_parallel = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...The formula for combining resistances in parallel. Adding more resistors in parallel always decreases the total resistance.68—
Two resistors in parallelA special case: R_total = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂) when exactly two resistors are in parallel.68—
Equivalent resistanceThe single resistance that can replace a combination of resistors (series, parallel, or mixed) without changing the current or p.d. in the rest of the circuit.68—
Mixed circuitA circuit containing both series and parallel combinations of components, requiring step-by-step simplification to find the total resistance.68—
BranchOne of the parallel paths in a parallel circuit, carrying a share of the total current determined by the resistance of that branch.68—
Current divider ruleThe principle that in a parallel circuit, the current through each branch is inversely proportional to its resistance: branches with lower resistance carry greater current.68—

5. Electrical Power and Energy (Spec 69)

TermExam-ready definitionSpec pointExam data
Electrical power (P)The rate at which energy is transferred by an electrical device or circuit. Defined as P = VI, where V is the potential difference across the device and I is the current through it.6924 marks, 8 sessions
Watt (W)The SI unit of power. One watt is one joule of energy transferred per second (1 W = 1 J s⁻¹ = 1 V A).69—
P = VIThe power equation: power = potential difference × current. This is the fundamental form, from which other forms can be derived using V = IR.69—
P = I²RAn alternative form of the power equation, derived by substituting V = IR into P = VI: P = I(IR) = I²R. Useful when current and resistance are known.69—
P = V²/RAn alternative form of the power equation, derived by substituting I = V/R into P = VI: P = V(V/R) = V²/R. Useful when p.d. and resistance are known.69—
Energy transferred (W)The total electrical energy transferred in a circuit or by a component, given by W = VIt = Pt, where V is p.d., I is current, and t is time. Measured in joules (J).69—
W = VItThe energy equation: energy transferred = potential difference × current × time. This gives the total energy in joules.69—
W = PtEnergy = power × time. Gives the energy transferred when power P is delivered for time t.69—
Power dissipatedThe rate at which electrical energy is converted to other forms (e.g. thermal energy) in a component; given by P = I²R for a resistor.69—
Power supplied by a cellThe total power output of a cell: P = Iε, where ε is the e.m.f. and I is the current. Not all of this power is delivered to the external circuit — some is dissipated in the internal resistance.69—
Power delivered to external circuitThe useful power output delivered to the external components: P_ext = VI = I²R_ext, where V is the terminal p.d.69—
Power wasted in internal resistanceThe power dissipated as thermal energy inside the cell itself: P_int = I²r, where r is the internal resistance.69—
Kilowatt-hour (kW h)A unit of electrical energy equal to the energy transferred by a power of 1 kW operating for 1 hour (1 kW h = 3.6 × 10⁶ J). Used for billing electrical energy.69—

6. I–V Characteristics (Spec 70)

TermExam-ready definitionSpec pointExam data
I–V characteristic (I–V graph)A graph showing how the current (I) through a component varies with the potential difference (V) across it. The shape of the graph reveals whether the component is ohmic or non-ohmic and indicates how its resistance changes.7012 marks, 5 sessions
I–V graph of an ohmic conductorA straight line through the origin. The gradient is constant, indicating constant resistance. Current is directly proportional to p.d.70—
I–V graph of a filament lampA curve that starts as a straight line through the origin but becomes less steep at higher currents. The decreasing gradient indicates increasing resistance as the filament heats up.70—
I–V graph of a thermistor (NTC)A curve that becomes steeper at higher temperatures (higher p.d.s cause heating). The increasing gradient indicates decreasing resistance as temperature increases.70—
I–V graph of a diodeA graph that shows approximately zero current for negative p.d. and low positive p.d., then a sharp increase in current above a threshold voltage (≈0.6 V for silicon). Current flows easily in only one direction.70—
Filament lampA lamp containing a thin metal wire (filament) that glows when current passes through it. As current increases, the filament temperature rises, increasing resistance due to increased lattice ion vibrations. It is a non-ohmic component.70—
FilamentThe thin wire inside a filament lamp, usually made of tungsten, which heats up and emits light when current flows through it.70—
DiodeA semiconductor component that allows current to flow easily in one direction (forward bias) but has very high resistance in the opposite direction (reverse bias).70—
Forward biasThe direction in which a diode allows current to flow easily (the p.d. is applied in the direction of easy current flow).70—
Reverse biasThe direction in which a diode has very high resistance and blocks current flow.70—

WPH12 Extension — Circuits QWC Templates (Definitions)

Scope: WPH12 Topic 2 (Electric circuits) and the Quality of Written Communication (QWC) extended-response questions that test it. The WPH11 audit identified that only 1 QWC template existed for circuits, and the spec requires at least one 6-mark QWC question per paper. This extension fills the gap by providing examiner-aligned definitions for every term needed to score L3 on circuits QWC answers. Source: Pearson Edexcel IAL Physics Specification Topic 2 (Electric circuits), Appendices 6 (Mathematical skills) and 9 (Practical skills), examiner reports Jan 2019–Jan 2025 (cross-referenced for common QWC errors). Purpose: Exam-ready definitions for every QWC and circuit term, grounded in the spec and examiner report phrasing. Each term is paired with the QWC Exam context that explains how it earns marks in a 6-mark extended-response answer.

1. QWC and Extended-Response Terms

1.1 Quality of Written Communication (QWC)

Spec ref: General A-level requirement; embedded in WPH12 mark schemes as "QWC" annotation Definition: A marking criterion that rewards clear, well-organised, spelt-and-grammatically-correct written English in extended-response answers. In WPH12 it is applied to 6-mark questions that require a sustained chain of physical reasoning. QWC is not a separate mark — it is judged holistically alongside the scientific content when deciding the level (L1/L2/L3) the answer is placed in. QWC Exam context: A student may write all 6 indicative content (IC) points but only score L2 (4 marks) because the answer is disorganised, lacks connective words, or has no clear cause-and-effect chain. Conversely, a student who writes only 5 IC points but links them with "therefore", "consequently", "because" can score L3 (5–6 marks). Always use connectives — they are the visible signal of QWC.

1.2 Extended-Response Question

Spec ref: Spec introduction — "At least one 6-mark QWC question per paper" Definition: A question worth 6 marks that requires a sustained written answer (typically 8–15 lines), structured as a logical argument. It cannot be answered with bullet points or one-word responses. Examiners mark it using level descriptors (L1/L2/L3) rather than point-by-point. QWC Exam context: Always the LAST sub-question of a multi-part circuit question (typically 6 marks in WPH12 Q12–Q16). Examiners expect a continuous paragraph, not a list. Writing "• Lattice vibrates • Collisions increase • Drift velocity drops" scores L1 (2 marks) even if the science is correct — the lack of linkage breaks QWC.

1.3 Level-Marked Question

Spec ref: Pearson Edexcel mark scheme conventions Definition: A question marked using level descriptors rather than a point-marking list. The marker reads the whole answer, judges the overall quality against L1/L2/L3 descriptors, and assigns a single mark within that level. Level-marked questions always include QWC in the judgement. QWC Exam context: The marker first decides the level (L1, L2, or L3) using both scientific content and QWC, then chooses a mark at the bottom, middle, or top of that level. A "top of L2" answer scores 4; a "bottom of L3" answer scores 5. The difference of one mark often comes down to whether connectives are used.

1.4 Level 1 (L1) — Basic

Spec ref: Mark scheme level descriptors Definition: 1–2 marks. The answer is fragmentary, with isolated correct points but no linkage. Scientific terms may be used incorrectly or vaguely. QWC is weak — the answer may be a list, may have spelling errors in technical terms, and shows no sustained argument. QWC Exam context: A typical L1 answer to "Explain why the resistance of a metal increases with temperature" reads: "Atoms vibrate more, electrons collide more, resistance goes up." This scores 2 marks: it has the right ideas but no causal connectives, no mention of drift velocity or mean free time, and no chain of reasoning. To escape L1, write at least one "because" or "therefore".

1.5 Level 2 (L2) — Clear

Spec ref: Mark scheme level descriptors Definition: 3–4 marks. The answer has a clear line of reasoning with some correct physics, but the explanation is incomplete or has minor errors. Most key terms are used correctly. QWC is adequate — the answer is generally readable and uses some connectives, but the argument may jump or miss a step. QWC Exam context: A typical L2 answer: "When the metal is heated, the lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude. This means electrons collide more frequently with the ions, so the drift velocity decreases and the current falls. From R = V/I, if I decreases then R increases." This scores 4 marks — it has the chain but skips "mean free time" and "mean free path" terminology that would lift it to L3.

1.6 Level 3 (L3) — Detailed and Accurate

Spec ref: Mark scheme level descriptors Definition: 5–6 marks. The answer has a sustained, logical, and complete chain of reasoning. All key terms are used correctly and in the right order. QWC is high — connectives link every step, technical spelling is accurate, and the answer reads as a single coherent argument. QWC Exam context: A typical L3 answer includes every IC point, in the right causal order, with explicit connectives ("because", "consequently", "therefore", "as a result"). It uses the full technical vocabulary (lattice ions, mean free time, drift velocity) and ends by linking the microscopic change back to the macroscopic observation (R = V/I increases). L3 is achievable by any student who memorises the model answers in ext-circuits-qwc-concepts.md.

2. Command Words for Extended Response

2.1 Discuss

Spec ref: Spec Appendix 5 (command words) Definition: Give an account of something, including different viewpoints or factors, and reach a conclusion. In circuits, "discuss" often asks the student to weigh competing effects (e.g. increased lattice vibration vs increased charge carrier release in a semiconductor). QWC Exam context: "Discuss how the resistance of an NTC thermistor changes with temperature" expects the student to acknowledge TWO competing effects (more charge carriers released → R decreases; more lattice vibration → R increases) and explain why the charge carrier effect dominates in a semiconductor. Missing one effect caps the answer at L2.

2.2 Evaluate

Spec ref: Spec Appendix 5 Definition: Consider different factors, weigh them up, and reach a judgement supported by evidence. In circuits, evaluate is often applied to experimental data or design (e.g. "evaluate whether the student's method gives a reliable value for internal resistance"). QWC Exam context: Requires the student to identify BOTH strengths and limitations and conclude which dominates. A purely critical answer or a purely positive answer caps at L2. The connective "however" is essential in evaluate answers — it signals the weighing-up.

2.3 Explain

Spec ref: Spec Appendix 5 Definition: Give reasons or causes; link cause to effect. The most common command word in circuits QWC. Requires a causal chain, not just a description. QWC Exam context: "Explain why the terminal p.d. is less than the EMF when current flows" expects the chain: cell has internal resistance → some energy dissipated inside cell → "lost volts" = Ir → terminal p.d. = ε − Ir → terminal p.d. < ε. Every step needs a connective ("because", "so", "therefore").

2.4 Describe

Spec ref: Spec Appendix 5 Definition: Give an account in words of what happens, without necessarily giving reasons. In QWC circuits questions, "describe" is rare — examiners prefer "explain" because it forces causal chains. When "describe" appears, students must still state the trend and link to a measurable change. QWC Exam context: "Describe how the resistance of an LDR varies with light intensity" expects: as light intensity increases, more charge carriers are released, number density increases, so R decreases. Even "describe" rewards causal chains in WPH12.

2.5 Compare

Spec ref: Spec Appendix 5 Definition: Identify similarities and differences between two or more things. In circuits, used for series vs parallel, metal vs thermistor, EMF vs terminal p.d. QWC Exam context: A compare answer must address BOTH items symmetrically. "In series, current is the same everywhere; in parallel, voltage is the same across each branch." Using "whereas", "in contrast", "on the other hand" signals comparison and earns the QWC mark.

2.6 Justify

Spec ref: Spec Appendix 5 Definition: Support a conclusion with evidence or reasoning. Often used in numerical questions ("show that", then "justify whether the result is reasonable"). QWC Exam context: "Justify whether the student's conclusion that r = 0.5 Ω is supported by the data" expects: a calculation, comparison to uncertainty, and a judgement. The connective "therefore" is essential: "...the uncertainty is ±0.2 Ω, therefore the value 0.5 Ω is consistent with the true value of 0.4 Ω."

3. Connective Words (QWC Signalling Language)

3.1 Therefore

Definition: Introduces a logical conclusion from the previous statement. The most important connective in physics QWC — it signals "the next sentence is the consequence of the previous one." QWC Exam context: "Lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude, therefore electrons collide more frequently." Examiners reward "therefore" because it shows the student is constructing a causal chain, not listing facts.

3.2 Consequently

Definition: As a result; introduces an effect that follows from a cause. Often used at the second or third step of a chain. QWC Exam context: "Mean free time decreases; consequently, drift velocity falls." "Consequently" signals a derived effect, which examiners recognise as L3 thinking.

3.3 As a result

Definition: Synonym for "therefore" / "consequently". Less formal but acceptable. Often used to introduce the final macroscopic observation. QWC Exam context: "Fewer electrons pass a point per second; as a result, the current I decreases."

3.4 Because

Definition: Introduces the cause of a previously stated effect. The reverse direction of "therefore" — "X because Y" is equivalent to "Y therefore X". QWC Exam context: "The terminal p.d. is less than the EMF because some energy is dissipated in the internal resistance." "Because" is the workhorse connective — it appears in nearly every L3 answer.

3.5 Due to

Definition: Synonym for "because of"; introduces a cause as a noun phrase rather than a clause. QWC Exam context: "The resistance increases due to more frequent collisions between electrons and lattice ions." Note: "due to" must be followed by a noun phrase, not a full clause ("due to more frequent collisions" ✓; "due to electrons collide more" ✗).

3.6 However

Definition: Introduces a contrast or opposing point. Essential in "discuss" and "evaluate" answers where two competing effects must be weighed. QWC Exam context: In a thermistor QWC: "Heating also causes more lattice vibration, however, in a semiconductor the dominant effect is the release of more charge carriers." Without "however", the answer fails to show the weighing-up that L3 requires.

3.7 In contrast

Definition: Synonym for "however" used specifically when comparing two distinct cases. QWC Exam context: "In a metal, resistance increases with temperature; in contrast, in an NTC thermistor resistance decreases with temperature." Signals explicit comparison — rewarded in "compare" QWC questions.

3.8 Whereas

Definition: Similar to "in contrast"; introduces a subordinate clause that contrasts with the main clause. QWC Exam context: "In series the same current flows through each component, whereas in parallel the current splits between branches."

4. Circuit Terms for QWC — Metal Resistance & Temperature

4.1 Lattice (Ion)

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 (current electricity), atomic model Definition: The regular three-dimensional array of positive ions in a metal that occupies fixed (mean) positions in the crystal structure. The ions are not truly stationary — they vibrate about their equilibrium positions with an amplitude determined by the temperature. QWC Exam context: In a metal-R-T QWC answer, the first IC point is always "lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude at higher T". Examiners reward "lattice ions" specifically — saying "atoms vibrate" scores lower because the metal's positive cores are ions, not neutral atoms.

4.2 Lattice Vibration

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 + Topic 4 (thermal physics cross-link) Definition: The oscillation of lattice ions about their equilibrium positions due to thermal energy. The amplitude of vibration increases with temperature. Lattice vibrations are quantised as phonons in solid-state physics. QWC Exam context: "Lattice vibrations increase with temperature" is the standard L3 phrasing. Avoid "the lattice shakes more" — examiners do not reward informal language. The phrase "increased amplitude of vibration" is the mark-scheme keyword.

4.3 Phonon

Spec ref: Beyond-spec extension, but appears in examiner reports when students mention it Definition: A quantum of lattice vibration energy. Phonons are the particle-like excitations that carry thermal energy through a crystal lattice. Electrons scatter off phonons in a metal, causing resistance. QWC Exam context: Mentioning "phonons" can lift an answer from L2 to L3 if used correctly ("electrons scatter off phonons, reducing mean free time"). However, using "phonon" without explaining its role scores no extra marks. Mention it only if the rest of the chain is already complete.

4.4 Thermal Agitation

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 (descriptive term for lattice motion) Definition: Random thermal motion of lattice ions, equivalent to "lattice vibration". A more formal term that examiners reward in L3 answers. QWC Exam context: "Increased thermal agitation of the lattice ions" is a mark-scheme phrase. Use it interchangeably with "lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude" to vary vocabulary — both score.

4.5 Mean Free Time

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 (Drift velocity equation context) Definition: The average time between successive collisions of a conduction electron with lattice ions. It decreases when lattice vibrations increase (because collisions become more frequent). Drift velocity v = (qEτ)/mₑ, where τ is the mean free time — so v decreases if τ decreases. QWC Exam context: Mentioning "mean free time decreases" is a key L3 discriminator — most L2 answers skip this and go straight to "drift velocity decreases". Stating "mean free time decreases because collisions become more frequent" earns the L3 step.

4.6 Mean Free Path

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 (related to mean free time) Definition: The average distance a conduction electron travels between successive collisions with lattice ions. Mean free path = mean free time × drift velocity. It decreases when lattice vibrations increase. QWC Exam context: Mentioning mean free path is an alternative to mean free time — both score the same IC point. Use whichever you remember; do not state both unless asked.

4.7 Drift Velocity

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 — I = nAvq Definition: The average velocity of charge carriers along a conductor in the direction of the electric field. It is much smaller than the random thermal velocity of the carriers (typically ~10⁻⁴ m/s vs ~10⁵ m/s for thermal motion) because the field only slightly biases the random motion. Drift velocity v = I/(nAq). QWC Exam context: Every metal-R-T QWC answer must end with "drift velocity decreases → current decreases → R = V/I increases". This is the L3 chain. Students who stop at "drift velocity decreases" miss the final macroscopic link.

5. Circuit Terms for QWC — Semiconductor & Thermistor

5.1 Semiconductor

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 (classification of materials) Definition: A material with electrical conductivity between that of a conductor (metal) and an insulator. Its resistivity decreases with temperature because thermal energy releases more charge carriers from the lattice. Examples: silicon, germanium. NTC thermistors are made from semiconductor materials. QWC Exam context: In a thermistor QWC, the opening sentence must identify the material as a semiconductor: "An NTC thermistor is made from a semiconductor." This is IC point #1 — without it, the answer cannot reach L3.

5.2 Charge Carrier

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 Definition: A particle that carries electric charge and is free to move through a material. In metals, charge carriers are delocalised electrons. In semiconductors, charge carriers are electrons in the conduction band AND holes (positive vacancies) in the valence band. In electrolytes, charge carriers are ions. QWC Exam context: In a thermistor QWC: "Thermal energy releases more charge carriers" is the key IC point. Saying "more electrons" is acceptable but "more charge carriers" is the mark-scheme phrase — use it.

5.3 Number Density (n)

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 — I = nAvq Definition: The number of charge carriers per unit volume (m⁻³). In a metal, n is essentially constant with temperature (~10²⁸ m⁻³ for copper). In a semiconductor, n increases rapidly with temperature because thermal energy promotes electrons from the valence band to the conduction band. QWC Exam context: "Number density n increases with temperature" is the central IC point of a thermistor QWC. Students must distinguish this from a metal, where n is constant. The contrast (n constant in metals; n increases in semiconductors) is what makes the two QWC topics distinguishable.

5.4 NTC Thermistor

Spec ref: Spec 68 (resistivity) + Spec 64-65 Definition: A thermistor with a Negative Temperature Coefficient of resistance — its resistance decreases as temperature increases. Made from a semiconductor (typically a metal oxide such as Mn₂O₃ or NiO). The negative coefficient arises because the increase in number density of charge carriers with temperature outweighs the increase in lattice vibration. QWC Exam context: Always introduce the topic with "NTC = negative temperature coefficient" to score the definition IC point. Then explain the mechanism: more charge carriers released → n increases → I = nAvq → I increases → R = V/I decreases.

5.5 Band Theory (Beyond-spec, contextual)

Spec ref: Beyond IAL spec but appears in examiner reports for L3 answers Definition: The model in which electrons in a solid occupy energy bands (valence band, conduction band) separated by a forbidden gap. In a semiconductor at absolute zero, the valence band is full and the conduction band is empty, so no conduction occurs. At higher temperatures, thermal energy promotes electrons across the gap, leaving holes in the valence band. QWC Exam context: Mentioning "electrons promoted from valence band to conduction band" can lift an answer to L3 — but it must be paired with the simpler explanation ("more charge carriers released"). Use band theory as a bonus, not as a replacement for the simpler mechanism.

6. Circuit Terms for QWC — Drift Velocity & Current

6.1 Drift Velocity Equation (I = nAvq)

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 — I = nAvq Definition: The equation relating current I to the number density n, cross-sectional area A, drift velocity v, and charge per carrier q. For electrons, q = e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Rearranged: v = I/(nAq). QWC Exam context: In a drift-velocity QWC, the answer must quote I = nAvq explicitly and explain what each symbol represents. This is the structural backbone of the answer — without the equation, the explanation has no anchor.

6.2 Conduction Electron

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 Definition: An electron in a metal that is delocalised (not bound to a particular atom) and free to move through the lattice. Approximately one per atom in metals. Conduction electrons form the "electron sea" that gives metals their conductivity. QWC Exam context: "Conduction electrons" is the preferred term over just "electrons" — it shows the student understands they are the charge carriers in a metal.

6.3 Random Thermal Motion

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 (qualitative) Definition: The random, high-speed (~10⁵ m/s) motion of conduction electrons in a metal due to thermal energy. Without an electric field, the random motion produces no net drift. When an electric field is applied, it superimposes a small drift (~10⁻⁴ m/s) on the random motion. QWC Exam context: In a "why is drift velocity so slow" QWC, mentioning random thermal motion is essential: "Electrons move randomly at high speed but only drift slowly along the field because they constantly collide with lattice ions." Without this, the answer is incomplete.

6.4 Collision (with Lattice Ions)

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 Definition: The interaction between a conduction electron and a lattice ion that scatters the electron, randomising its velocity. Collisions are the mechanism of resistance — they interrupt the drift, so the electron's net forward motion is much slower than its speed between collisions. QWC Exam context: "Electrons collide frequently with lattice ions" is the key IC point in a drift-velocity QWC. The phrase "frequent collisions" is the mark-scheme keyword.

7. Circuit Terms for QWC — Internal Resistance & EMF

7.1 Electromotive Force (EMF, ε)

Spec ref: Spec 66-67 — ε = W/Q Definition: The total electrical energy per unit charge produced by a source (e.g. cell, battery). Unit: volts (V = J/C). EMF is the energy converted from chemical (or other) to electrical form per coulomb of charge that passes through the source. The symbol is ε (epsilon). For an ideal source with no internal resistance, terminal p.d. = EMF at all currents. QWC Exam context: In an internal-resistance QWC, the opening sentence must define EMF: "The EMF is the total energy per coulomb supplied by the cell." Then introduce internal resistance: "In a real cell, some of this energy is dissipated in the internal resistance." This is the L3 chain.

7.2 Internal Resistance (r)

Spec ref: Spec 66-67 — V = ε − Ir Definition: The resistance to current flow inside a source of EMF, caused by the resistance of the electrolyte, electrodes, and internal connections. Internal resistance causes some of the energy per coulomb to be dissipated inside the source itself, so the terminal p.d. is less than the EMF when current flows. Symbol: r. Unit: Ω. QWC Exam context: "Real cells have internal resistance r" is the second IC point in an internal-resistance QWC. The phrase "real" or "non-ideal" is important — examiners reward the distinction between ideal and real sources.

7.3 Lost Volts

Spec ref: Spec 66-67 — V = ε − Ir Definition: The voltage "lost" across the internal resistance of a source when current flows, equal to Ir. The term is informal but appears in mark schemes. Lost volts = EMF − terminal p.d. = Ir. The greater the current, the greater the lost volts. QWC Exam context: The phrase "lost volts" appears verbatim in mark schemes — use it. "The lost volts (Ir) increase with current" is the L3 phrasing. Avoid "missing voltage" or "lost energy" — these do not score.

7.4 Terminal Potential Difference (V)

Spec ref: Spec 66-67 — V = ε − Ir Definition: The potential difference across the terminals of a source when current is flowing. It is less than the EMF by the lost volts: V = ε − Ir. When no current flows (open circuit), V = ε. Unit: V. QWC Exam context: The QWC chain must explicitly compare terminal p.d. to EMF: "terminal p.d. < EMF when current flows." The inequality is the key mark — examiners look for the explicit comparison.

7.5 Open Circuit (No Current)

Spec ref: Spec 66-67 Definition: A circuit condition in which no current flows (e.g. switch open, or voltmeter with very high resistance connected across a cell). In open circuit, Ir = 0, so V = ε. The terminal p.d. equals the EMF. QWC Exam context: "When no current flows, the lost volts are zero, so the voltmeter reads the EMF." This is the standard explanation for why a voltmeter across an isolated cell reads the EMF. Use the phrase "no current flows" — examiners reward this directly.

7.6 Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (in internal resistance context)

Spec ref: Spec 66-67 (energy conservation in loops) Definition: The sum of EMFs around any closed loop in a circuit equals the sum of potential drops (including across internal resistance). For a single loop: ε = Ir + IR_external. This is energy conservation applied to charge. QWC Exam context: Mentioning "energy is conserved" can lift an answer to L3 in internal resistance QWC: "Energy supplied per coulomb by the cell equals energy dissipated in the internal resistance plus energy dissipated in the external circuit." The phrase "energy is conserved" or "energy per coulomb" is rewarded.

8. Circuit Terms for QWC — Series and Parallel

8.1 Series Circuit

Spec ref: Spec 68 Definition: A circuit in which components are connected end-to-end so that the same current flows through each component. The total resistance is the sum of individual resistances: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ... The p.d. across the combination equals the sum of p.d.s across each component. QWC Exam context: "In series, the same current flows through every component, and the p.d. splits across them" — the two key IC points. Use "splits" or "divides" — both score.

8.2 Parallel Circuit

Spec ref: Spec 68 Definition: A circuit in which components are connected across the same two points so that the same p.d. is applied across each component. The current splits between the branches. The reciprocal of total resistance equals the sum of reciprocals of individual resistances: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ... The total resistance is LESS than the smallest individual resistance. QWC Exam context: "In parallel, the same p.d. is across each branch, and the current splits between branches." Plus the non-obvious point: "R_total is less than the smallest individual resistor." The "less than smallest" point is a common L3 discriminator.

8.3 Equivalent Resistance

Spec ref: Spec 68 Definition: The single resistance that could replace a combination of resistors without changing the current drawn from the source. Calculated using R_total = ΣR for series, or 1/R_total = Σ(1/R) for parallel. QWC Exam context: Use "equivalent resistance" rather than "total resistance" in formal answers — both score, but "equivalent" is the more precise term.

9. Circuit Terms for QWC — Potential Divider

9.1 Potential Divider

Spec ref: Spec 69-71 Definition: A circuit of two (or more) resistors in series across a source, used to provide a fraction of the source p.d. The output voltage is taken across one of the resistors. V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁ + R₂), where R₂ is the resistor across which V_out is measured. QWC Exam context: The formula V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁ + R₂) is the structural anchor of a potential-divider QWC. Always write it down and explain what each symbol is.

9.2 Light-Dependent Resistor (LDR)

Spec ref: Spec 69-71 (sensor application) Definition: A resistor whose resistance decreases when light intensity increases (made from a semiconductor such as CdS). More photons release more charge carriers, increasing n and decreasing R. In a potential divider, replacing R₁ or R₂ with an LDR gives a light-sensing circuit. QWC Exam context: "In light, the LDR's resistance decreases" — the IC point. Then link to V_out: "if the LDR is R₂, V_out decreases in bright light; if the LDR is R₁, V_out increases in bright light." The direction of V_out change depends on which resistor the LDR replaces.

9.3 Thermistor in a Potential Divider

Spec ref: Spec 69-71 Definition: Using an NTC thermistor as one resistor in a potential divider gives a temperature-sensing circuit. As T increases, R_thermistor decreases, so V_out across it decreases (if it is R₂) or increases (if it is R₁). QWC Exam context: The QWC chain: "As T increases, R_thermistor decreases (NTC), so the thermistor's share of V_in decreases, so V_out across it decreases." Plus the application: "This can switch on a heater when V_out drops below a threshold."

9.4 Potentiometer

Spec ref: Spec 69-71 (variable potential divider) Definition: A variable resistor with three terminals, used as an adjustable potential divider. A sliding contact moves along a resistive track, allowing V_out to be varied continuously from 0 to V_in. QWC Exam context: Mentioned in L3 answers as a practical application: "A potentiometer allows V_out to be varied continuously from 0 to V_in." Use this as a closing application point.

10. Cross-Cutting Terms

10.1 Ohm's Law

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 — V = IR Definition: For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, the current is proportional to the p.d.: V = IR. The constant of proportionality is the resistance. Ohm's Law applies to metals at constant temperature but NOT to thermistors, diodes, or lamps (whose temperature — and hence resistance — changes with current). QWC Exam context: Used as the final link in metal-R-T QWC: "Since R = V/I and I has decreased (at constant V), R increases." Quoting Ohm's Law explicitly is rewarded.

10.2 Resistivity (ρ)

Spec ref: Spec 68 — R = ρL/A Definition: An intrinsic property of a material, defined as the resistance of a 1 m cube of the material. Unit: Ω·m. Resistivity depends on temperature (increases with T for metals, decreases for semiconductors) but not on the dimensions of the sample. QWC Exam context: In a metal-R-T QWC, mentioning "resistivity increases with temperature" is rewarded alongside "resistance increases." Use both — they reinforce each other.

10.3 Ohmic vs Non-Ohmic

Spec ref: Spec 64-65 Definition: An ohmic conductor obeys Ohm's Law (V vs I graph is a straight line through the origin) at constant temperature. A non-ohmic conductor does not (V vs I graph is curved or non-linear). Metals are ohmic at constant T; thermistors, filament lamps, and diodes are non-ohmic. QWC Exam context: In a "compare metal and thermistor" QWC, the contrast "metals are ohmic at constant T, thermistors are non-ohmic" is rewarded as an L3 point.

Group 1: Electric Current and Potential Difference (Spec 64–65)

What Electric Current Actually Is — Microscopic Picture

What it is and why it matters: Electric current is defined as the rate of flow of electric charge: I = ΔQ/Δt. This is a macroscopic definition — it tells you what you measure with an ammeter. But to understand why current behaves as it does in circuits, you need the microscopic picture: current in a metal is the net drift of conduction electrons through a lattice of positive ions. The current value depends not just on how fast the electrons move (the drift velocity), but on how many charge carriers are available (the number density, n) and the cross-sectional area of the conductor (A). This distinction between macroscopic measurement and microscopic mechanism is tested repeatedly in explanations and calculations.

How it works — the two pictures:

Macroscopic: An ammeter reads current in amperes. One ampere means one coulomb of charge passes a point each second. The charge Q flowing in time t is Q = It. Charge is conserved: whatever enters one end of a wire must leave the other end.

Microscopic: In a metal wire, billions of free electrons exist per cubic metre (n ≈ 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³ for copper). These electrons move randomly at very high speeds (~10⁵ m s⁻¹) due to thermal energy, but this random motion averages to zero net current. When a p.d. is applied, a small additional drift velocity (v ≈ 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹) is superimposed on the random motion, producing a net flow of charge. The current depends on all four factors: I = nqvA.

Conventional current vs electron flow: Conventional current is defined as flowing from positive to negative terminal — this is the historical convention established before electrons were discovered. Electron flow in metals is actually from negative to positive. In exams, always use conventional current direction unless asked specifically about electron flow. For circuit diagrams and Kirchhoff's law applications, conventional current is the standard.

Connections to other terms: Current connects to charge via I = ΔQ/Δt (spec 64). It connects to p.d. and resistance via Ohm's law V = IR (spec 66). The microscopic picture links directly to drift velocity I = nqvA (spec 73). Charge conservation at a point underpins Kirchhoff's first law (spec 67a).

Real-world examples: A car starter motor draws about 200 A — this means 200 coulombs of charge flow each second, which is an enormous number of electrons (~1.25 × 10²¹). A mobile phone charger delivers about 1–2 A. An LED indicator might carry only 10 mA.

Potential Difference as Energy per Unit Charge

What it is and why it matters: Potential difference (p.d.) is defined as the energy transferred per unit charge between two points: V = W/Q. One volt means one joule of energy is transferred for each coulomb of charge that passes. This definition is the bridge between the electrical world (volts, amps, coulombs) and the energy/physics world (joules, watts). Understanding p.d. as energy per coulomb is essential for explaining why Kirchhoff's second law works (energy conservation), for understanding what e.m.f. actually means, and for interpreting the meaning of "lost volts" across internal resistance.

How it works: When a charge Q moves through a component across which there is a p.d. V, the energy transferred is W = QV. This energy is transferred from electrical form to other forms (thermal in a resistor, light in a lamp, kinetic in a motor). Conversely, a cell or battery transfers energy to the charge: the e.m.f. is the energy supplied per coulomb by the cell. The voltmeter measures p.d. by connecting in parallel across a component — it essentially measures how much energy each coulomb loses (or gains) as it passes through.

Connections to other terms: V = W/Q leads directly to electrical power: P = W/t = QV/t = VI (spec 69). The e.m.f. of a cell is the energy supplied per coulomb by the cell (spec 77). Kirchhoff's second law states that the total energy per coulomb supplied by sources equals the total energy per coulomb dissipated by components around any closed loop (spec 67b).

Real-world examples: A 12 V car battery supplies 12 joules of energy per coulomb of charge. A USB port provides 5 V, meaning each coulomb carries 5 J of energy. The p.d. across a kettle heating element (240 V in the UK) means each coulomb of charge delivers 240 J of thermal energy — at 10 A, this is 2400 W, enough to boil water rapidly.

Worked Example: Charge and Energy from Current and Time

A heater element passes a current of 8.5 A for 3.0 minutes. Calculate (a) the total charge that flows, and (b) the total energy transferred if the p.d. across the heater is 230 V.

(a) Charge:
Q = It
t = 3.0 minutes = 3.0 × 60 = 180 s
Q = 8.5 × 180
Q = 1530 C

(b) Energy transferred:
W = QV
W = 1530 × 230
W = 351 900 J
W = 3.5 × 10⁵ J  (to 2 s.f.)

Common error: Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds. Time must always be in seconds for I = Q/t and W = VIt to give correct SI units.

Group 2: Resistance and Ohm's Law (Spec 66)

Resistance — Microscopic Meaning

What it is and why it matters: Resistance is defined as the ratio R = V/I. A component with high resistance requires a large p.d. to drive a given current through it. At the microscopic level, resistance arises because conduction electrons repeatedly collide with the vibrating lattice ions in a metal, losing energy and being slowed down. Each collision transfers kinetic energy from the electron to the lattice, heating the material. The more frequent or more energetic these collisions, the higher the resistance.

How it works — the microscopic mechanism: When a p.d. is applied across a metal, electrons gain kinetic energy and accelerate. However, they travel only a very short distance (the mean free path, ~10⁻⁸ m) before colliding with a lattice ion. The collision randomises the electron's velocity direction, so the electron loses its directed motion and must be re-accelerated by the electric field. This repeated acceleration-collision cycle limits the average drift velocity. The resistance depends on three factors:

  • Number density of charge carriers (n): more carriers means more current for a given drift velocity
  • Mean free path: the average distance between collisions (affected by temperature and impurities)
  • Cross-sectional area (A): a wider wire provides more "lanes" for current to flow

Connections to other terms: R = V/I is the defining equation. Ohm's law states that for an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, R is constant. The resistivity ρ (spec 71) quantifies resistance as a material property: R = ρl/A. Resistance changes with temperature differently for metals (R increases) and semiconductors (R decreases) — see spec 79.

Ohmic vs Non-Ohmic Behaviour

What it is and why it matters: Ohm's law states that for an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to p.d. (I ∝ V), meaning resistance is constant. A non-ohmic conductor does not obey this relationship — its resistance changes with current or p.d. This distinction matters because many common components (filament lamps, thermistors, diodes) are non-ohmic, and exam questions frequently test whether you can identify which is which and explain why.

How it works — ohmic conductors: A fixed resistor (at constant temperature) has an I-V graph that is a straight line through the origin. The resistance R = V/I is the same at every point. This is because the lattice ion vibration amplitude doesn't change significantly (the resistor doesn't heat up noticeably), so the collision rate stays constant.

How it works — non-ohmic conductors: A filament lamp heats up as current increases. The higher temperature causes larger lattice ion vibrations, more frequent collisions, and higher resistance. The I-V graph curves away from the straight line — current increases less rapidly at higher p.d. values. A thermistor (NTC) behaves oppositely: higher current causes heating, which releases more charge carriers, decreasing resistance — the I-V curve becomes steeper at higher p.d.

The constant temperature condition: Ohm's law only holds at constant temperature. A question might say "a resistor is kept at constant temperature" — this means you can assume constant R. If the question doesn't state this, and the component is a filament lamp, you must read R from the I-V graph at the specific operating point.

Real-world examples: A copper wire at room temperature is approximately ohmic over a wide current range. A tungsten filament lamp is strongly non-ohmic — its resistance at operating temperature is about 10 times its cold resistance. A silicon diode is extremely non-ohmic: resistance is near-infinite in one direction and very low in the other.

Worked Example: Identifying Ohmic vs Non-Ohmic Behaviour

A student measures the current through a component for different p.d.s:

V (V)1.02.03.04.05.0
I (mA)1530445769

Determine whether this component is ohmic.

Step 1: Calculate R = V/I at each point
At V = 1.0 V:  R = 1.0 / (15 × 10⁻³) = 1.0 / 0.015 = 66.7 Ω
At V = 2.0 V:  R = 2.0 / (30 × 10⁻³) = 2.0 / 0.030 = 66.7 Ω
At V = 3.0 V:  R = 3.0 / (44 × 10⁻³) = 3.0 / 0.044 = 68.2 Ω
At V = 4.0 V:  R = 4.0 / (57 × 10⁻³) = 4.0 / 0.057 = 70.2 Ω
At V = 5.0 V:  R = 5.0 / (69 × 10⁻³) = 5.0 / 0.069 = 72.5 Ω

Step 2: Check if R is constant
R increases from 66.7 Ω to 72.5 Ω as p.d. increases.
The resistance is NOT constant.
Therefore, this component is non-ohmic.

Note: The small increase in R is consistent with slight heating of the component. If R were exactly constant at all points (e.g. all values ≈ 66.7 Ω), the component would be ohmic.

Group 3: Kirchhoff's Laws (Spec 67a, 67b)

Kirchhoff's First Law — Charge Conservation at Junctions

What it is and why it matters: Kirchhoff's first law states that the sum of currents entering any junction equals the sum of currents leaving it: ΣI_in = ΣI_out. This is a direct consequence of the conservation of charge — charge cannot accumulate or disappear at a junction. This law is the foundation for understanding how current divides in parallel circuits and for setting up circuit equations. It has appeared in QWC questions (Jun 2022, 6 marks) where you must explain the law using specific component labels from the given circuit.

How it works: At any junction (node) in a circuit, every ampere of current flowing in must flow out. If a wire carrying 3 A splits into two branches, the branch currents must add to 3 A — e.g. 1.8 A in one branch and 1.2 A in the other. Mathematically, you can write: I_total = I₁ + I₂ + ... If the question asks you to identify the physical principle, the answer is conservation of charge — this is what the mark scheme requires.

Applying it in exams:

  1. Identify every junction in the circuit
  2. Write an equation for each junction: currents in = currents out
  3. Solve the system of equations alongside other constraints (e.g. V = IR for each branch)

Exam trap (Jun 2022 QWC): The mark scheme required students to refer to the specific resistors labelled A, B, C in the given circuit diagram, not just give a general statement of Kirchhoff's first law. Generic answers without application to the specific circuit lost marks. Always apply the law to the components named in the question.

Kirchhoff's Second Law — Energy Conservation Around Loops

What it is and why it matters: Kirchhoff's second law states that the sum of potential differences around any closed loop equals the sum of electromotive forces in that loop: ΣV = Σε. This is a consequence of the conservation of energy — the energy per coulomb supplied by the source must equal the energy per coulomb dissipated by all components around the loop. This law explains why p.d.s are shared in series circuits and is essential for analysing any circuit with multiple loops.

How it works — the energy perspective: A cell with e.m.f. ε supplies ε joules of energy per coulomb. As each coulomb travels around the closed loop, it must lose exactly ε joules of energy across the components. If a 12 V battery drives current through two resistors in series, the p.d.s must satisfy V₁ + V₂ = 12 V. No energy is created or destroyed — this is Kirchhoff's second law in action.

Applying it in exams: For any closed loop:

  1. Choose a direction to travel around the loop (clockwise or anticlockwise — be consistent)
  2. As you cross each component, note the p.d. (taking it as positive if you go from − to +, negative if + to −)
  3. As you cross each source of e.m.f., note the e.m.f.
  4. Set ΣV_drops = Σe.m.f.s (or equivalently, the algebraic sum of all p.d.s and e.m.f.s around the loop = 0)

Connections to other terms: Kirchhoff's second law explains why V₁ + V₂ = ε for series resistors (spec 68). For a cell with internal resistance: the terminal p.d. V plus the "lost volts" Ir equals the e.m.f. ε — this is Kirchhoff's second law applied to the complete circuit including the cell's internal resistance (spec 77).

Worked Example: Applying Kirchhoff's Laws

A circuit has a 12 V battery connected to two parallel resistors: R₁ = 4.0 Ω and R₂ = 6.0 Ω. Calculate the current through each resistor and the total current from the battery.

Step 1: Apply Kirchhoff's second law (p.d. is the same across parallel branches)
V₁ = V₂ = ε = 12 V

Step 2: Calculate current through each resistor (V = IR)
I₁ = V/R₁ = 12 / 4.0 = 3.0 A
I₂ = V/R₂ = 12 / 6.0 = 2.0 A

Step 3: Apply Kirchhoff's first law at the junction
I_total = I₁ + I₂ = 3.0 + 2.0 = 5.0 A

Step 4: Verify using total resistance
1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ = 1/4.0 + 1/6.0 = 0.250 + 0.167 = 0.417 Ω⁻¹
R_total = 1/0.417 = 2.40 Ω
I_total = V/R_total = 12/2.40 = 5.0 A ✓

Group 4: Series and Parallel Circuits (Spec 68)

Series Circuits — Current, P.D., and Resistance

What it is and why it matters: In a series circuit, components are connected one after another along a single path. Three rules govern series circuits, all derivable from Kirchhoff's laws:

  1. Current is the same everywhere (Kirchhoff's first law — there are no junctions)
  2. P.d.s add up (Kirchhoff's second law — V₁ + V₂ + ... = ε)
  3. Resistances add up (R_total = R₁ + R₂ + ...)

These rules are fundamental for simplifying circuits and solving problems. Adding more resistors in series always increases total resistance — each additional resistor adds another obstacle for current to overcome.

How it works: If a 6 V battery is connected to a 2 Ω and a 4 Ω resistor in series, the total resistance is 6 Ω. The current is I = V/R_total = 6/6 = 1 A (same through both). The p.d. across the 2 Ω resistor is V₁ = IR₁ = 1 × 2 = 2 V. The p.d. across the 4 Ω resistor is V₂ = IR₂ = 1 × 4 = 4 V. Check: V₁ + V₂ = 2 + 4 = 6 V = ε ✓. The larger resistor gets a larger share of the total p.d.

Real-world example: Old-style fairy lights are wired in series. If one bulb fails (breaks the circuit), all bulbs go out. This is because the current has only one path, and a break anywhere stops current everywhere. Modern fairy lights use parallel wiring so each bulb operates independently.

Parallel Circuits — Current, P.D., and Resistance

What it is and why it matters: In a parallel circuit, components are connected across the same two junctions, creating multiple paths for current. Three rules govern parallel circuits:

  1. P.d. is the same across each branch (all branches connect between the same two points)
  2. Currents add up (Kirchhoff's first law — I_total = I₁ + I₂ + ...)
  3. Reciprocal resistances add up (1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...)

Adding more resistors in parallel always decreases total resistance — each additional branch provides another path for current, so more current can flow for the same p.d.

How it works: If a 12 V supply is connected across two parallel resistors R₁ = 3 Ω and R₂ = 6 Ω, the p.d. across each is 12 V. Current through R₁: I₁ = 12/3 = 4 A. Current through R₂: I₂ = 12/6 = 2 A. Total current: I_total = 4 + 2 = 6 A. Total resistance: 1/R_total = 1/3 + 1/6 = 0.333 + 0.167 = 0.500, so R_total = 2.0 Ω. Note that R_total (2.0 Ω) is less than either individual resistor — this is always the case for parallel combinations.

Two-resistor shortcut: For exactly two resistors in parallel, R_total = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂). This avoids dealing with reciprocals.

Real-world example: Household appliances are wired in parallel. Each appliance has the full mains p.d. (230 V in the UK) across it. Turning off one appliance doesn't affect the others because each has its own independent path to the power supply.

Step-by-Step Simplification of Mixed Circuits

What it is and why it matters: A mixed circuit contains both series and parallel combinations. To find the total resistance, you must simplify step by step: first combine parallel groups into equivalent resistances, then add series resistances (or vice versa, depending on the circuit structure). This is tested frequently (7 of 13 papers for spec 68) and is a common source of errors.

How it works — systematic approach:

  1. Identify which resistors are in series (same current) and which are in parallel (same p.d.)
  2. Replace each parallel group with a single equivalent resistance using 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...
  3. Add all series resistances: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + ...
  4. Check: does the simplified circuit make physical sense?

Exam trap: A very common error (appearing in multiple papers) is adding parallel resistances directly without using the reciprocal formula — e.g. writing R_total = R₁ + R₂ for parallel resistors. Another error is getting confused about which resistors are truly in parallel (they must share the same two junctions, not just be drawn side by side).

Worked Example: Mixed Circuit Simplification

A 24 V supply is connected to the following circuit: a 6.0 Ω resistor in series with a parallel combination of a 4.0 Ω and a 12 Ω resistor. Calculate (a) the total resistance, (b) the total current, (c) the p.d. across the parallel combination, and (d) the current through the 12 Ω resistor.

Step 1: Calculate the equivalent resistance of the parallel combination
1/R_parallel = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ = 1/4.0 + 1/12
1/R_parallel = 0.250 + 0.0833 = 0.333 Ω⁻¹
R_parallel = 1/0.333 = 3.0 Ω

Step 2: Total resistance (series combination with the 6.0 Ω resistor)
R_total = 6.0 + R_parallel = 6.0 + 3.0 = 9.0 Ω

(b) Total current:
I_total = V/R_total = 24/9.0 = 2.67 A

(c) P.D. across the parallel combination:
V_parallel = I_total × R_parallel = 2.67 × 3.0 = 8.0 V

(Verify: p.d. across 6.0 Ω resistor = 2.67 × 6.0 = 16.0 V. 16.0 + 8.0 = 24 V ✓)

(d) Current through the 12 Ω resistor:
I₁₂ = V_parallel / R₁₂ = 8.0 / 12 = 0.667 A

(Verify: current through 4.0 Ω = 8.0/4.0 = 2.0 A. 2.0 + 0.667 = 2.67 A ✓)

Common error: Using the shortcut R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂) when there are three or more parallel resistors. This formula works for TWO resistors only. For three or more, you must use the reciprocal formula.

Group 5: Electrical Power and Energy (Spec 69)

Three Forms of the Power Equation — When to Use Each

What it is and why it matters: Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer: P = VI. This is the fundamental form. From it, two alternative forms can be derived using Ohm's law (V = IR):

  • P = I²R — substitute V = IR into P = VI → P = I(IR) = I²R
  • P = V²/R — substitute I = V/R into P = VI → P = V(V/R) = V²/R

All three are equivalent for ohmic conductors. However, choosing the right form makes calculations faster and less error-prone, and for non-ohmic components, you must be careful about which form you apply.

How to choose which form to use:

  • Use P = VI when you know both V and I (most general — works even for non-ohmic components)
  • Use P = I²R when you know I and R but not V directly
  • Use P = V²/R when you know V and R but not I directly

Critical point for non-ohmic components: For non-ohmic components (filament lamps, diodes, thermistors), V ≠ IR with constant R. So P = I²R and P = V²/R use the instantaneous resistance R = V/I at that operating point, which is fine if you read V and I from the I-V graph. But you cannot assume R is the same as the "cold" resistance.

Connections to other terms: P = VI connects to W = Pt (energy = power × time) and W = VIt (energy = p.d. × current × time). The total power from a cell is P = Iε (spec 77), which splits into useful power P_ext = I²R_ext and wasted power P_int = I²r. A kilowatt-hour (kW h) is a unit of energy used for billing: 1 kW h = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.

Power Balance with Internal Resistance

What it is and why it matters: When a cell with internal resistance r drives a current I through an external circuit of resistance R_ext, the power equations split into:

  • Total power supplied by cell: P_total = Iε
  • Power delivered to external circuit: P_ext = VI = I²R_ext (useful power)
  • Power wasted inside cell: P_int = I²r (dissipated as heat in the cell)

Conservation of energy requires: P_total = P_ext + P_int, i.e. Iε = I²R_ext + I²r. The maximum power is delivered to the external circuit when R_ext = r (maximum power transfer theorem), though this is less commonly tested than the basic power balance.

How it works: Consider a cell with ε = 9.0 V, r = 1.5 Ω, connected to R_ext = 4.5 Ω. The current is I = ε/(R_ext + r) = 9.0/6.0 = 1.5 A. Total power = Iε = 1.5 × 9.0 = 13.5 W. Power to external circuit = I²R_ext = 1.5² × 4.5 = 2.25 × 4.5 = 10.1 W. Power wasted = I²r = 1.5² × 1.5 = 2.25 × 1.5 = 3.4 W. Check: 10.1 + 3.4 = 13.5 W ✓.

Exam trap (Jun 2023 QWC): A question asked what happens to the ammeter and voltmeter readings when light intensity on an LDR increases. The mark scheme stated it "does not allow if stated that both ammeter and voltmeter readings decrease" — this is a contradiction. If LDR resistance decreases, total resistance decreases, current increases (ammeter reading goes UP), and terminal p.d. changes accordingly. You cannot have both readings decrease when resistance decreases, because P = Iε and ε is constant.

Worked Example: Power in a Circuit with Internal Resistance

A cell of e.m.f. 6.0 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω is connected to an external resistor of 5.5 Ω. Calculate (a) the current, (b) the terminal p.d., (c) the power delivered to the external resistor, and (d) the power wasted in the internal resistance.

(a) Current:

I = 6.0 / (5.5 + 0.50)

I = 1.0 A

(b) Terminal p.d.:
V = ε - Ir = 6.0 - (1.0 × 0.50)
V = 6.0 - 0.50
V = 5.5 V

(c) Power delivered to external resistor:
P_ext = I²R_ext = 1.0² × 5.5
P_ext = 1.0 × 5.5
P_ext = 5.5 W

Alternative: P_ext = VI = 5.5 × 1.0 = 5.5 W ✓

(d) Power wasted in internal resistance:
P_int = I²r = 1.0² × 0.50
P_int = 1.0 × 0.50
P_int = 0.50 W

Group 6: I–V Characteristics (Spec 70)

Ohmic Conductor, Filament Lamp, Thermistor, Diode — Shape of Graphs and WHY

What it is and why it matters: The I–V characteristic (I–V graph) of a component reveals everything about its behaviour: whether it obeys Ohm's law, how its resistance changes with p.d., and the microscopic mechanisms at work. Four components are specified in the IAL Physics specification, each with a distinctive graph shape and physical explanation. Questions can ask you to sketch any of these graphs, identify which graph corresponds to which component, or explain the shape in terms of microscopic physics.

Ohmic conductor (e.g. a fixed resistor at constant temperature):

  • Graph shape: Straight line through the origin
  • Why: Constant temperature → constant lattice vibration amplitude → constant collision frequency → constant resistance → I ∝ V (Ohm's law)
  • Resistance: R = V/I is constant everywhere on the graph

Filament lamp:

  • Graph shape: Curve through the origin, starts steep then becomes less steep (current increases more slowly at higher p.d.)
  • Why: Higher current → more heating → higher temperature → larger lattice ion vibrations → more frequent collisions with electrons → resistance increases → for a given increase in V, the increase in I gets smaller
  • Resistance: R = V/I increases along the curve. At 2 V the resistance might be 4 Ω; at 10 V it might be 40 Ω
  • Key point: The mechanism is lattice vibrations — a metal mechanism

NTC thermistor (semiconductor):

  • Graph shape: Curve through the origin, starts shallow then becomes steeper (current increases more rapidly at higher p.d.)
  • Why: Higher current → self-heating → higher temperature → more electrons promoted to conduction band → more charge carriers (n increases) → resistance decreases → for a given increase in V, the increase in I gets larger
  • Resistance: R = V/I decreases along the curve
  • Key point: The mechanism is charge carrier release — a semiconductor mechanism, fundamentally different from the filament lamp

Diode:

  • Graph shape: Approximately zero current for negative p.d. (reverse bias) and for small positive p.d.; then a sharp upward turn above the threshold voltage (~0.6 V for silicon)
  • Why: In reverse bias, the depletion region blocks current. In forward bias, below the threshold voltage, the p-n junction still presents a significant barrier. Above the threshold voltage, the barrier is overcome and current flows easily with very little additional p.d.
  • Resistance: Very high in reverse bias and low forward bias below threshold; very low above threshold

Exam trap: The gradient of an I-V graph is I/V (= 1/R), NOT V/I (= R). A steeper I-V graph means LOWER resistance. For a filament lamp, the graph becomes less steep at higher V (gradient decreases), meaning resistance INCREASES. For a thermistor, the graph becomes steeper at higher V, meaning resistance DECREASES. Students often confuse the gradient with the reciprocal of the gradient.

Reading Resistance from an I-V Graph

What it is and why it matters: At any point on an I-V graph, the resistance is R = V/I (the ratio of the coordinates), NOT the gradient (ΔI/ΔV). For an ohmic conductor, these happen to give the same result because the graph is a straight line. But for non-ohmic components, the ratio V/I changes along the curve.

How it works: To find R at a specific operating point:

  1. Read the V and I values from the graph at that point
  2. Calculate R = V/I

To estimate R from the gradient: the gradient of the I-V graph is ΔI/ΔV = 1/R. So R = 1/(gradient). This gives the same answer as V/I only if the graph is a straight line through the origin.

Worked example: At a point on a filament lamp I-V graph where V = 6.0 V and I = 0.25 A: R = 6.0/0.25 = 24 Ω. At another point where V = 3.0 V and I = 0.20 A: R = 3.0/0.20 = 15 Ω. The resistance has increased from 15 Ω to 24 Ω as the p.d. doubled, confirming non-ohmic behaviour.

Worked Example: I-V Graph for a Filament Lamp

A filament lamp has the following I-V data:

V (V)1.02.03.04.05.0
I (A)0.200.320.400.460.50

(a) Calculate the resistance at V = 2.0 V and V = 5.0 V. (b) Explain why the resistance increases, referring to the microscopic mechanism.

(a) At V = 2.0 V:
R = V/I = 2.0 / 0.32 = 6.25 Ω

At V = 5.0 V:
R = V/I = 5.0 / 0.50 = 10.0 Ω

The resistance increases from 6.25 Ω to 10.0 Ω.

(b) As the p.d. (and current) increases, the filament heats up.
At higher temperature, the lattice ions vibrate more vigorously
with greater amplitude. This increases the frequency of collisions
between the conduction electrons and the lattice ions.
The mean drift velocity of the electrons therefore decreases,
which means the resistance increases.
This is a METAL mechanism — do NOT mention charge carrier release.

Group 7: Resistivity (Spec 71)

R = ρl/A — Material Property vs Component Property

What it is and why it matters: Resistivity (ρ) is a fundamental property of a material (not a component). It is defined by R = ρl/A, where R is resistance, l is length, and A is cross-sectional area. Resistivity tells you how strongly a material opposes current flow, independent of the shape or size of the sample. This distinction between resistivity (intrinsic to the material) and resistance (depends on the specific component's dimensions) is a key concept tested in calculations and explanations. Resistivity has appeared in 10 of 13 papers, making it the single highest-yield topic in Ch 2.4.

How it works: From R = ρl/A:

  • Resistance is directly proportional to length (doubling the length doubles the resistance — electrons must pass through twice as many lattice ions)
  • Resistance is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area (doubling the area halves the resistance — a wider wire provides more "lanes" for current)
  • Resistivity ρ is the constant of proportionality that depends only on the material and temperature

Typical values:

  • Copper: ρ ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m (excellent conductor)
  • Nichrome: ρ ≈ 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m (used for heating elements — high resistance metal)
  • Silicon (semiconductor): ρ ≈ 6.4 × 10⁻² Ω m
  • Glass (insulator): ρ ≈ 10¹² Ω m

Connections to other terms: R = ρl/A connects to the microscopic picture via drift velocity and number density — high n and large mean free path give low ρ (good conductors). Temperature dependence of ρ connects to spec 79: metals have ρ that increases with temperature; semiconductors have ρ that decreases.

Graphical Method for Determining Resistivity

What it is and why it matters: The standard method for finding resistivity experimentally is to measure R for different lengths l of a wire of constant cross-sectional area A, plot R against l, and determine ρ from the gradient. This graphical method is heavily tested because it combines measurement, plotting, and calculation skills.

How it works:

  1. Measure the resistance R of the wire at several different lengths l
  2. Plot a graph of R (y-axis) against l (x-axis)
  3. Draw the best-fit straight line through the points
  4. Calculate the gradient: gradient = ΔR/Δl
  5. Since R = ρl/A, and A is constant, R = (ρ/A) × l. This is of the form y = mx, so the gradient = ρ/A
  6. Calculate resistivity: ρ = gradient × A

Why this method is good: Using a graph averages out random errors in individual measurements, making the result more reliable than a single calculation. It also allows you to identify anomalous points. A line through the origin confirms R ∝ l.

Unit Conversions — The Most Common Source of Error

What it is and why it matters: In resistivity calculations, the cross-sectional area is often given in mm² but must be converted to m² for the formula ρ = RA/l (since ρ has units Ω m). The conversion is: 1 mm² = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m². This is the single most common calculation error in Ch 2.4 — using mm² directly gives an answer that is wrong by a factor of 10⁶. The mark scheme often includes a mark for correct unit conversion.

How to convert diameter to area in SI units:

  1. If diameter is given in mm, convert to m: d(m) = d(mm) × 10⁻³
  2. Calculate area: A = πd²/4 (with d in m, giving A in m²)
  3. OR: calculate area in mm² as A = πd²/4 (with d in mm), then convert: A(m²) = A(mm²) × 10⁻⁶

Common error chain: Diameter = 0.50 mm → radius = 0.25 mm (not 0.25 m) → A = π(0.25 × 10⁻³)² = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m². Students who write A = π(0.25)² = 0.196 m² are off by a factor of 10⁷.

Worked Example: Calculating Resistivity from Measurements

A nichrome wire has length 1.20 m and diameter 0.40 mm. When a p.d. of 3.0 V is applied across it, the current is 0.87 A. Calculate the resistivity of nichrome.

Step 1: Calculate the resistance
R = V/I = 3.0 / 0.87
R = 3.45 Ω

Step 2: Convert diameter to metres and calculate area
d = 0.40 mm = 0.40 × 10⁻³ m = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m
A = πd²/4 = π × (4.0 × 10⁻⁴)² / 4
A = π × 1.6 × 10⁻⁷ / 4
A = 5.03 × 10⁻⁸ / 4
A = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m²

Step 3: Calculate resistivity
ρ = RA/l = (3.45 × 1.26 × 10⁻⁷) / 1.20
ρ = 4.35 × 10⁻⁷ / 1.20
ρ = 3.62 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m
ρ = 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m  (to 2 s.f.)

Group 7a: Core Practical 7 — Determining Resistivity (Spec 72)

How to Determine Resistivity Experimentally

What it is and why it matters: Spec point 72 — the core practical CP7 — requires the candidate to experimentally determine the resistivity ρ of a metal wire. This is distinct from Spec 71 (calculating ρ from given R, l, A in a single-step question). CP7 tests practical skills: choosing instruments, designing a circuit, controlling variables, processing uncertainty, and selecting a graphical method. CP7 first appeared as an explicitly-tagged spec point in Jan 2025 (Q13b, 3 marks) and Jun 2025 (Q11, 3 marks); the historical WPH12 series (Jun 2019 – Oct 2024, 11 papers) contains 0 marks attributable to spec 72 directly. However, the underlying resistivity theory (Spec 71, R = ρl/A) is the single highest-yield topic in Ch 2.4: 26 total marks across 10 of 13 papers, avg 3.7 marks/session. The Jan 2025 + Jun 2025 papers tested CP7 in two complementary directions: Jan 2025 Q13b asks for ρ from R, l, d (the "forward" calculation), and Jun 2025 Q11 asks for d from ρ, R, l (the "inverted" calculation).

Apparatus (with resolutions and justifications): A length of test wire (constantan or nichrome, ~1 m, diameter ~0.3–0.5 mm) taped to a metre rule; a micrometer (resolution ±0.01 mm — chosen because A ∝ d², so diameter error is doubled in area); a metre rule (resolution ±1 mm — adequate because %unc(l) is typically <0.2 %); a low-voltage DC power supply (0–6 V); a variable resistor/rheostat (0–20 Ω, ≥1 A); an ammeter (digital, ±0.01 A); a voltmeter (digital, ±0.01 V, internal resistance ≥1 MΩ); a switch; connecting leads; crocodile clips ×2. Optional: a thermometer to confirm constant wire temperature.

How it works — the canonical CP7 procedure:

  1. Check the micrometer for zero error. Close jaws fully using the ratchet (not the thimble — the ratchet ensures consistent pressure). If reading is non-zero, record the zero error and subtract/add it from every diameter reading.
  2. Measure the diameter d at three positions along the wire and at two perpendicular orientations at each position (six readings total), using a micrometer. Take the mean — this averages out non-uniformity and ovality.
  3. Calculate A = πd²/4 (or πr² with r = d/2), with d converted from mm to m first (×10⁻³). For a rectangular strip/panel, use A = width × thickness.
  4. Set up the circuit: ammeter in series with the wire, voltmeter in parallel across the wire (between crocodile clips that contact the wire at chosen length positions — NOT across the supply or rheostat), variable resistor in series to keep the current constant, switch in series to break the circuit between readings.
  5. Vary the length l of wire between the voltmeter contacts (e.g. 1.000, 0.900, 0.800, 0.700, 0.600, 0.500 m — six data points minimum). At each length, close the switch, read V and I, then open the switch. Adjust the variable resistor as needed to keep I constant (≤ 0.5 A).
  6. Calculate R = V/I at each length (or use an ohmmeter in place of the V and I measurements).
  7. Plot R (y-axis) against l (x-axis). The relationship R = ρl/A (with A constant) is of the form y = mx, so the best-fit straight line has gradient = ρ/A. A non-zero y-intercept indicates contact resistance at the crocodile clips or a voltmeter zero error.
  8. Calculate ρ = gradient × A. Compare the experimental value to the accepted value (e.g. constantan 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m, nichrome 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m) and quote with percentage uncertainty.

Why keep the current low and use a switch: A current through the wire dissipates power P = I²R as heat. If the wire heats up, its resistivity rises (for a metal: lattice vibrations increase → more collisions → higher ρ), so ρ is no longer constant across the l values — the R–l graph would no longer be a straight line through the origin. Keeping I low (≤ 0.5 A) and opening the switch between readings keeps the wire near room temperature. This is the single most-frequently-missed procedural point in candidate scripts (cited in 7+ WPH13 examiner reports).

Uncertainty propagation — the "d² trap": Because A = πd²/4 depends on d squared, the percentage uncertainty in A is twice the percentage uncertainty in d. From ρ = RA/l:

  • %unc(ρ) = %unc(R) + %unc(A) + %unc(l)
  • %unc(A) = 2 × %unc(d)
  • Therefore: %unc(ρ) = %unc(R) + 2 × %unc(d) + %unc(l)

The diameter term dominates the uncertainty budget: for d = 0.30 mm measured with a ±0.005 mm half-resolution micrometer, 2 × %unc(d) ≈ 3.3 % — an order of magnitude larger than the length term (0.05 % for l = 1.000 m). This is WHY the micrometer (rather than callipers or a rule) is mandatory for the diameter measurement. For Jan 2025 Q13b's data (d = 0.51 mm, micrometer ±0.01 mm): %unc(d) = 0.01/0.51 ≈ 2.0 %, so %unc(A) ≈ 4.0 %.

Group 7b: Core Practical 8 — Determining EMF and Internal Resistance (Spec 78)

How to Determine EMF and Internal Resistance Experimentally

What it is and why it matters: Spec point 78 — the core practical CP8 — requires the candidate to experimentally determine both the electromotive force ε and the internal resistance r of a cell (or battery). CP8 first appeared as an explicitly-tagged spec point in Jan 2025; the historical WPH12 series (Jun 2019 – Oct 2024) contains 0 marks attributable to spec 78 directly. However, the underlying e.m.f./internal-resistance theory (Spec 77, V = ε − Ir) is the second-highest-yield topic in Ch 2.4: 23 total marks across 9 of 13 papers, avg 3.3 marks/session. Across Jan 2025 + Jun 2025, CP8 contributed 7 marks (avg 3.5/session). The full practical method (apparatus, circuit, V-I graph, gradient/intercept extraction, polarisation, errors) is examined in WPH13, but every WPH12 paper tests at least one CP8-flavoured sub-skill — circuit diagram, graph interpretation, single-data-point calculation, power in internal resistance, or real-world application.

Apparatus (canonical CP8): A cell (1.5 V D-cell fresh, or 9 V PP3 battery — must be a real source, NOT a stabilised power supply which has r ≈ 0); a variable resistor/rheostat (0–50 Ω, ≥1 A); an ammeter (digital, ±0.01 A, low internal resistance ≤0.1 Ω); a high-resistance voltmeter (digital, ±0.01 V, internal resistance ≥1 MΩ); a switch (SPST, ≥2 A); short, thick connecting leads with crocodile clips (≥1 mm² to minimise stray series resistance).

Circuit diagram (canonical CP8):

    +─────[ammeter]─────[variable resistor]─────[switch]─────+
    │                                                          │
   [cell]                                                      │
    │                                                          │
    │     +────[voltmeter]────+                                 │
    │     │                    │                                │
    +─────┴────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────+

The ammeter is in series with the cell and variable resistor; the voltmeter is in parallel across the cell's terminals (NOT across the variable resistor — placing it across the rheostat would measure V_external = IR_external only, missing the lost-volts comparison). The variable resistor must be present to vary the current.

How it works — the method:

  1. Inspect the cell and zero-check the meters. Confirm the cell is fresh (open-circuit voltmeter reading ≥ 1.5 V for a D-cell).
  2. Set up the series circuit as above. Connect the voltmeter last, directly across the cell terminals.
  3. Set the variable resistor to its MAXIMUM resistance before closing the switch (limits initial current, protects the cell and ammeter).
  4. Close the switch. Read and record the ammeter (I) and voltmeter (V) values. Open the switch immediately after the reading to prevent polarisation and discharge.
  5. Decrease the variable resistance slightly. Close the switch, read I and V, open the switch.
  6. Repeat to obtain at least 6 (preferably 8) pairs of (I, V) readings spread across the full available current range (e.g. 0.05 A to 0.80 A). Aim for roughly even spacing.
  7. Open-circuit check (recommended). With the switch open, read the voltmeter. This should equal ε. Compare with the y-intercept of the V–I graph.
  8. Plot V (y-axis) against I (x-axis). Draw the best-fit straight line. The line should pass through (or as close as possible to) as many points as possible, with roughly equal numbers above and below.
  9. Read off ε from the y-intercept (where I = 0, V = ε).
  10. Calculate the gradient using a large triangle on the best-fit line: gradient = ΔV/ΔI. The gradient is negative (V decreases as I increases).
  11. Calculate r = |gradient|. Because V = ε − Ir is of the form y = mx + c with m = −r, the magnitude of the gradient equals r.

Why open the switch between readings (CP8 procedural mark): A cell in continuous use polarises — chemical reaction products (e.g. hydrogen bubbles on the positive electrode) build up at the electrodes, lowering ε and raising r over time. If the switch is left closed, the cell's ε and r drift between readings, so the (I, V) data set no longer reflects a single (ε, r) pair. Opening the switch lets the cell depolarise between readings, keeping (ε, r) constant.

The single-data-point method (Jun 2025 Q14a, Oct 2023 Q13b(i)): If only one (V, I) pair is available (or the question gives ε, V, R_ext as numbers), the V–I graph method is replaced by direct algebra: I = V/R_ext (using the terminal p.d., NOT ε), then r = (ε − V)/I = (ε − V)R_ext/V. This is what Jun 2025 Q14a tests: ε = 1.5 V, V = 1.4 V across R_ext = 10 Ω → I = 0.14 A → r = 0.1/0.14 = 0.71 Ω.

The MCQ method (Jan 2025 Q10): When the question asks for the correct algebraic expression for r (rather than a numerical value), start from ε = V + Ir and rearrange: r = (ε − V)/I. Substituting I = V/R_ext gives r = (ε − V)R_ext/V, which can be rewritten as R_ext × (ε − V)/V — matching the Jan 2025 Q10 correct option "10(9 − 6)/6" (with R_ext = 10 Ω, ε = 9 V, V = 6 V).

Why the gradient is negative (physical picture): As I increases (more current drawn from the cell), more charge flows through the internal resistance r per second. The "lost volts" Ir increases. By conservation of energy (Kirchhoff's second law): ε = V + Ir, so as Ir increases, V must decrease. Hence V falls as I rises — a downward-sloping straight line with gradient = −r.

Two graph forms and why both matter: CP8 can be examined using two graph forms:

  • Form 1 — Standard V-I graph (Jun 2023 Q12a, Jan 2024 Q14c(i), etc.): y-intercept = ε, gradient = −r (so r = |gradient|).
  • Form 2 — R-against-1/I graph (Jan 2023 Q17a/b — only paper in audit window using this form): divide ε = IR + Ir by I → R = ε/I − r. Gradient = ε, y-intercept = −r (NEGATIVE — students must take the magnitude).

Two cells in series: When two identical cells (each ε, r) are connected in series, both the e.m.f.s and the internal resistances add: ε_total = 2ε, r_total = 2r. On the V-I graph: y-intercept doubles and gradient doubles (becomes more negative). On the R-against-1/I graph: gradient doubles (to 2ε) and y-intercept doubles in magnitude (to −2r). Tested in Jan 2023 Q17d (draw new line) and Jun 2023 Q12b (describe change qualitatively).

Cells in parallel: When N identical cells (each ε, r) are connected in parallel, the e.m.f. stays the same (ε_total = ε) and the internal resistances combine in parallel: r_total = r/N. Common trap: parallel cells DO NOT add e.m.f.s. Tested in Jan 2024 Q19c (parallel vs series power comparison, 5 marks).

Uncertainty in CP8 (worst-fit lines): Draw error bars on each point (vertical bars of ±ΔV, horizontal bars of ±ΔI). Draw the steepest possible line that still passes through (or within one error bar of) every point — read its gradient m_max and intercept c_max. Draw the shallowest possible line with the same property — read m_min and c_min. The uncertainties are: Δm = ½ × |m_max − m_min| → Δr = Δm; Δc = ½ × |c_max − c_min| → Δε = Δc. The % uncertainty in r is typically significantly larger than in ε because the gradient is more sensitive to scatter.

Group 8: Drift Velocity (Spec 73)

I = nqvA — The Microscopic Current Equation

What it is and why it matters: The equation I = nqvA connects the macroscopic current I to four microscopic quantities: n (number density of charge carriers), q (charge of each carrier), v (mean drift velocity), and A (cross-sectional area). This equation explains why different materials and wire dimensions produce different currents, and it gives the counterintuitive result that good conductors have very slow electron drift. It has appeared in 8 of 13 papers and is consistently a source of confusion due to the counterintuitive nature of drift velocity.

How it works — physical meaning of each term:

  • I — the macroscopic current you measure (A)
  • n — number of free charge carriers per cubic metre (m⁻³). This is enormously high in metals (~8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³ for copper) and much lower in semiconductors (~10¹⁰–10¹⁶ m⁻³)
  • q — charge of one carrier (C). For metals, q = e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
  • v — the mean drift velocity (m s⁻¹). This is the tiny average velocity superimposed on the much larger random thermal motion
  • A — cross-sectional area of the conductor (m²)

Why drift velocity is tiny in metals (THE counterintuitive result): Rearranging: v = I/(nqA). For copper, n ≈ 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. Even for a current of 1 A through a wire of area 1 mm², v = 1.0/(8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.0 × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 7.4 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹ — about 0.07 mm per second, slower than a snail. The current is high not because electrons move fast, but because there are so enormously many of them.

In semiconductors, n is much lower (~10¹⁶ m⁻³), so for the same current and area, v ≈ 6 × 10² m s⁻¹ — millions of times faster than in copper. This is because fewer carriers must move faster to carry the same current.

Exam trap (3 papers): Students intuitively expect good conductors to have fast-moving electrons. The exam repeatedly tests that you understand the opposite: high n means low v. If asked "compare drift velocity in copper vs silicon for the same current," the answer is that silicon has much HIGHER drift velocity.

Drift Velocity in Series vs Parallel Circuits

What it is and why it matters: The relationship v = I/(nqA) has different implications for series and parallel circuits. This context-switch is tested in 2+ papers, where one part asks about drift velocity in a parallel configuration and another asks about it in a series configuration with different wire cross-sections.

In series: Current I is the same everywhere (Kirchhoff's first law). If a wire changes cross-sectional area (e.g. a thick wire connected to a thin wire), the drift velocity must change. Where the wire is thinner (smaller A), the drift velocity is HIGHER: v = I/(nqA). Think of water in a pipe: where the pipe narrows, the water flows faster.

In parallel: Current divides between branches. Each branch carries a share of the total current. For identical wires in parallel, each carries I_total/N, so the drift velocity in each branch is lower than if the total current flowed through a single wire. For wires of different cross-sectional areas, each has different drift velocity depending on its own I and A.

Exam trap: A question might give a circuit where two wires are in parallel (part i), then change to a circuit where they are in series (part ii). The drift velocity relationship changes completely. Always check the circuit configuration before applying v = I/(nqA).

Worked Example: Calculating Drift Velocity in Copper

A copper wire has diameter 1.0 mm and carries a current of 2.0 A. The number density of free electrons in copper is 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. Calculate the mean drift velocity of the electrons.

Step 1: Calculate the cross-sectional area
d = 1.0 mm = 1.0 × 10⁻³ m
A = πd²/4 = π × (1.0 × 10⁻³)² / 4
A = π × 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ / 4
A = 7.85 × 10⁻⁷ m²

Step 2: Rearrange I = nqvA and solve for v
v = I / (nqA)
v = 2.0 / (8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 7.85 × 10⁻⁷)

Step 3: Calculate the denominator
nqA = 8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 7.85 × 10⁻⁷
nqA = 1.064 × 10³ m s⁻¹ per A

Step 4: Calculate v
v = 2.0 / 1.064 × 10³
v = 1.88 × 10⁻³ m s⁻¹
v = 1.9 × 10⁻³ m s⁻¹  (to 2 s.f.)

Interpretation: The electrons drift at about 1.9 mm per second — incredibly slow. Yet the current (2.0 A) is substantial because there are ~10²² electrons per metre of wire all drifting together.

Worked Example: Drift Velocity in Series with Changing Cross-Section

A metal wire of cross-sectional area 2.0 mm² carries a current of 3.0 A. The wire narrows to a cross-sectional area of 0.50 mm². Calculate the ratio of drift velocities in the narrow section to the wide section.

Step 1: Use v = I/(nqA) for each section
For the wide section: v₁ = I/(nqA₁)
For the narrow section: v₂ = I/(nqA₂)

v₂/v₁ = A₁/A₂

Step 3: Substitute values

Group 9: Potential Along a Uniform Wire (Spec 74)

Linear Potential Distribution

What it is and why it matters: For a uniform wire (constant cross-sectional area and constant resistivity) carrying a steady current, the potential decreases linearly from one end to the other. The p.d. per unit length is constant: V/l = IR/l = Iρ/A = constant. This means the p.d. between any two points is proportional to the distance between them. This principle underpins potentiometers and is the basis for the potential divider concept.

How it works: Consider a uniform wire of length L carrying current I. The total resistance of the wire is R = ρL/A. The p.d. across the whole wire is V_total = IR. The resistance per unit length is R/L = ρ/A. The p.d. per unit length is V_total/L = IR/L. For a contact point at distance x from one end, the p.d. from that end is:

V(x) = (V_total / L) × x = (ρI / A) × x

This is a linear relationship: a graph of potential V against position x is a straight line with gradient −ρI/A (negative because potential decreases in the direction of current flow).

Proportional reasoning: If a 100 cm wire has 12 V across it, then the p.d. across the first 25 cm is 25/100 × 12 = 3.0 V. The p.d. across the middle 50 cm is 50/100 × 12 = 6.0 V. This proportional relationship works for any uniform wire.

Connections to other terms: The linear potential distribution is the physical basis for potential dividers (spec 75) and the potentiometer (spec 76). A potentiometer is essentially a uniform wire with a sliding contact that taps off a variable fraction of the total p.d.

Worked Example: Potential at a Point Along a Wire

A uniform resistance wire of length 80 cm has a p.d. of 6.0 V across its full length. A voltmeter is connected between one end and a contact point 25 cm from that end. Calculate the reading on the voltmeter.

The p.d. is proportional to the distance (for a uniform wire):
V = V_total × (distance / total length)
V = 6.0 × (25 / 80)
V = 6.0 × 0.3125
V = 1.875 V
V = 1.9 V  (to 2 s.f.)

Group 10: Potential Divider Circuits (Spec 75–76)

Fixed Potential Dividers — The P.D. Ratio Rule

What it is and why it matters: A potential divider is two (or more) resistors in series across a supply, used to produce a specific fraction of the supply p.d. across one of the resistors. The fundamental principle is the p.d. ratio rule: V₁/V_total = R₁/R_total. In other words, the p.d. across each resistor is proportional to its share of the total resistance. This is one of the most frequently tested circuit concepts and appears in 8 of 13 papers when combining fixed and variable dividers (spec 75–76).

How it works: For two resistors R₁ and R₂ in series across a supply p.d. V_in:

  • Current: I = V_in / (R₁ + R₂)
  • P.d. across R₁: V₁ = IR₁ = V_in × R₁/(R₁ + R₂)
  • P.d. across R₂: V₂ = IR₂ = V_in × R₂/(R₁ + R₂)
  • Check: V₁ + V₂ = V_in × (R₁ + R₂)/(R₁ + R₂) = V_in ✓

The larger resistor gets the larger share of the p.d. If R₁ = 3R₂, then V₁ = 3V₂. If you need V_out = 4.0 V from a 12 V supply, you need V_out/V_in = R₂/(R₁ + R₂) = 4/12 = 1/3, so R₂ must be one third of the total resistance.

Exam trap (3 papers): The most common error is applying the ratio to the wrong component. Always draw the circuit, identify which component the voltmeter is measuring across, and use that component's resistance in the numerator. If the voltmeter is across the fixed resistor, use R_fixed in the formula. If it's across the thermistor, use R_thermistor.

Variable Potential Dividers — Sensor Circuits with LDRs and Thermistors

What it is and why it matters: A variable potential divider replaces one of the fixed resistors with a sensing component (NTC thermistor or LDR), so the output p.d. changes in response to temperature or light intensity. These sensor circuits are the basis for automatic switching systems: street lights, heating controllers, burglar alarms, and greenhouse ventilation. Three of four Ch 2.4 QWC questions involve sensor circuits, making this an extremely high-value topic for explanations.

How it works — NTC thermistor circuit:

Consider an NTC thermistor and a fixed resistor in series across a constant supply ε. The voltmeter is across the fixed resistor.

  1. Temperature increases
  2. Thermistor is an NTC semiconductor → its resistance decreases
  3. Total circuit resistance R_total = R_fixed + R_thermistor decreases
  4. Current increases (I = ε/R_total, since ε is constant)
  5. P.d. across the fixed resistor: V_fixed = IR_fixed increases (both I↑ and R_fixed constant)
  6. P.d. across the thermistor: V_thermistor = ε − V_fixed decreases

If the voltmeter is across the thermistor instead, the output DECREASES with increasing temperature. Which component the voltmeter is across completely changes the answer.

How it works — LDR circuit:

Consider an LDR and a fixed resistor in series across a constant supply ε. The voltmeter is across the fixed resistor.

  1. Light intensity increases
  2. More photons per second incident on the LDR → more electrons promoted to conduction band → more charge carriers released → LDR resistance decreases
  3. Total circuit resistance decreases
  4. Current increases
  5. P.d. across the fixed resistor increases

The complete causal chain for QWC answers: All QWC questions on this topic require the full chain: stimulus change → microscopic mechanism → resistance change → current change → p.d. change → final result. Isolated statements without causal links lose the 2 structure marks.

Worked Example: Fixed Potential Divider

A 9.0 V battery is connected to a potential divider consisting of a 4.0 kΩ and an 8.0 kΩ resistor in series. Calculate the p.d. across (a) the 4.0 kΩ resistor and (b) the 8.0 kΩ resistor.

(a) P.d. across the 4.0 kΩ resistor:
V₁ = V_in × R₁ / (R₁ + R₂)
V₁ = 9.0 × 4000 / (4000 + 8000)
V₁ = 9.0 × 4000 / 12000
V₁ = 9.0 × 0.333
V₁ = 3.0 V

(b) P.d. across the 8.0 kΩ resistor:
V₂ = V_in × R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)
V₂ = 9.0 × 8000 / 12000
V₂ = 9.0 × 0.667
V₂ = 6.0 V

Verify: V₁ + V₂ = 3.0 + 6.0 = 9.0 V = V_in ✓

Note: The 8.0 kΩ resistor (larger resistance) gets a larger share (6.0 V).
The ratio V₂/V₁ = R₂/R₁ = 8000/4000 = 2.0, so V₂ = 2 × V₁ ✓

Worked Example: Variable Potential Divider with LDR

An LDR and a 500 Ω fixed resistor are connected in series across a 12 V supply. The voltmeter is connected across the fixed resistor. In darkness, the LDR has a resistance of 5000 Ω. In bright light, the LDR resistance drops to 500 Ω. Calculate the voltmeter reading in (a) darkness and (b) bright light.

V_fixed = 12 × 500 / (500 + 5000)

V_fixed = 12 × 0.0909
V_fixed = 1.09 V

(b) In bright light (R_LDR = 500 Ω):

V_fixed = 12 × 500 / 1000
V_fixed = 12 × 0.500

The voltmeter reading increases from 1.09 V (dark) to 6.0 V (bright).

by using a relay that activates when V_fixed drops below a threshold.

Group 11: E.M.F. and Internal Resistance (Spec 77)

ε = V + Ir — The Fundamental Cell Equation

What it is and why it matters: A real cell or battery has an e.m.f. (ε) and an internal resistance (r). The e.m.f. is the total energy per coulomb supplied by the cell. The internal resistance causes some energy to be dissipated inside the cell when current flows, reducing the p.d. available to the external circuit. The equation ε = V + Ir (or equivalently V = ε − Ir) is Kirchhoff's second law applied to the complete circuit: the e.m.f. equals the terminal p.d. plus the "lost volts" across the internal resistance. This has appeared in 9 of 13 papers and is frequently combined with QWC explanations.

How it works — the energy perspective: Each coulomb of charge gains ε joules of energy from the chemical reactions inside the cell. As it passes through the external circuit, it delivers V joules per coulomb. The remaining Ir joules per coulomb are dissipated as thermal energy inside the cell (in the internal resistance). So ε = V + Ir is a statement of conservation of energy per coulomb.

Key consequences:

  • Open circuit (I = 0): V = ε. The voltmeter reads the full e.m.f. because no energy is lost internally.
  • Increasing current: As I increases, V = ε − Ir decreases linearly. A higher current means more "lost volts" inside the cell.
  • Short circuit (R_ext = 0): I = ε/r (maximum possible current), V = 0 (all e.m.f. is lost internally).

Exam trap: Students often confuse e.m.f. with terminal p.d. E.m.f. is a property of the cell itself (measurable with no current flowing). Terminal p.d. is what the voltmeter reads when current IS flowing, and it is always less than the e.m.f. (unless I = 0). If a question says "a voltmeter reads 5.0 V across the cell terminals while a current of 0.5 A flows," the terminal p.d. is 5.0 V, NOT the e.m.f. The e.m.f. must be calculated from ε = V + Ir.

V-I Graph Interpretation

What it is and why it matters: Plotting terminal p.d. (V) on the y-axis against current (I) on the x-axis for a real cell produces a straight line with negative gradient. This graph allows you to determine both the e.m.f. and the internal resistance experimentally. Graph interpretation is one of the most common question formats for this topic.

How to read the graph:

  • y-intercept (where I = 0): V = ε − 0 = ε. The y-intercept equals the e.m.f.
  • Gradient = ΔV/ΔI = −r. The magnitude of the (negative) gradient equals the internal resistance. Since V = ε − Ir, this is a line with slope −r and intercept ε.
  • x-intercept (where V = 0): 0 = ε − Ir, so I = ε/r. This is the short-circuit current.

Worked example from graph data: A V-I graph has y-intercept at 9.0 V and passes through the point (2.0 A, 7.2 V).

  • e.m.f. = y-intercept = 9.0 V
  • Gradient = (7.2 − 9.0)/(2.0 − 0) = −1.8/2.0 = −0.90 Ω
  • Internal resistance r = 0.90 Ω

Maximum Power Transfer

What it is and why it matters: The maximum power transfer theorem states that the maximum power is delivered to the external circuit when the external resistance equals the internal resistance: R_ext = r. This is relevant for designing efficient circuits and occasionally appears in calculation questions.

How it works: The power delivered to the external circuit is P_ext = I²R_ext = ε²R_ext/(R_ext + r)². Using calculus (or a table of values), this is maximised when R_ext = r. At this point, exactly half the total power goes to the external circuit and half is wasted internally. Note that this is maximum POWER, not maximum EFFICIENCY — the efficiency at R_ext = r is only 50%. For maximum efficiency, R_ext should be as large as possible (but this delivers very little power).

Worked Example: V-I Graph Analysis

A student plots a graph of terminal p.d. (V) against current (I) for a battery. The best-fit line passes through (0, 12.0) and (3.0, 10.5). Determine (a) the e.m.f., (b) the internal resistance, and (c) the short-circuit current.

(a) e.m.f. = y-intercept = 12.0 V

(b) Internal resistance from gradient:
gradient = ΔV/ΔI = (10.5 - 12.0) / (3.0 - 0)
gradient = -1.5 / 3.0
gradient = -0.50 Ω
r = |gradient| = 0.50 Ω

(c) Short-circuit current (x-intercept):
I_sc = ε / r = 12.0 / 0.50 = 24 A

Worked Example: Calculating Terminal P.D. and Lost Volts

A cell has e.m.f. 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.80 Ω. It is connected to an external resistor of 7.0 Ω. Calculate (a) the current, (b) the terminal p.d., and (c) the lost volts.

(a) Current:
I = ε / (R_ext + r) = 1.5 / (7.0 + 0.80)
I = 1.5 / 7.8
I = 0.192 A

(b) Terminal p.d.:
V = ε - Ir = 1.5 - (0.192 × 0.80)
V = 1.5 - 0.154
V = 1.35 V

Alternatively: V = IR_ext = 0.192 × 7.0 = 1.34 V
(Small difference due to rounding; use unrounded I for exact answer)

(c) Lost volts:
Lost volts = Ir = 0.192 × 0.80 = 0.154 V
Lost volts = 0.15 V (to 2 s.f.)

Verify: V + Ir = 1.35 + 0.15 = 1.50 V = ε ✓

Group 12: Resistance–Temperature Model (Spec 79)

Metal Mechanism — Lattice Vibrations

What it is and why it matters: When the temperature of a metal increases, its resistance increases. The mechanism involves the thermal vibration of lattice ions — the positive metal ions that form the regular crystal lattice. This is one half of the R-T model and is the explanation required for any question about metals, filament lamps, or standard resistors at varying temperature. Confusing this mechanism with the semiconductor mechanism is the #1 exam trap in Ch 2.4, appearing in 4 papers.

How it works — the complete causal chain:

  1. Temperature increases
  2. The lattice ions gain thermal energy
  3. The lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude about their fixed positions
  4. The conduction electrons (which are free to move through the lattice) collide more frequently with the vibrating ions
  5. The collision frequency increases
  6. The mean drift velocity of the electrons decreases (electrons are scattered more often)
  7. Current decreases for the same p.d. → resistance increases

Key mark scheme points: The mark scheme consistently requires mention of "lattice ions" (not just "atoms"), "vibrate more" or "greater amplitude of vibration," and the consequence that electrons "collide more frequently" leading to decreased drift velocity. Simply stating "resistance increases" without the mechanism scores minimal marks.

Real-world example: A tungsten filament lamp at room temperature has a resistance of about 4 Ω. When operating at 2500 °C, its resistance is about 40 Ω — ten times higher. This is why filament lamps are non-ohmic: the operating temperature changes the resistance significantly.

Semiconductor Mechanism — Charge Carrier Release (NTC Thermistor)

What it is and why it matters: When the temperature of a semiconductor (such as an NTC thermistor) increases, its resistance decreases. This is the opposite behaviour to metals and requires a completely different microscopic explanation. The mechanism involves the release of additional charge carriers into the conduction band as thermal energy increases. This is the explanation required for thermistor questions and for sensor circuit QWC answers.

How it works — the complete causal chain:

  1. Temperature increases
  2. More electrons in the semiconductor gain sufficient thermal energy to break free from their atoms
  3. These electrons are promoted to the conduction band (become free charge carriers)
  4. The number density of charge carriers (n) increases
  5. More charge carriers are available to carry current
  6. Current increases for the same p.d. → resistance decreases

Key distinction from metals: The semiconductor mechanism is about increasing n (more charge carriers). The metal mechanism is about increasing scattering (more collisions with same number of carriers). These are fundamentally different. The mark scheme EXPLICITLY REJECTS "lattice vibrations" as an explanation for thermistors. You must use "more charge carriers" or "more conduction electrons released."

Why "negative temperature coefficient": NTC means that the temperature coefficient dR/dT is negative — resistance goes DOWN when temperature goes UP. Most thermistors used in physics are NTC type. Positive temperature coefficient (PTC) thermistors exist but are not in the IAL specification.

R-T Graphs — Metal vs Thermistor

Metal R-T graph: Approximately a straight line with positive gradient. Resistance increases roughly linearly with temperature. The gradient is small for metals with low temperature coefficients (e.g. copper) and larger for others.

NTC thermistor R-T graph: A curve showing resistance decreasing non-linearly with temperature. At low temperatures, resistance is very high. At high temperatures, resistance drops dramatically. The relationship is approximately exponential: R ≈ R₀ × exp(B/T) where B is a constant and T is absolute temperature.

Worked example: A thermistor has a resistance of 5000 Ω at 20 °C and 200 Ω at 80 °C. This dramatic decrease (25×) is characteristic of semiconductor behaviour and contrasts sharply with metals where a 60 °C temperature rise might increase resistance by only 20–30%.

Exam Trap: The #1 Most Frequent Error in Ch 2.4

Trap description: Questions ask about resistance change with temperature for both metals AND semiconductors — often in the same paper. Candidates apply the wrong mechanism to the wrong material.

What the mark scheme accepts:

  • Metal: lattice vibrations ↑ → more collisions → drift velocity ↓ → R ↑
  • Thermistor (NTC): more electrons released/promoted to conduction band → n ↑ → R ↓

What the mark scheme REJECTS:

  • Saying a thermistor's resistance increases because of lattice vibrations
  • Saying a metal's resistance decreases because more charge carriers are released
  • Using either mechanism for the wrong material type

How to avoid this: Memorise the two mechanisms as a pair. Every time you see "temperature" in a question, first identify whether the component is a metal (filament, resistor) or semiconductor (thermistor), then apply the correct mechanism.

Group 13: Resistance–Illumination Model / LDRs (Spec 80)

Photon-Induced Charge Carrier Release

What it is and why it matters: A light-dependent resistor (LDR) is a semiconductor component whose resistance decreases as the intensity of light falling on it increases. The mechanism is analogous to the thermal mechanism in thermistors, but the energy source is photons instead of thermal energy. The mark scheme requires a particle-level explanation — stating "resistance decreases" without explaining WHY loses marks. This has appeared in 5 papers, including QWC questions (Jun 2023, Jan 2025).

How it works — the complete causal chain:

  1. Light intensity increases
  2. More photons per second are incident on the LDR semiconductor material
  3. Each photon that is absorbed can transfer energy to an electron in the semiconductor
  4. Electrons gain enough energy to break free from their atoms
  5. Electrons are promoted to the conduction band (become free charge carriers)
  6. The number density of charge carriers (n) increases
  7. More charge carriers are available to carry current → resistance decreases

Connection to the photoelectric effect (Ch 2.3): The mechanism is similar to the photoelectric effect: photons transfer energy to electrons, and if the photon energy exceeds the work function (or band gap in a semiconductor), electrons are freed. However, there are differences: the LDR mechanism involves promoting electrons across a small band gap within a semiconductor (not ejecting them from a metal surface), and the energy threshold is much lower than in the photoelectric effect.

Real-world examples: Automatic street lights use an LDR circuit: when it gets dark, LDR resistance increases, and the changing p.d. triggers a switch to turn on the lights. Camera light meters use LDRs to measure light intensity. Greenhouse automatic ventilation systems use LDRs to detect excessive sunlight and activate fans.

LDRs in Circuits — Combining with Internal Resistance

What it is and why it matters: The most demanding Ch 2.4 QWC question (Jan 2025 Q16b*, 6 marks) required linking the LDR mechanism with internal resistance. This combination tests whether you can track a physical change through multiple stages of a circuit that includes both a sensor component and a cell with internal resistance.

How it works — the full causal chain for an LDR + internal resistance circuit:

  1. Light intensity increases
  2. More photons → more charge carriers released in LDR → LDR resistance decreases
  3. The internal resistance r is a property of the cell — it is CONSTANT (does not change with light)
  4. Total circuit resistance R_total = R_LDR + r decreases (because only R_LDR changes)
  5. Current I = ε / R_total increases (ε is constant)
  6. Lost volts Ir increases (because I increases and r is constant)
  7. Terminal p.d. V = ε − Ir decreases
  8. The voltmeter reading decreases

Exam trap (Jan 2025 QWC): Candidates who studied LDRs and internal resistance as separate topics failed to link them. The chain requires explicitly stating that the internal resistance is constant, then tracking the consequences through the circuit. Skipping any step loses marks. The "or" in the mark scheme for the final point recognises that the terminal p.d. equals the p.d. across the LDR (when the LDR is the only external component), so either argument is accepted.

Worked Example: LDR Circuit Analysis

An LDR and a 400 Ω fixed resistor are connected in series with a cell of e.m.f. 9.0 V and negligible internal resistance. The voltmeter is across the fixed resistor. In dim light, the LDR has a resistance of 4000 Ω. In bright light, the LDR resistance drops to 400 Ω. Calculate the change in the voltmeter reading.

Step 1: In dim light (R_LDR = 4000 Ω):
R_total = 400 + 4000 = 4400 Ω
I = V/R_total = 9.0/4400 = 2.045 × 10⁻³ A
V_fixed = IR_fixed = 2.045 × 10⁻³ × 400 = 0.818 V

Step 2: In bright light (R_LDR = 400 Ω):
R_total = 400 + 400 = 800 Ω
I = V/R_total = 9.0/800 = 0.01125 A
V_fixed = IR_fixed = 0.01125 × 400 = 4.5 V

Step 3: Change in voltmeter reading:
ΔV = 4.5 − 0.8 = 3.7 V

The voltmeter reading increases from 0.8 V to 4.5 V
as light intensity increases.

Note: If the voltmeter were across the LDR instead, the readings would be:

  • Dim: V_LDR = 9.0 − 0.8 = 8.2 V
  • Bright: V_LDR = 9.0 − 4.5 = 4.5 V

The reading would DECREASE. This demonstrates why identifying which component the voltmeter is across is critical.

Worked Example: Full QWC-Style Explanation

Question: An LDR is connected in series with a cell of e.m.f. ε and internal resistance r. A voltmeter is connected across the terminals of the cell. Explain what happens to the voltmeter reading when the light intensity on the LDR increases.

As the light intensity on the LDR increases, more photons per second
are incident on the LDR. These photons transfer energy to electrons in
the semiconductor material of the LDR, promoting more electrons to the
conduction band. This releases more charge carriers, increasing the
number density n of charge carriers in the LDR, which causes the LDR's
resistance to decrease.

The internal resistance r of the cell is a constant property of the
cell itself and does not change. Since the total circuit resistance
R_total = R_LDR + r, and R_LDR has decreased while r is constant, the
total resistance decreases.

Because the e.m.f. ε of the cell is constant, the current in the
circuit increases: I = ε / R_total, and R_total has decreased.

The terminal p.d. is V = ε − Ir. Since I has increased and r is
constant, the lost volts (Ir) have increased. Therefore, the terminal
p.d. V decreases, and the voltmeter reading decreases.

Exam Traps Summary — Quick Reference

Trap 1: Confusing Metal and Semiconductor R-T Mechanisms (4 papers)

  • Metal: lattice vibrations ↑ → collisions ↑ → drift velocity ↓ → R ↑
  • NTC thermistor: more electrons released → n ↑ → R ↓
  • LDR: more photons → more electrons released → n ↑ → R ↓
  • Never mix these up. The mark scheme explicitly rejects wrong mechanisms.

Trap 2: Drift Velocity Counterintuitive Result (3 papers)

  • High n → low v (v = I/nqA)
  • Copper has tiny drift velocity (~10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹) despite being an excellent conductor
  • Semiconductors have much higher drift velocity than metals for the same current

Trap 3: Wrong Resistance Ratio in Potential Dividers (3 papers)

  • Always identify which component the voltmeter is across
  • V_out = V_in × R_measured / R_total
  • If measuring across the fixed resistor, use R_fixed in the ratio

Trap 4: Series vs Parallel Drift Velocity Context-Switch (2 papers)

  • Series: same I, different v where A changes (v ∝ 1/A)
  • Parallel: I divides, different v in each branch
  • Always check the circuit configuration before applying I = nqvA

Trap 5: E.m.f. vs Terminal P.D. Confusion

  • E.m.f. is the total energy per coulomb from the cell (measured with I = 0)
  • Terminal p.d. = ε − Ir (what the voltmeter reads when current flows)
  • On V-I graph: y-intercept = ε, gradient = −r

Trap 6: LDR Mechanism Not Explained at Particle Level (2 papers)

  • "Resistance decreases" is NOT sufficient — you must explain WHY
  • Full chain: more photons → more charge carriers released → n increases → R decreases

Trap 7: Series/Parallel Calculation Errors (multiple papers)

  • Parallel: use 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ (NOT R = R₁ + R₂)
  • For non-linear components, read R from I-V graph at the operating point
  • Mixed circuits: simplify step by step, not all at once

Topic 1 — Why Metal Resistance Increases with Temperature

1a. Typical Question Stem

"A student heats a length of copper wire while keeping the p.d. across it constant. The current through the wire decreases. Explain, in terms of the motion of conduction electrons and lattice ions, why the resistance of the wire increases with temperature. (6 marks)"

Variants: replace "copper" with any metal; replace "heats" with "passes a large current that warms the wire"; ask to "discuss" rather than "explain". The IC points are identical.

1b. Level Descriptor Breakdown

Level 1 (1–2 marks) — Basic:

  • States that "atoms vibrate more" or "more collisions".
  • No mention of drift velocity, mean free time, or R = V/I.
  • No causal chain — facts are listed.

Level 2 (3–4 marks) — Clear:

  • Mentions lattice ions (not just "atoms") vibrating with greater amplitude.
  • Links this to more frequent collisions and reduced drift velocity.
  • May mention current decreases.
  • Missing: mean free time terminology, or the final R = V/I link, or one step of the chain.

Level 3 (5–6 marks) — Detailed and Accurate:

  • Full causal chain: lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude → more frequent electron–ion collisions → mean free time decreases → drift velocity decreases → current decreases (at constant V) → R = V/I increases.
  • Uses correct terminology throughout (lattice ions, drift velocity, mean free time).
  • Connectives link every step.

1c. Model L3 Answer (connectives bolded)

When the copper wire is heated, the lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude about their equilibrium positions because the thermal energy increases their kinetic energy. Consequently, conduction electrons collide more frequently with the vibrating lattice ions, so the mean free time between collisions decreases. Since drift velocity v = qEτ/mₑ (where τ is the mean free time), the reduced τ means the drift velocity of the electrons decreases. As a result, fewer electrons pass a given cross-section per second, therefore the current I decreases (the p.d. is held constant). Because R = V/I and V is constant while I has decreased, the resistance R of the wire increases with temperature. The resistivity of copper likewise increases with temperature.

Topic 2 — Why NTC Thermistor Resistance Decreases with Temperature

2a. Typical Question Stem

"An NTC thermistor is connected in series with a cell and an ammeter. When the thermistor is heated, the ammeter reading increases. Explain why the resistance of the thermistor decreases as its temperature increases. (6 marks)"

Variants: ask for "discuss" (which expects acknowledgement of the competing lattice-vibration effect); ask to compare with a metal.

2b. Level Descriptor Breakdown

Level 1 (1–2 marks) — Basic:

  • Says "heat makes resistance go down" with no mechanism.
  • May mention "more electrons" but without context.

Level 2 (3–4 marks) — Clear:

  • Identifies that the thermistor is a semiconductor.
  • Mentions more charge carriers released.
  • Links to increased current and decreased R.
  • Missing: the "competing effects" discussion, or number density terminology, or I = nAvq.

Level 3 (5–6 marks) — Detailed and Accurate:

  • Identifies semiconductor material.
  • Explains that thermal energy releases more charge carriers (electrons promoted from valence band to conduction band).
  • States that number density n increases.
  • Uses I = nAvq to show I increases.
  • Acknowledges that increased lattice vibration would increase R, BUT the charge-carrier effect dominates in a semiconductor.
  • Concludes R = V/I decreases.

2c. Model L3 Answer (connectives bolded)

An NTC thermistor is made from a semiconductor material. When it is heated, thermal energy is absorbed by the lattice and this promotes electrons from the valence band into the conduction band, releasing more charge carriers. Consequently, the number density n of free charge carriers increases sharply with temperature. Because the current is given by I = nAvq, the increase in n means the drift velocity contribution per charge carrier is unchanged but the total current I increases (at constant V). However, the increased temperature also causes greater lattice vibration, which would tend to reduce drift velocity and increase resistance — as in a metal. In contrast to a metal, in a semiconductor the increase in charge carrier number density outweighs the lattice vibration effect, so the net effect is that I increases. Therefore, since R = V/I and V is constant while I has increased, the resistance of the NTC thermistor decreases with increasing temperature.

Topic 3 — Drift Velocity: Why It Is So Slow Despite Huge Current

3a. Typical Question Stem

"A current of 5.0 A flows in a copper wire of cross-sectional area 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m². The number density of conduction electrons in copper is 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. Calculate the drift velocity of the electrons, and explain why this drift velocity is so small even though the current is large. (6 marks)"

Variants: ask only for the explanation (no calculation); ask to "explain why the electrons drift so slowly compared with the speed of the current signal".

3b. Level Descriptor Breakdown

Level 1 (1–2 marks) — Basic:

  • States the equation I = nAvq.
  • Substitutes values and gets a numerical answer.
  • No explanation of why v is small.

Level 2 (3–4 marks) — Clear:

  • Calculates v correctly.
  • Mentions that n is very large (~10²⁸ m⁻³) so v can be small for a given I.
  • May mention collisions with lattice.
  • Missing: random thermal motion, or the role of q being fixed and A being limited.

Level 3 (5–6 marks) — Detailed and Accurate:

  • Calculates v correctly with units.
  • Rearranges I = nAvq and explains each term: n is huge, q is fixed, A is small.
  • Explains that v must therefore be small (~mm/s) for a reasonable I.
  • Explains WHY electrons move slowly: they constantly collide with lattice ions, randomising their motion; the drift is only a tiny bias on top of fast random thermal motion (~10⁵ m/s).
  • Distinguishes drift velocity from the speed of the electrical signal (~10⁸ m/s, near c).

3c. Model L3 Answer (connectives bolded)

The current is given by I = nAvq, where n is the number density of charge carriers, A is the cross-sectional area, v is the drift velocity, and q = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C is the charge per electron. Rearranging, v = I/(nAq) = 5.0 / (8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 3.7 × 10⁻⁴ m/s (about 0.4 mm/s). This drift velocity is very small because n is enormous (on the order of 10²⁸ m⁻³ in a metal), so even a tiny drift of each carrier produces a large current when multiplied by n, A, and q. Furthermore, q is a fixed fundamental constant and A is limited by the wire's physical size, therefore v must be small to give a reasonable current. The reason the electrons drift so slowly is that they are in constant random thermal motion at high speed (~10⁵ m/s) but collide frequently with the lattice ions; each collision randomises their velocity, so the electric field only imposes a tiny net drift on top of the random motion. However, the electrical signal itself travels at nearly the speed of light along the wire (because the field propagates at c), whereas the charge carriers drift only at ~mm/s.

Topic 4 — Internal Resistance and Lost Volts

4a. Typical Question Stem

"A cell of EMF 6.0 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω is connected to an external resistor of 5.5 Ω. Explain why the potential difference across the external resistor is less than 6.0 V. Include in your answer the terms 'EMF', 'internal resistance', and 'lost volts'. (6 marks)"

Variants: ask why a voltmeter across an isolated cell reads 6.0 V but reads less when current flows; ask how to determine r experimentally.

4b. Level Descriptor Breakdown

Level 1 (1–2 marks) — Basic:

  • States that "the cell has resistance inside" or "some voltage is lost".
  • No equation; no use of all three required terms.

Level 2 (3–4 marks) — Clear:

  • Defines EMF as total energy per coulomb.
  • States that internal resistance dissipates some energy inside the cell.
  • Quotes V = ε − Ir.
  • Missing: explicit "lost volts = Ir" terminology, or the open-circuit explanation, or the energy-conservation framing.

Level 3 (5–6 marks) — Detailed and Accurate:

  • Defines EMF (total energy per coulomb from the cell).
  • Identifies internal resistance r as the cause of energy dissipation inside the cell.
  • States V = ε − Ir and identifies Ir as the "lost volts".
  • Explains that terminal p.d. V < ε when current flows.
  • Explains why a voltmeter reads ε when no current flows (Ir = 0).
  • Uses energy-conservation framing: energy per coulomb supplied = energy per coulomb dissipated in r + energy per coulomb dissipated in external R.

4c. Model L3 Answer (connectives bolded)

The EMF (ε) of the cell is the total electrical energy per coulomb supplied by the chemical reactions in the cell — here 6.0 V means 6.0 J per coulomb. However, a real cell is not ideal: it has internal resistance r (= 0.50 Ω) due to the resistance of its electrolyte and electrodes. When a current I flows, some of the energy per coulomb is dissipated inside the cell itself as heat in the internal resistance. This energy loss per coulomb is called the "lost volts" and is equal to Ir. Therefore, the terminal potential difference V — the energy per coulomb actually delivered to the external circuit — is given by V = ε − Ir. Because current is flowing (I = ε/(r + R) = 6.0/6.0 = 1.0 A), the lost volts are Ir = 1.0 × 0.50 = 0.50 V, so the terminal p.d. is V = 6.0 − 0.50 = 5.5 V, which is less than the EMF. In contrast, when no current flows (e.g. an open circuit, or a voltmeter of very high resistance connected across the cell), Ir = 0, therefore V = ε and the voltmeter reads the full EMF. This is consistent with energy conservation: the energy per coulomb supplied by the cell equals the energy per coulomb dissipated in the internal resistance plus the energy per coulomb dissipated in the external circuit.

Topic 5 — Comparing Series and Parallel Circuits

5a. Typical Question Stem

"Compare the current, potential difference, and total resistance in a series circuit with those in a parallel circuit, each containing two resistors R₁ and R₂. (6 marks)"

Variants: ask to compare only current and p.d.; ask to derive the parallel formula; ask which combination gives the larger total resistance for given R₁, R₂.

5b. Level Descriptor Breakdown

Level 1 (1–2 marks) — Basic:

  • States one correct point for each circuit (e.g. "series adds them up; parallel is harder to calculate").
  • No equations; no comparison language.

Level 2 (3–4 marks) — Clear:

  • States that in series the current is the same and the p.d. splits.
  • States that in parallel the p.d. is the same and the current splits.
  • Gives the series formula R_total = R₁ + R₂.
  • Missing: parallel formula, or the "less than smallest" point, or the explicit comparison language ("whereas", "in contrast").

Level 3 (5–6 marks) — Detailed and Accurate:

  • Compares current: same in series, splits in parallel.
  • Compares p.d.: splits in series, same in parallel.
  • Gives both formulas: R_total = R₁ + R₂ (series); 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ (parallel).
  • States that in parallel R_total < smallest individual resistor.
  • Uses explicit comparison language throughout ("whereas", "in contrast", "on the other hand").

5c. Model L3 Answer (connectives bolded)

In a series circuit, the same current flows through every component, whereas in a parallel circuit the current splits between the branches according to each branch's resistance. In contrast to current, the potential difference in a series circuit splits across the components (V = V₁ + V₂), whereas in a parallel circuit the same p.d. is applied across every branch. For the total resistance, in series the resistances simply add: R_total = R₁ + R₂, so the combined resistance is always greater than either resistor alone. However, in parallel the reciprocal resistances add: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂, therefore R_total = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂). Consequently, the total parallel resistance is always less than the smallest individual resistor, because adding another branch provides an additional path for current, reducing the overall opposition to flow. As a result, two 6 Ω resistors in series give 12 Ω, whereas two 6 Ω resistors in parallel give 3 Ω — a factor of 4 difference for the same components.

Topic 6 — Explaining the Potential Divider

6a. Typical Question Stem

"A potential divider consists of two resistors R₁ = 4.0 kΩ and R₂ = 6.0 kΩ connected in series across a 9.0 V battery of negligible internal resistance. (a) Calculate the p.d. across R₂. Variants: replace LDR with NTC thermistor; ask for both V_out across R₁ and across R₂; ask to design a circuit that switches on a heater when temperature falls.

6b. Level Descriptor Breakdown

Level 1 (1–2 marks) — Basic:

  • States that "the LDR's resistance changes" without direction.
  • May give the formula V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁+R₂).
  • No mechanism; no application.

Level 2 (3–4 marks) — Clear:

  • States that LDR resistance decreases in bright light.
  • Links this to V_out changing.
  • May give a correct application (light switch, burglar alarm).
  • Missing: mechanism (more charge carriers), or correct direction of V_out change, or the formula.

Level 3 (5–6 marks) — Detailed and Accurate:

  • States the formula V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁+R₂) and identifies R₂ as the LDR.
  • Explains the LDR mechanism: more light → more charge carriers released (semiconductor) → R_LDR decreases.
  • Uses the formula to show that if R₂ (= LDR) decreases, V_out decreases.
  • States the application (e.g. light-activated switch, street lamp controller) and explains which way V_out moves.
  • Optionally: distinguishes V_out across the LDR vs V_out across the fixed resistor (one increases, the other decreases).

6c. Model L3 Answer (connectives bolded)

The output voltage of a potential divider is given by V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁ + R₂), where R₂ is the resistor across which V_out is measured. When R₂ is replaced by an LDR (a light-dependent resistor made from a semiconductor), the LDR's resistance decreases as the light intensity increases, because photons of light release more charge carriers (electrons) into the conduction band, increasing the number density n and reducing R = ρL/A. Substituting this into the potential divider formula: as R₂ (= LDR) decreases, the fraction R₂/(R₁ + R₂) decreases, therefore V_out across the LDR decreases as the light intensity increases. Consequently, the voltage across the fixed resistor R₁ increases by the same amount (since V_in is constant and V₁ + V₂ = V_in). A common application is a light-activated switch: when V_out across the LDR falls below a threshold, a transistor switches on a street lamp at dusk. In contrast, if V_out is taken across R₁ instead, V_out increases with light intensity — useful for a burglar alarm that triggers when a beam of light is broken (V_out rises when light is present).

Topic 7 — Bonus: Why Voltmeter Across Cell with No Current Reads EMF

This is a sub-topic of internal resistance but appears as a standalone 6-mark question often enough to deserve its own template.

7a. Typical Question Stem

"A student connects a high-resistance voltmeter across a cell and notes the reading. The student then connects the cell to a lamp and notes that the voltmeter reading drops. Explain these observations. (6 marks)"

7b. Model L3 Answer (connectives bolded)

When the voltmeter is connected across the isolated cell, its very high resistance means that the current flowing through the circuit is negligible (I ≈ 0). Because the lost volts are Ir and I ≈ 0, the lost volts are effectively zero, therefore the terminal p.d. V = ε − Ir ≈ ε. Consequently, the voltmeter reads the EMF of the cell directly. However, when the lamp is connected in parallel with the voltmeter, the total external resistance drops sharply, so a significant current I now flows from the cell. As a result, the lost volts Ir are now non-zero, and the terminal p.d. V = ε − Ir is now less than the EMF. This is why the voltmeter reading drops: the same EMF is being shared between the internal resistance and the lamp. In an ideal cell with r = 0, there would be no lost volts and the voltmeter would read ε in both cases, whereas a real cell always shows this drop when current flows.

Common Pitfalls

Unit conversion — mm² to m² (multiple papers): The most common calculation error in Ch 2.4. If diameter is given in mm, convert to m before calculating area: d(m) = d(mm) × 10⁻³, then A = πd²/4. Using mm² directly gives an answer off by a factor of 10⁶.

Diameter vs radius in A = πr² (multiple papers): The MS for Oct 2022 Q13b(i) explicitly does NOT award MP1 if diameter is used directly. Always halve the diameter first. For Jun 2019 Q11, the diameter is 0.36 mm — radius = 0.18 mm = 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ m.

Non-linear components and resistivity (Jun 2023 Q16b(ii), Jun 2024 Q14d): When a filament lamp is in a resistivity context, R is not constant — it changes with operating point. 1. How QWC Is Tested in WPH12

1.1 The Spec Requirement

The Pearson Edexcel IAL Physics specification states that every WPH12/01 paper must include at least one 6-mark QWC question. In practice, examiners typically include 1–2 such questions per paper, worth 6–12 marks total out of the 80-mark paper (7.5–15%).

1.2 Where QWC Questions Appear

QWC questions in WPH12 appear in Section B (the long-answer section), typically as the final sub-question of a multi-part circuits question (often Q14, Q15, or Q16). They are flagged in the mark scheme with the annotation "QWC" and are level-marked (L1/L2/L3) rather than point-marked.

2. Mark Scheme Structure for QWC Questions

2.1 Indicative Content (IC) Points

Every QWC mark scheme lists 6 Indicative Content (IC) points. Each IC point corresponds to 1 mark of scientific content. The IC points are NOT awarded one-by-one — they are used as a checklist to decide the LEVEL of the answer.

A typical mark scheme reads:

Indicative content: IC1 — Lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude at higher T IC2 — More frequent electron–ion collisions IC3 — Mean free time decreases IC4 — Drift velocity decreases IC5 — Current decreases (at constant V) IC6 — R = V/I increases

2.2 Level Descriptors (Generic for WPH12 QWC)

LevelMarksDescriptor
L11–2Information is basic; isolated points; little or no linkage; limited use of technical vocabulary. QWC: weak — may be a list.
L23–4Information is clear; some linkage between points; technical vocabulary mostly correct; explanation may be incomplete. QWC: adequate — some connectives used.
L35–6Information is detailed, accurate and sustained; full chain of reasoning; technical vocabulary accurate throughout. QWC: high — connectives link every step.

2.3 The Linkage / QWC Boost

The QWC element is judged holistically. A student with all 6 IC points but no connectives scores L2 (4 marks). A student with 5 IC points but excellent linkage scores L3 (5–6 marks). Connectives are the visible signal of QWC.

The mark scheme typically includes a phrase such as:

"For L3, the answer must show a sustained chain of reasoning using connective words such as 'therefore', 'because', 'consequently', 'as a result'."

2.4 Marking Decision Tree

1. Read the whole answer.
2. Count the IC points present (0 to 6).
3. Judge the linkage / QWC.
   ├── 5–6 IC points + good linkage → L3 (5–6 marks)
   ├── 5–6 IC points + weak linkage → L2 (4 marks)
   ├── 3–4 IC points + good linkage → L2 (3–4 marks)
   ├── 3–4 IC points + weak linkage → L1–L2 (2–3 marks)
   └── 1–2 IC points → L1 (1–2 marks)

Implication: The fastest way to move from L2 to L3 is to ADD CONNECTIVES, not to add more facts. Always rewrite your answer with "therefore" / "because" / "consequently" between every step.

Practice Question 1 — Metal R-T (Standard)

Question: A student measures the current through a length of nichrome wire while keeping the p.d. across it constant at 4.0 V. The student observes that the current decreases over time as the wire heats up. Explain, in terms of the motion of conduction electrons and lattice ions, why the current decreases. (6 marks)

Model L3 answer:

As the wire heats up, the lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude about their equilibrium positions because thermal energy increases their kinetic energy. Consequently, conduction electrons collide more frequently with the vibrating lattice ions, so the mean free time between collisions decreases. Since drift velocity v = qEτ/mₑ (where τ is the mean free time), the reduced τ means the drift velocity of the electrons decreases. As a result, fewer electrons pass a given cross-section per second, therefore the current I = nAvq decreases. Because the p.d. is held constant at 4.0 V and the current has fallen, the resistance R = V/I has increased — explaining why the ammeter reading drops over time.

IC breakdown:

ICPoint
1Lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude
2More frequent electron–ion collisions
3Mean free time τ decreases
4Drift velocity decreases (v ∝ τ)
5Current I = nAvq decreases
6R = V/I increases (at constant V)

Common L1/L2 errors: Saying "atoms" not "lattice ions"; skipping mean free time; not linking to R = V/I.

Practice Question 2 — Metal R-T (Calculation + Explain Hybrid)

Question: A copper wire of cross-sectional area 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m² carries a current of 3.0 A at 20 °C; the drift velocity is 2.2 × 10⁻⁴ m/s. When the wire is heated to 80 °C at the same p.d., the drift velocity falls to 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m/s. Explain why the drift velocity decreases when the wire is heated, and calculate the percentage increase in the resistance of the wire. (6 marks)

Model L3 answer:

When the wire is heated, the lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude because their thermal kinetic energy increases. Consequently, conduction electrons collide more frequently with the lattice ions, so the mean free time τ between collisions decreases. Since v = qEτ/mₑ, the reduced τ reduces the drift velocity for a given electric field. At the same p.d., the reduced drift velocity means a reduced current (I = nAvq), therefore R = V/I has increased. From the ratio of drift velocities: v₂/v₁ = 1.5/2.2 = 0.68, so I₂/I₁ = 0.68 (since n, A, q are constant). Therefore R₂/R₁ = V/I₂ ÷ V/I₁ = I₁/I₂ = 2.2/1.5 = 1.47, giving a 47% increase in resistance.

IC breakdown:

ICPoint
1Lattice ions vibrate more at higher T
2More frequent collisions reduce τ
3v = qEτ/mₑ → v decreases
4At same V, lower v means lower I (I = nAvq)
5R = V/I increases

Common L1/L2 errors: Forgetting to mention mean free time; not linking drift velocity change to R change; calculation error from inverting the ratio.

Practice Question 3 — NTC Thermistor R-T (Standard)

Question: An NTC thermistor is connected in series with a 6.0 V battery and an ammeter. The ammeter reading increases when the thermistor is warmed. Explain why the resistance of the thermistor decreases as its temperature rises. (6 marks)

Model L3 answer:

An NTC thermistor is made from a semiconductor material. When it is heated, thermal energy is absorbed by the lattice and this promotes electrons from the valence band into the conduction band, releasing more charge carriers. Consequently, the number density n of free charge carriers increases sharply with temperature. Because I = nAvq, the increase in n means the current I increases (at constant V), even though the drift velocity per carrier may fall slightly. However, the increased temperature also causes greater lattice vibration, which would tend to reduce drift velocity and increase resistance — as in a metal. In contrast to a metal, in a semiconductor the increase in charge carrier number density outweighs the lattice vibration effect, so the net effect is that I increases. Therefore, since R = V/I and V is constant while I has increased, the resistance of the NTC thermistor decreases with increasing temperature.

IC breakdown:

ICPoint
1Thermistor is a semiconductor
2Thermal energy releases more charge carriers
3Number density n increases
4I = nAvq → current increases
5Charge carrier effect dominates over lattice vibration
6R = V/I decreases

Common L1/L2 errors: Not saying "semiconductor"; not mentioning competing lattice-vibration effect; confusing direction of n change.

Practice Question 4 — NTC Thermistor (Comparison with Metal)

Question: A student has a copper wire and an NTC thermistor. Both are heated from 20 °C to 80 °C while the p.d. across each is held constant. The current through the copper wire decreases; the current through the thermistor increases. Compare the mechanisms that lead to these opposite observations. (6 marks)

Model L3 answer:

In the copper wire (a metal), the number density n of conduction electrons is essentially constant with temperature. When the wire is heated, the lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude, so electrons collide more frequently and the mean free time τ decreases. Consequently, the drift velocity decreases and the current I = nAvq falls (at constant V), therefore the resistance R = V/I increases. In contrast, in the NTC thermistor (a semiconductor), n is NOT constant: heating promotes electrons from the valence band to the conduction band, so n increases sharply. Although the lattice vibration also increases (which alone would reduce drift velocity), the increase in n dominates, and the current I = nAvq rises (at constant V). Therefore, R = V/I decreases for the thermistor. The key difference is that in a metal n is constant and only τ changes, whereas in a semiconductor n changes and this dominates the behaviour.

IC breakdown:

ICPoint
1Metal: n is constant; only lattice vibration changes
2Metal: τ decreases, v decreases, I decreases, R increases
3Semiconductor: n increases with T (charge carriers released)
4Semiconductor: I increases (n effect dominates)
5Semiconductor: R = V/I decreases
6Comparison: "whereas" / "in contrast" links the two cases

Common L1/L2 errors: Describing only one material; not using comparison connectives; saying "n changes in metals".

Practice Question 5 — Drift Velocity (Standard)

Question: A current of 2.0 A flows in an aluminium wire of cross-sectional area 8.0 × 10⁻⁷ m². The number density of conduction electrons in aluminium is 6.0 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. Calculate the drift velocity of the electrons, and explain why this drift velocity is so small even though the current is large. (6 marks)

Model L3 answer:

The current is given by I = nAvq, where n is the number density of charge carriers, A is the cross-sectional area, v is the drift velocity, and q = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C is the charge per electron. Rearranging, v = I/(nAq) = 2.0 / (6.0 × 10²⁸ × 8.0 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴ m/s (about 0.3 mm/s). This drift velocity is very small because n is enormous (~10²⁸ m⁻³), so even a tiny drift of each carrier produces a large current when multiplied by n, A, and q. Furthermore, q is a fixed fundamental constant and A is limited by the wire's physical size, therefore v must be small to give a reasonable current. The reason the electrons drift so slowly is that they are in constant random thermal motion at high speed (~10⁵ m/s) but collide frequently with lattice ions; each collision randomises their velocity, so the electric field only imposes a tiny net drift on top of the random motion. However, the electrical signal itself travels at nearly the speed of light along the wire (because the field propagates at c), whereas the charge carriers drift only at ~mm/s.

IC breakdown:

Practice Question 6 — Drift Velocity (Comparison Wire)

Question: Two copper wires A and B carry the same current. Wire A has twice the cross-sectional area of wire B. Compare the drift velocities of electrons in the two wires, and explain why connecting two wires of different thicknesses affects the drift velocity. (6 marks)

Model L3 answer:

The current is given by I = nAvq, where all symbols have their usual meanings. For two copper wires of the same material at the same temperature, n and q are the same. Rearranging for drift velocity: v = I/(nAq). If both wires carry the same current I, then the wire with the larger cross-sectional area A will have a smaller drift velocity, because the same current is shared over more charge carriers per unit length. Specifically, if wire A has twice the area of wire B (A_A = 2 A_B), then v_A = v_B / 2 — the drift velocity in wire A is half that in wire B. This is because the larger area means more charge carriers are available to carry the same current, so each carrier drifts more slowly. The current itself is the same in both wires (series connection), whereas the drift velocity adjusts to compensate for the change in area. Consequently, thicker wires can carry the same current with a slower drift velocity, which is why high-current cables are made thick — to reduce the drift velocity and hence the resistance heating.

IC breakdown:

ICPoint
1States I = nAvq
2For same material, n and q are the same
3v = I/(nAq) — v inversely proportional to A
4Wire A (2× area) has half the drift velocity
5Explains: more carriers per unit length, each drifts less
6Application: thicker wires for high current (less drift, less heat)

Common L1/L2 errors: Saying v is the same in both (wrong — v differs if A differs); saying v doubles in the thicker wire (inverted); not linking to real-world application.

Practice Question 7 — Internal Resistance (Standard)

Question: A cell of EMF 9.0 V and internal resistance 0.80 Ω is connected to a 4.2 Ω external resistor. Explain why the potential difference across the external resistor is less than 9.0 V. Use the terms 'EMF', 'internal resistance', and 'lost volts' in your answer. (6 marks)

Model L3 answer:

The EMF (ε) of the cell is the total electrical energy per coulomb supplied by the chemical reactions in the cell — 9.0 V means 9.0 J per coulomb. However, a real cell is not ideal: it has internal resistance r (= 0.80 Ω) due to the resistance of its electrolyte and electrodes. When a current I flows, some of the energy per coulomb is dissipated inside the cell itself as heat in the internal resistance. This energy loss per coulomb is called the "lost volts" and is equal to Ir. Therefore, the terminal potential difference V — the energy per coulomb actually delivered to the external circuit — is given by V = ε − Ir. Because current is flowing (I = ε/(r + R) = 9.0/5.0 = 1.8 A), the lost volts are Ir = 1.8 × 0.80 = 1.44 V, so the terminal p.d. is V = 9.0 − 1.44 = 7.56 V, which is less than the EMF. This is consistent with energy conservation: the energy per coulomb supplied by the cell equals the energy per coulomb dissipated in the internal resistance plus the energy per coulomb dissipated in the external resistor (ε = Ir + IR).

IC breakdown:

ICPoint
1EMF = total energy per coulomb supplied by the cell
2Real cell has internal resistance r
3Lost volts = Ir (energy dissipated inside cell)
4V = ε − Ir < ε when current flows
5Numerical: I = 1.8 A, lost volts = 1.44 V, V = 7.56 V
6Energy conservation: ε = Ir + IR

Common L1/L2 errors: Not using all three required terms; not writing V = ε − Ir; not computing the numerical current.

Practice Question 8 — Internal Resistance (Voltmeter Behaviour)

Question: A high-resistance voltmeter connected across an isolated 12 V battery reads 12.0 V. When a 10 Ω resistor is connected across the battery in parallel with the voltmeter, the voltmeter reads 11.2 V. Explain these observations and determine the internal resistance of the battery. (6 marks)

Model L3 answer:

When the voltmeter is connected across the isolated battery, its very high resistance means the current drawn is negligible (I ≈ 0). Because the lost volts are Ir and I ≈ 0, the lost volts are effectively zero, therefore the terminal p.d. V = ε − Ir ≈ ε = 12.0 V. Consequently, the voltmeter reads the EMF directly. However, when the 10 Ω resistor is connected in parallel, the total external resistance drops sharply, so a significant current I now flows. As a result, the lost volts Ir are now non-zero, and the terminal p.d. V = ε − Ir is now 11.2 V, less than the EMF. The lost volts are ε − V = 12.0 − 11.2 = 0.8 V. The current through the external resistor is I = V/R = 11.2 / 10 = 1.12 A. Therefore, the internal resistance r = lost volts / I = 0.8 / 1.12 = 0.71 Ω. In an ideal cell with r = 0, there would be no lost volts and the voltmeter would read ε in both cases, whereas this real cell shows the drop because r ≠ 0.

IC breakdown:

ICPoint
1Voltmeter high resistance → I ≈ 0 when isolated
2Lost volts Ir ≈ 0, so V = ε
3Connecting resistor drops external R → I increases
4Lost volts now non-zero → V = 11.2 V < ε
5Lost volts = 0.8 V; I = 1.12 A; r = 0.71 Ω
6Contrasts real (r ≠ 0) vs ideal (r = 0) cell

Common L1/L2 errors: Not explaining the open-circuit case; not computing r; using wrong V or I in r = lost volts/I.

Practice Question 9 — Series vs Parallel Comparison

Question: A student has two 12 Ω resistors. Compare the total resistance, current, and potential difference when the two resistors are connected (a) in series, and (b) in parallel, across a 6.0 V battery of negligible internal resistance. (6 marks)

Model L3 answer:

In series, the same current flows through every component, whereas in parallel the current splits between the branches. For total resistance: in series the resistances add, R_total = R₁ + R₂ = 12 + 12 = 24 Ω, so the combined resistance is always greater than either resistor alone. However, in parallel the reciprocal resistances add, 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ = 1/12 + 1/12 = 1/6, therefore R_total = 6 Ω, which is less than the smallest individual resistor (12 Ω), because adding another branch provides an additional path for current. For the current drawn from the 6.0 V battery: in series, I = V/R_total = 6.0/24 = 0.25 A; in parallel, I = V/R_total = 6.0/6 = 1.0 A. Consequently, the parallel combination draws four times the current of the series combination from the same battery. For the p.d.: in series the p.d. splits (6.0 V across the combination splits as 3.0 V + 3.0 V across each resistor), whereas in parallel the same 6.0 V is across each branch.

IC breakdown:

ICPoint
1Series: same current; parallel: current splits
2Series R_total = 24 Ω (R₁ + R₂)
3Parallel 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ → R_total = 6 Ω
4Parallel R_total < smallest individual R
5Series I = 0.25 A; parallel I = 1.0 A (4× more)
6P.d. in series splits (3 V + 3 V); in parallel, 6 V across each

Common L1/L2 errors: Not computing the numerical currents; not stating "R_parallel < smallest"; using "and" instead of "whereas".

Practice Question 10 — Potential Divider with LDR

Question: A potential divider consists of a fixed 5.0 kΩ resistor R₁ in series with an LDR (R₂), connected across a 9.0 V battery of negligible internal resistance. The output voltage V_out is taken across the LDR. Explain how V_out changes as the light intensity increases, and describe one practical application of this circuit. (6 marks)

Model L3 answer:

The output voltage of a potential divider is given by V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁ + R₂), where R₂ is the LDR (the resistor across which V_out is measured). When the light intensity increases, the LDR's resistance decreases, because photons of light release more charge carriers into the conduction band of the semiconductor material (cadmium sulfide), increasing the number density n and reducing R = ρL/A. Substituting this into the formula: as R₂ (= LDR) decreases, the fraction R₂/(R₁ + R₂) decreases, therefore V_out across the LDR decreases. Consequently, the voltage across the fixed resistor R₁ increases by the same amount (since V₁ + V₂ = V_in = 9.0 V is constant). A practical application is a light-activated switch for street lamps: at dusk, the light intensity falls, the LDR's resistance rises, V_out across the LDR rises above a threshold, and a transistor switches on the street lamp. In contrast, if V_out were taken across R₁ instead, V_out would rise with light intensity — useful for a burglar alarm that triggers when a light beam is broken.

IC breakdown:

ICPoint
1States potential divider formula V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁+R₂)
2LDR is a semiconductor; R decreases with light intensity
3Mechanism: more light → more charge carriers → n increases → R decreases
4R₂ decreasing → V_out across LDR decreases
5V across R₁ increases by the same amount (V₁ + V₂ = V_in)
6Application: street lamp / burglar alarm with correct V_out direction

Common L1/L2 errors: Not writing the formula; saying "LDR R increases with light" (wrong direction); not stating an application; confusing which V_out direction.

Full-Mark Answer Templates (Common CP7/CP8 Question Types)

CP7 Type A — Calculate ρ from given l, d, V, I (3 marks): (1) Convert diameter to metres and calculate A = π(d/2)². (2) Use R = V/I (if V, I given), then substitute into ρ = RA/l. (3) State final value with units and appropriate significant figures.

CP7 Type B — "Show that" cross-sectional area from diameter (2 marks): (1) Use A = πr² (with r = d/2) — must halve diameter first. (2) Correct numerical answer within tolerance. Critical insight: MS does NOT award MP1 if A = πd² is used.

CP7 Type C — Describe the graphical method for determining ρ (3 marks): (1) State chosen axes and what is held constant (A constant for wire; l varied). (2) Identify what gradient equals in terms of ρ and A. (3) State ρ = gradient × A (or equivalent rearrangement). Acceptable axes: R vs l (gradient = ρ/A); R vs l/A (gradient = ρ); RA vs l (gradient = ρ); l vs R (gradient = A/ρ); RA vs 1/ρ (gradient = l); l/A vs R (gradient = 1/ρ); V vs Il (gradient = ρ/A).

CP7 Type D — Describe full procedure to determine resistivity (4 marks): (1) Measure length L with a metre rule. (2) Measure thickness/diameter with micrometer or vernier callipers — repeat at multiple positions and average. (3) Measure R using ohmmeter, OR measure V (voltmeter in parallel) and I (ammeter in series) and use R = V/I. (4) Use ρ = RA/l with appropriate area formula (A = πr² for wire; A = width × thickness for sheet).

CP7 Type E — Deduce material/temperature from a ρ table (3 marks): (1) Use R = V/I to find resistance. (2) Use R = ρl/A to find resistivity. (3) Compare calculated ρ to table values and identify material/temperature (with interpolation if needed).

CP8 Type A — Determine ε and r from a V-I graph (3 marks): (1) Read y-intercept (= ε). (2) Calculate gradient using two well-separated points on the line. (3) State r = |gradient| with units and significant figures.

CP8 Type B — Draw circuit diagram for ε/r investigation (2–3 marks): Required: (1) Cell/battery. (2) Ammeter in series. (3) Variable resistor (or potential divider) in series. (4) Voltmeter in parallel across cell ONLY. (5) Switch (optional but preferred).

CP8 Type C — Explain how ε and r are determined from V-I graph (4 marks): (1) Use conservation of energy (Kirchhoff's 2nd law) to obtain ε = V + Ir. (2) Rearrange to V = ε − Ir, compare with y = mx + c. (3) y-intercept = ε. (4) Gradient = −r (so r = |gradient|).

CP8 Type D — Calculate r from single V, I, R data (3 marks): (1) Use V = IR with V = terminal p.d. to find I. (2) Calculate "lost volts" = ε − V. (3) Use V_lost = Ir to find r = V_lost/I.

CP8 Type E — Cells in series vs parallel: power comparison (5 marks): (1) Series: ε_total = 2ε, r_total = 2r. (2) Parallel: ε_total = ε (unchanged), r_total = r/2. (3) Calculate both powers using P = I²R. (4) Calculate ratio. (5) State clear judgement.

CP8 Type F — Explain why a real-world battery gets hot (4 marks): (1) Stimulus: more power required. (2) P = IV → I increases. (3) V_internal = Ir increases. (4) P_internal = I²r increases → battery gets hot.

CP8 Type G — QWC: LDR + terminal p.d. causal chain (6 marks): IC1: more photons per second on LDR. IC2: more conduction electrons released. IC3: R_LDR decreases. IC4: r (internal) is constant. IC5: total R decreases → I increases. IC6: lost volts Ir increases → terminal p.d. V = ε − Ir decreases → voltmeter reading decreases. Use connectives ("because", "so", "therefore") to link all six points.

Quick-Reference Revision Checklist

CP7 — Resistivity Practical:

  • Memorise ρ = RA/l and rearrangements R = ρl/A, A = ρl/R, l = RA/ρ
  • Know area formulas: A = πr² = πd²/4 for wires; A = width × thickness for sheets/foils
  • Know the 3 instruments: metre rule (length), micrometer or vernier callipers (diameter/thickness), ohmmeter OR ammeter+voltmeter (resistance)
  • Know 3 valid graphical methods: R vs l (gradient = ρ/A), R vs l/A (gradient = ρ), RA vs l (gradient = ρ)
  • Know typical ρ values: copper 1.7 × 10⁻⁸, nichrome 1.1 × 10⁻⁶, tungsten (hot) ~10⁻⁷, semiconductors ~10⁻³ to 10³ Ω m
  • Practise mm → m (×10⁻³), mm² → m² (×10⁻⁶), mΩ → Ω (×10⁻³) conversions
  • Always halve diameter before squaring (or use A = πd²/4)
  • For inverted direction (find d from ρ, R, l): use d = 2√(A/π) — remember the factor of 2
  • For uncertainty: %unc(ρ) = %unc(R) + 2 × %unc(d) + %unc(l) — remember to double the diameter term

CP8 — E.M.F. and Internal Resistance Practical:

  • Memorise ε = V + Ir and rearrangements V = ε − Ir, r = (ε − V)/I, r = R_ext × (ε − V)/V
  • Know the standard circuit: cell + ammeter (series) + variable resistor (series) + voltmeter (parallel across cell) + switch
  • V = ε − Ir in y = mx + c form: y-intercept = ε, gradient = −r (so r = |gradient|)
  • Alternative graph: R vs 1/I gives gradient = ε, intercept = −r (negative; r = |intercept|)
  • Practise "lost volts" = ε − V; do NOT use ε in V = IR for the external circuit
  • Cells combine: series → ε_total = Nε, r_total = Nr; parallel (identical) → ε_total = ε, r_total = r/N
  • Maximum current = ε/r (when R_external → 0, short circuit)
  • For QWC: always use connectives and follow the chain stimulus → mechanism → electrical consequence → result
  • Power dissipated in internal resistance: P_int = I²r (NOT I²R — MS forbids R for Jan 2023 Q17c)
