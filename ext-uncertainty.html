Uncertainty in Waves & Electricity Practicals

Definitions

0. Recap of Generic Terms

The general uncertainty vocabulary — absolute / fractional / percentage uncertainty, random / systematic / zero / parallax error, resolution / precision / accuracy / repeatability / reproducibility, uncertainty in a mean / sum / difference / product / quotient / power / gradient / intercept, anomaly, significant figures, standard form, prefix conversions, line of best fit / worst acceptable line / error bar, control / independent / dependent variable — is defined in full in the WPH11 extension file. You must know all of these for WPH12. The three combination rules are summarised here because every WPH12 calculation depends on them:

  • Addition / subtraction → add ABSOLUTE uncertainties
  • Multiplication / division → add PERCENTAGE uncertainties
  • Powers → multiply PERCENTAGE uncertainty by the power

The WPH12 file below covers only terms and contexts that the WPH11 version does not address.

1. WPH12-Specific Instrument Uncertainties

1.1 Oscilloscope Time-Base Uncertainty

Spec ref: Appendix 10 + CP4 (speed of sound using oscilloscope)

Definition: The uncertainty in a time interval read from an oscilloscope, equal to ± the smallest division of the time-base setting when the trace is read to the nearest division (or ± half the smallest division when interpolated by eye). For a time-base set to 0.1 ms/div, a one-division reading has uncertainty ±0.1 ms (one division) or ±0.05 ms (interpolated). The time-base itself is calibrated by the oscilloscope; the dominant uncertainty is the visual reading of where the trace crosses the graticule.

WPH12 Exam context: CP4 — the time delay between two microphone pulses on a dual-beam oscilloscope is read from the horizontal separation of the traces. Examiners expect the student to state the time-base setting (e.g. "0.1 ms/div") and convert the number of divisions to time with its ± uncertainty. Examiner reports (Jan 2023, Oct 2024) show the most common error is quoting only the time without the time-base uncertainty, or quoting the uncertainty in divisions instead of in ms.

1.2 Frequency Measurement Uncertainty (Signal Generator)

Spec ref: Appendix 10 + CP5 (vibrating string / sonometer)

Definition: The uncertainty in the frequency delivered by a signal generator as read from its digital or analogue display. A digital signal generator read to 1 Hz has an absolute uncertainty of ±1 Hz (the resolution); an analogue dial read to the nearest 10 Hz has an absolute uncertainty of ±5 Hz (half the smallest division). The display reading is the resolution-limited uncertainty; the actual frequency delivered to the vibrator may also drift, but the exam treats the display reading as the dominant uncertainty.

WPH12 Exam context: CP5 — the student varies the frequency f of a signal generator driving a vibrating string and measures the length L of the first-harmonic loop. %f = (1 Hz / f) × 100. At low f (e.g. 50 Hz), %f is large (2%); at high f (e.g. 500 Hz), %f is small (0.2%). Examiner reports (Oct 2022) show students often forget to convert from Hz to percentage uncertainty when combining with %L in f = (1/2L)√(T/μ).

1.3 Protractor / Angle Measurement Uncertainty

Spec ref: Appendix 10 + CP6 (diffraction by a grating)

Definition: The absolute uncertainty in an angle measured with a standard protractor. A protractor graduated every 1° has a reading uncertainty of ±0.5° (analogue, half the smallest division). If the angle is measured by the difference of two protractor readings (e.g. θ = θ₁ − θ₂, the diffracted ray angle minus the straight-through ray angle), the absolute uncertainty DOUBLES to ±1° because absolute uncertainties add for subtraction.

WPH12 Exam context: CP6 — the angle θ to the n-th order diffraction maximum is measured with a protractor. Examiner reports (Jan 2024) show the critical error is treating the angle uncertainty as a percentage uncertainty directly: students write "%sin θ = %θ" — this is WRONG. The correct conversion is given in §2.2 below. The protractor uncertainty is small in degrees but can become significant when propagated through sin θ.

1.4 Micrometer Uncertainty (WPH12 — Resistivity)

Spec ref: Appendix 10 + CP7 (resistivity of a wire)

Definition: The absolute uncertainty in a wire diameter measured with a micrometer screw gauge. A standard micrometer has a resolution of 0.01 mm, so a single reading has an absolute uncertainty of ±0.01 mm. When repeat readings are taken at three points along the wire and in two perpendicular orientations (to allow for non-circular cross-section), the absolute uncertainty in the mean diameter is ½ × range of the repeats. A zero error (micrometer reading when the anvil and spindle are closed) is a systematic error and must be subtracted from every reading before the uncertainty is calculated.

WPH12 Exam context: CP7 — this is the most important single instrument uncertainty in WPH12. The diameter d is squared in A = πd²/4, so %A = 2 × %d (the doubling rule, §2.1). A ±0.01 mm uncertainty on a 0.40 mm wire is %d = 2.5%, so %A = 5%. This dominates the resistivity uncertainty. Examiner reports (Oct 2023, Jan 2025) show students routinely forget to double the diameter percentage when propagating to area — this is the single most penalised uncertainty error in WPH12.

1.5 Ammeter and Voltmeter Uncertainty

Spec ref: Appendix 10 + CP7 (resistivity) + CP8 (EMF/internal resistance)

Definition: The absolute uncertainty in a current or voltage reading from a digital multimeter. A school digital ammeter reading to 0.01 A has an absolute uncertainty of ±0.01 A (the resolution); a digital voltmeter reading to 0.01 V has ±0.01 V. Analogue meters have ± half the smallest scale division. The percentage uncertainty in R = V/I is therefore %V + %I (the multiply/divide rule). For internal resistance work (CP8), the voltmeter resistance is finite (not infinite) so a small current flows through it — this is a systematic, not random, error and is not captured by the ± uncertainty on a single reading.

WPH12 Exam context: CP7 — R is read from a voltmeter/ammeter pair as V/I; the %R feeds directly into %ρ = %R + %A + %L. CP8 — the V and I readings form the V–I graph from which the gradient (–r) and intercept (ε) are extracted, and their scatter determines the worst-acceptable-line spread. Examiner reports (Jan 2022) note that students often forget the %R contribution when calculating %ρ, treating the resistance as exact.

1.6 Metre Rule Uncertainty (WPH12 — CP4, CP5, CP7)

Spec ref: Appendix 10

Definition: A metre rule graduated in mm has a resolution of 1 mm. A single length reading taken by eye against the rule has an absolute uncertainty of ±0.5 mm = ±0.0005 m (analogue, half the smallest division). When the length is the difference of two readings (e.g. wire length between two markers, or microphone separation), the absolute uncertainty DOUBLES to ±1 mm because absolute uncertainties add for subtraction. For a 1.000 m length, %L = 0.1%; for a 0.200 m loop length (CP5), %L = 0.5%.

WPH12 Exam context: Used in CP4 (microphone separation d), CP5 (string loop length L), CP7 (wire length L). The length uncertainty is usually small but not negligible. Examiner reports (Oct 2023) show students forget to double the absolute uncertainty when the length is a difference of two readings.

2. WPH12-Specific Propagation Contexts

2.1 Diameter-to-Area Doubling Rule (CP7 — THE TRAP)

Spec ref: Appendix 10 + CP7 + Appendix 7 (Formulae A = πd²/4)

Definition: Because the cross-sectional area of a wire is A = πd²/4, and d is raised to the power 2, the percentage uncertainty in A is exactly twice the percentage uncertainty in d: %A = 2 × %d. The constants π and 4 have no uncertainty. This is a direct application of the power rule (multiply the percentage uncertainty by the power). It is the WPH12 analogue of the WPH11 t² trap in g = 2s/t².

WPH12 Exam context: Heavily tested in CP7 — every resistivity paper includes a "% uncertainty in ρ" question that requires the doubling. The full propagation is:

  • ρ = R A / L → %ρ = %R + %A + %L
  • Substitute %A = 2 × %d → %ρ = %R + 2 × %d + %L

Examiner reports (Oct 2023, Jan 2024, Oct 2024, Jan 2025) show 50–60% of students forget to double the %d and write %ρ = %R + %d + %L instead. This single error costs 1 mark out of 3 on every CP7 paper.

2.2 Angle-to-Sin Conversion (CP6 — THE TRAP)

Spec ref: Appendix 10 + Appendix 6 (Mathematical skills — use of sin/cos) + CP6

Definition: When an angle θ (with absolute uncertainty Δθ in degrees) is propagated through a function such as sin θ, the uncertainty in sin θ is NOT sin(Δθ) and NOT Δθ treated as a percentage. The correct relationship comes from the calculus of errors: Δ(sin θ) = cos θ × Δθ with Δθ in radians. To use this rule, convert the angle uncertainty from degrees to radians first (Δθ_rad = Δθ_deg × π/180), then multiply by cos θ. The percentage uncertainty is then %sin θ = (Δ(sin θ) / sin θ) × 100.

WPH12 Exam context: CP6 — nλ = d sin θ requires sin θ for the calculation. Examiner reports (Jan 2024) note that the most common error is calculating sin(25.5°) − sin(25°) instead of cos(25°) × (0.5° × π/180). The two give different results because sin is non-linear; the calculus-of-errors approach is the correct one. See the Worked Examples section for a full worked example.

2.3 V–I Graph Gradient Uncertainty (CP8)

Spec ref: Appendix 10 + CP8 (EMF and internal resistance) + Appendix 7 (V = ε − Ir)

Definition: In the equation V = ε − Ir, a plot of V (y-axis) against I (x-axis) gives a straight line with gradient = −r and y-intercept = ε. The absolute uncertainty in r is found by the worst-acceptable-line method: draw the steepest and shallowest lines that pass through all the error bars, calculate their gradients m_max and m_min, then Δr = ½ × |m_max − m_min| (the modulus because r is the magnitude of the gradient). Similarly Δε = ½ × |c_max − c_min| where c is the y-intercept of each worst line.

WPH12 Exam context: CP8 — virtually every CP8 question asks for r ± Δr and ε ± Δε. Examiner reports (Oct 2022, Jan 2023, Oct 2024) show the three common errors: (1) drawing only one worst line, (2) using the full range (|m_max − m_min|) instead of half the range, (3) forgetting that r is the magnitude of the gradient and reporting a negative uncertainty. The negative sign on the gradient tells you the slope direction; r itself is positive.

2.4 Propagation through f = (1/2L)√(T/μ) (CP5)

Spec ref: Appendix 10 + CP5 + Appendix 7 (First harmonic formula)

Definition: For the first harmonic of a stretched string, f = (1/2L) √(T/μ). Breaking this into operations: the term √(T/μ) involves a division (T/μ, so add %T + %μ) then a square root (power ½, so multiply by ½). The factor 1/(2L) involves a division by L (so add %L). The two terms are multiplied, so their percentage uncertainties add. The full propagation is:

%f = %L + ½(%T + %μ) = %L + ½%T + ½%μ

The factor of 2 in 1/(2L) is an exact constant with no uncertainty.

WPH12 Exam context: CP5 — the student varies L or T and measures f. Examiner reports (Oct 2021) show students frequently forget the ½ multiplier on %T and %μ (because of the square root) and add the full %T to %L. They also sometimes apply the ½ to %L by mistake — this is wrong because L is raised to the power 1 in 1/(2L), not power ½.

2.5 Propagation through v = d/t (CP4)

Spec ref: Appendix 10 + CP4 + Appendix 7 (v = d/t)

Definition: For the speed of sound measured as v = d/t where d is the microphone separation and t is the time delay read from the oscilloscope, the propagation is the simple division rule: %v = %d + %t. There are no powers, so no multiplier. The uncertainty is usually dominated by %t because the oscilloscope time-base (e.g. ±0.1 ms) divided by a typical delay (e.g. 1 ms) gives a large percentage (10%), while the metre rule uncertainty on d = 1.000 m is only 0.1%.

WPH12 Exam context: CP4 — straightforward application of the multiply/divide rule. Examiner reports (Oct 2023) show the main error is converting the time-base uncertainty incorrectly (e.g. confusing ms and µs prefixes) and so mis-stating %t by a factor of 1000.

3. CP-Specific Uncertainty Vocabulary (CP4–CP8)

3.1 CP4 — Speed of Sound (Oscilloscope) Uncertainties

Spec ref: Appendix 9 CP4

Definition: The uncertainties specific to the speed-of-sound determination: (a) the microphone separation d read from a metre rule (±0.5 mm or ±1 mm if a difference of two readings); (b) the time delay t read from the oscilloscope time-base (± the time-base division, typically ±0.1 ms); (c) the temperature drift of the air (a systematic effect on the true v, not on the measured v); (d) the alignment of the microphones on the same axis as the source (a method error that biases d). The first two feed into %v = %d + %t.

WPH12 Exam context: Examiner expects the student to (i) state d with units and ± uncertainty, (ii) read the time-base from the oscilloscope trace and convert divisions to ms, (iii) state the percentage uncertainty in v with both contributions shown separately. ~3 marks per CP4 paper.

3.2 CP5 — Vibrating String Uncertainties

Spec ref: Appendix 9 CP5

Definition: The uncertainties specific to the standing-wave-on-a-string determination: (a) the frequency f read from the signal generator (±1 Hz digital, or ±5 Hz analogue); (b) the loop length L measured with a metre rule between the vibration generator and the bridge or between two nodes (±0.5 mm or ±1 mm if a difference); (c) the tension T = mg from hanging masses (uncertainty dominated by the mass, since g is exact to the precision needed — typically ±0.1 g on a 100 g mass = 0.1%); (d) the mass per unit length μ of the string (uncertainty from weighing and length-measuring the string sample, usually 1–2%).

WPH12 Exam context: The f–L relationship is usually plotted as f vs 1/L (straight line through origin, gradient = ½√(T/μ)) or L vs √T (gradient related to f and μ). Examiner expects the student to identify which variable's percentage uncertainty dominates. ~3–4 marks per CP5 paper.

3.3 CP6 — Diffraction Grating Uncertainties

Spec ref: Appendix 9 CP6

Definition: The uncertainties specific to the diffraction-grating determination of wavelength: (a) the angle θ measured with a protractor (±0.5° for a single reading, ±1° if a difference of two readings); (b) the grating spacing d calculated from the lines-per-mm specification N (d = 1/N; the manufacturer's quoted N is exact to the precision printed, so %d is usually negligible); (c) the order number n (an exact integer, no uncertainty); (d) the visual judgement of where the brightest part of the maximum falls on the protractor scale (a method error). The propagation is %λ = %d + %sin θ (since nλ = d sin θ, so λ = d sin θ / n).

WPH12 Exam context: The angle-to-sin conversion (§2.2) is the unique skill tested. Examiner reports show this is the most-missed single mark in CP6 — students who write Δsin θ = sin(0.5°) lose 1 mark out of 2.

3.4 CP7 — Resistivity Uncertainties

Spec ref: Appendix 9 CP7

Definition: The uncertainties specific to the resistivity determination: (a) the wire diameter d measured with a micrometer (±0.01 mm) at three points and two orientations, mean taken, zero error subtracted; (b) the wire length L between two marked points (±1 mm as a difference of two readings); (c) the resistance R = V/I from a voltmeter/ammeter pair (combined %R = %V + %I, but in practice the voltmeter and ammeter are precise enough that %R is dominated by the load resistor tolerance and the contact resistance at the crocodile clips — a systematic effect that increases the measured R); (d) the temperature rise in the wire from sustained current (a systematic effect on the true ρ because ρ increases with T for a metal). The propagation is %ρ = %R + 2×%d + %L (the doubling rule, §2.1).

WPH12 Exam context: THE key CP7 calculation. Examiner reports show this is the single most-tested propagation in WPH12 — appears with the doubling rule in 7 of the last 8 CP7 papers. ~4–5 marks per CP7 paper.

3.5 CP8 — EMF and Internal Resistance Uncertainties

Spec ref: Appendix 9 CP8

Definition: The uncertainties specific to the EMF and internal resistance determination from a V–I graph: (a) the terminal p.d. V read from a voltmeter (±0.01 V); (b) the current I read from an ammeter (±0.01 A); (c) the gradient uncertainty from the worst-acceptable-line method (Δr = ½|m_max − m_min|); (d) the intercept uncertainty from the same worst lines (Δε = ½|c_max − c_min|); (e) the heating of the internal resistance during current flow (a systematic drift that causes the points to curve away from a straight line at high I — method error, not captured by ± uncertainties).

WPH12 Exam context: Always asked as a 4-mark question: "Use the graph to determine r and ε, and state the uncertainty in each." Examiner reports show the three common errors listed in §2.3. ~4 marks per CP8 paper.

Worked Examples

1. Why WPH12 Uncertainty Matters

The WPH11 version of this extension covers the generic rules: add absolutes for sums/differences, add percentages for products/quotients, multiply by the power for powers, half-the-range for repeats, both worst lines for gradients, sig fig matching between value and uncertainty. You must know all of these before reading this file.

WPH12 then layers five CP-specific traps on top of those rules. Each trap costs an average of 1 mark per paper and the traps are independent (a student can know the doubling rule for CP7 and still miss the angle-to-sin conversion in CP6). The five worked examples in this file cover every trap, in the order CP4 → CP5 → CP6 → CP7 → CP8.

The spec says 32 of the 80 marks on each WPH12/01 paper are at Level 2 maths or above. Of those 32, typically 8–12 marks are uncertainty-related — see the exam-connections file §1 for the per-paper frequency table.

2. CP4 — Speed of Sound (Oscilloscope) Worked Example

Set-up: A student places two microphones 1.000 m apart facing a loudspeaker. The microphones are connected to a dual-beam oscilloscope set to a time-base of 0.1 ms/div. The trace shows the second microphone pulse is 3.0 divisions to the right of the first microphone pulse.

Readings:

  • Microphone separation d = 1.000 m, measured with a metre rule as a single reading (the rule is placed once with one end at microphone 1 and the other end at microphone 2). Absolute uncertainty = ±0.5 mm = ±0.0005 m (analogue, half the 1 mm resolution).
  • Time delay t = 3.0 div × 0.1 ms/div = 0.30 ms = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ s.
  • The time-base uncertainty is ±0.1 ms (±1 division on the graticule), since the trace is read to the nearest division. So t = (3.0 ± 1.0) × 0.1 ms = 0.30 ± 0.10 ms. Convert to seconds: t = (3.0 ± 1.0) × 10⁻⁴ s.

Step 1 — Calculate v:

  • v = d / t = 1.000 / (3.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 3333.3 m/s = 3.33 × 10³ m/s (3 sig figs to match the 2 sig figs in t = 3.0, but quoting 3 sig figs is acceptable here because the leading value 3.0 has 2 sig figs and the answer is rounded to match the limiting input — 2 sig figs would also be acceptable: 3.3 × 10³ m/s).

Step 2 — Calculate %uncertainties:

  • %d = (0.0005 / 1.000) × 100 = 0.05%
  • %t = (1.0 / 3.0) × 100 = 33.3%
  • %v = %d + %t = 0.05% + 33.3% = 33.4% (addition rule for division)

Step 3 — Convert back to absolute uncertainty:

  • Δv = 33.4% × 3333.3 / 100 = 1113 m/s ≈ 1100 m/s (2 sig figs in the uncertainty)
  • Result: v = 3300 ± 1100 m/s (or, more honestly, v = (3.3 ± 1.1) × 10³ m/s)

Step 4 — Diagnose:

The %t dominates overwhelmingly. The 1-division uncertainty on the oscilloscope trace is the bottleneck. Improvement (mark-scheme phrasing): "Increase d so the time delay is larger, reducing %t" OR "Use a faster time-base (e.g. 0.05 ms/div) so the same trace occupies more divisions and the relative reading uncertainty falls."

Common error (examiner report, Oct 2023): Students forget to convert 0.10 ms to seconds and write t = (3.0 ± 1.0) × 10⁻¹ s, giving v in m/ms (= m × 10³/s) — wrong units by a factor of 1000. Always carry units through every step.

3. CP5 — Vibrating String Worked Example

Set-up: A student investigates the first harmonic of a stretched string of mass per unit length μ = 0.00060 ± 0.00001 kg/m. The tension is provided by a hanging mass m = 0.200 ± 0.001 kg. The first-harmonic loop length is measured as L = 0.450 ± 0.001 m (a difference of two metre-rule readings, so ±1 mm).

Step 1 — Calculate f:

  • T = mg = 0.200 × 9.81 = 1.962 N (g = 9.81 N/kg exact to 3 sig fig)
  • f = (1 / 2L) × √(T/μ) = (1 / (2 × 0.450)) × √(1.962 / 0.00060)
  • f = (1 / 0.900) × √(3270) = 1.111 × 57.18 = 63.5 Hz

Step 2 — Calculate %uncertainties for each input:

  • %L = (0.001 / 0.450) × 100 = 0.222%
  • %T = %m (since T = mg and g is exact) = (0.001 / 0.200) × 100 = 0.500%
  • %μ = (0.00001 / 0.00060) × 100 = 1.667%

Step 3 — Propagate through f = (1/2L)√(T/μ):

  • The factor 1/(2L) contributes %L = 0.222% to %f (L is to the power 1 — no multiplier).
  • The factor √(T/μ) is (T/μ)(1/2). The division T/μ contributes %T + %μ = 0.500 + 1.667 = 2.167%. The square root (power ½) multiplies this by ½: ½ × 2.167 = 1.083%.
  • The two factors are multiplied, so their percentage uncertainties add:
  • %f = %L + ½(%T + %μ) = 0.222% + 1.083% = 1.305% ≈ 1.3%

Step 4 — Convert to absolute uncertainty:

  • Δf = 1.3% × 63.5 / 100 = 0.83 Hz ≈ 0.8 Hz (1 sig fig in uncertainty)
  • Result: f = 64 ± 1 Hz (round the value to the same decimal place as the uncertainty — here to the nearest integer because Δf is to the nearest integer)

Common errors (examiner report, Oct 2021):

  1. Applying the ½ to %L (writing ½%L + ½%T + ½%μ) — wrong, because L is raised to power 1 in 1/(2L), not power ½. The ½ only applies inside the square root.
  2. Forgetting the ½ on %T and %μ (writing %L + %T + %μ) — wrong, because the square root is a power of ½.
  3. Treating the constant 2 in 1/(2L) as having uncertainty — exact constants have no uncertainty.

Tip: Rewrite the formula as f = (1/2) × L−1 × T(1/2) × μ(−1/2) before propagating. The exponent on each variable tells you the multiplier: −1 on L, ½ on T, −½ on μ. Then %f = 1×%L + ½×%T + ½×%μ (use the absolute value of the exponent for the multiplier, because percentage uncertainties always add).

4. CP6 — Diffraction Grating Worked Example (Angle-to-Sin Trap)

Set-up: A student uses a diffraction grating with N = 300 lines/mm (so d = 1/N mm) to measure the wavelength of a laser. The first-order (n = 1) maximum is measured at an angle θ = 25° from the straight-through direction. The angle is read from a protractor graduated every 1°, so the absolute uncertainty is ±0.5° (analogue, half the smallest division). The angle θ is a single reading taken from the straight-through direction.

Step 1 — Calculate d and λ:

  • d = 1 / 300 mm = (1/300) × 10⁻³ m = 3.333 × 10⁻&sup6; m
  • λ = (d × sin θ) / n = (3.333 × 10⁻&sup6; × sin 25°) / 1 = 3.333 × 10⁻&sup6; × 0.4226 = 1.409 × 10⁻&sup6; m
  • λ = 1.41 × 10⁻&sup6; m = 633 nm (3 sig figs to match d and θ)

Step 2 — Calculate %d:

The lines-per-mm specification N = 300 is the manufacturer's value, treated as exact to the precision printed (3 sig figs). So %d = 0% (negligible compared to the angle contribution). If the question specifies a tolerance on N (e.g. "300 ± 5 lines/mm"), use it; otherwise take d as exact.

Step 3 — Calculate %sin θ using the angle-to-sin conversion (the trap):

  • The angle uncertainty is Δθ = 0.5°. Convert to radians: Δθ_rad = 0.5 × π/180 = 0.00873 rad.
  • Apply the calculus-of-errors formula: Δ(sin θ) = cos θ × Δθ_rad = cos(25°) × 0.00873 = 0.9063 × 0.00873 = 0.00791.
  • sin θ = sin(25°) = 0.4226.
  • %sin θ = (0.00791 / 0.4226) × 100 = 1.87%

Step 4 — Propagate through λ = d sin θ / n:

  • n is an exact integer (no uncertainty).
  • %λ = %d + %sin θ = 0% + 1.87% = 1.87%

Step 5 — Convert to absolute uncertainty:

  • Δλ = 1.87% × 1.409 × 10⁻&sup6; / 100 = 2.6 × 10⁻&sup8; m ≈ 3 × 10⁻&sup8; m (1 sig fig)
  • Result: λ = (1.41 ± 0.03) × 10⁻&sup6; m or equivalently λ = 1410 ± 30 nm

Common errors (examiner report, Jan 2024):

  1. Writing Δ(sin θ) = sin(0.5°) = 0.00873 — coincidentally similar to the correct 0.00791 in this case (because for small angles sin ≈ angle in radians), but this is the WRONG method and gives different values for larger angles. Always use cos θ × Δθ_rad.
  2. Treating the 0.5° as a percentage directly: writing %sin θ = 0.5% — wrong by a factor of 4.
  3. Forgetting to convert degrees to radians: writing Δ(sin θ) = cos(25°) × 0.5 = 0.45 — wrong by a factor of 180/π ≈ 57. This is the single most-penalised error in CP6.
  4. Calculating (sin(25.5°) − sin(25°)) as the uncertainty — numerically close to the correct calculus-of-errors value for small Δθ but conceptually wrong because it measures the function's change rather than the propagated uncertainty. Examiners accept it for small Δθ but the spec-endorsed method is the cos θ × Δθ_rad formula.

Tip for remembering: The calculus-of-errors formula Δf(x) = |f'(x)| × Δx for any function f. For f(θ) = sin θ, f'(θ) = cos θ, so Δ(sin θ) = |cos θ| × Δθ with Δθ in radians. Practise this once with θ = 60°, Δθ = 0.5° — you should get Δ(sin θ) = cos 60° × 0.00873 = 0.5 × 0.00873 = 0.00437.

5. CP7 — Resistivity Worked Example (THE Doubling Trap)

Set-up: A student measures the resistivity of a nichrome wire. The wire diameter is measured with a micrometer (resolution 0.01 mm) at three points along the wire and in two perpendicular orientations at each point. The six readings are: 0.38, 0.39, 0.38, 0.40, 0.38, 0.39 mm. The micrometer has a zero error of +0.01 mm (reads 0.01 mm when the anvil and spindle are closed). The wire length between two marked points is L = 1.000 ± 0.001 m. The resistance between the two marked points is R = 5.20 ± 0.10 Ω (the ±0.10 Ω combines the voltmeter/ammeter percentage uncertainties and the contact resistance variation).

Step 1 — Correct the diameter readings for the zero error:

  • Subtract the +0.01 mm zero error from every reading (systematic correction):
  • Corrected readings: 0.37, 0.38, 0.37, 0.39, 0.37, 0.38 mm

Step 2 — Calculate the mean diameter and its uncertainty:

  • Mean d = (0.37 + 0.38 + 0.37 + 0.39 + 0.37 + 0.38) / 6 = 2.26 / 6 = 0.3767 mm
  • Range = 0.39 − 0.37 = 0.02 mm
  • Absolute uncertainty from repeats = ½ × range = 0.01 mm
  • Compare with the micrometer resolution (±0.01 mm). Take the larger: ±0.01 mm.
  • d = 0.38 ± 0.01 mm (round the value to the same decimal place as the uncertainty)

Step 3 — Calculate the cross-sectional area A:

  • A = πd²/4 = π × (0.38 × 10⁻³)² / 4 = π × 1.444 × 10⁻&sup7; / 4 = 1.134 × 10⁻&sup7; m²

Step 4 — Calculate ρ:

  • ρ = R A / L = (5.20 × 1.134 × 10⁻&sup7;) / 1.000 = 5.90 × 10⁻&sup7; Ω·m

Step 5 — Calculate %uncertainties:

  • %d = (0.01 / 0.38) × 100 = 2.63%
  • %A = 2 × %d = 2 × 2.63% = 5.26% (THE DOUBLING RULE — d is squared in A = πd²/4)
  • %L = (0.001 / 1.000) × 100 = 0.10%
  • %R = (0.10 / 5.20) × 100 = 1.92%

Step 6 — Propagate through ρ = R A / L:

  • ρ = R × A / L is a product of R and A, divided by L. All operations are multiplication/division, so add all percentage uncertainties:
  • %ρ = %R + %A + %L = 1.92% + 5.26% + 0.10% = 7.28% ≈ 7.3%
  • Substituting the doubling rule: %ρ = %R + 2×%d + %L = 1.92 + 5.26 + 0.10 = 7.3%

Step 7 — Convert to absolute uncertainty:

  • Δρ = 7.3% × 5.90 × 10⁻&sup7; / 100 = 4.3 × 10⁻&sup8; Ω·m
  • Result: ρ = (5.9 ± 0.4) × 10⁻&sup7; Ω·m (uncertainty to 1 sig fig; value to the same decimal place)

Common errors (examiner reports, Oct 2023 / Jan 2024 / Jan 2025):

  1. Forgetting the doubling rule — writing %ρ = %R + %d + %L = 1.92 + 2.63 + 0.10 = 4.7%. This is wrong because d is squared inside A; the %d must be doubled before adding to %ρ. Costs 1 mark out of 3.
  2. Not subtracting the zero error — using the raw 0.38–0.40 mm readings, getting d_mean = 0.387 mm instead of 0.377 mm. This is a systematic error and shifts ρ by ~5%.
  3. Adding the resolution AND the half-range uncertainty — writing Δd = 0.01 + 0.01 = 0.02 mm. Wrong: take the LARGER of the two, do not add.
  4. Using the absolute uncertainty in d directly as the absolute uncertainty in A — writing ΔA = 0.01 mm². Wrong: percentage uncertainties, not absolute, propagate through the square.
  5. Rounding the diameter before calculating A — using d = 0.38 mm (2 sig figs) for the area calculation gives A = 1.13 × 10⁻&sup7; m², but if the full d = 0.3767 mm is used, A = 1.115 × 10⁻&sup7; m² — a 2% difference. Always use unrounded values for intermediate calculations and round only at the end.

Why this dominates the resistivity uncertainty: %A (5.3%) is the largest single contribution to %ρ, and it is entirely due to the d² term. Even with a perfect micrometer (±0.005 mm), %A would still be 2.6%. This is why the spec CP7 statement explicitly requires the student to take repeat diameter readings — the only way to reduce %A is to reduce %d, and the only way to reduce %d is to take repeats (halving the range) and use a more precise micrometer.

6. CP8 — EMF and Internal Resistance Worked Example (V–I Graph)

Set-up: A student varies the load resistor across a cell and measures the terminal p.d. V (voltmeter) and current I (ammeter). She plots V (y-axis) against I (x-axis) and draws the best-fit line and the two worst acceptable lines through the error bars. The best-fit gradient is −1.20 V/A; the steepest worst line gradient is −1.40 V/A; the shallowest worst line gradient is −1.00 V/A. The best-fit y-intercept is 1.50 V; the steepest worst line intercept is 1.65 V; the shallowest worst line intercept is 1.35 V.

Step 1 — Identify what the gradient and intercept represent:

  • The equation is V = ε − Ir, which is y = ε + (−r) × I when y = V and x = I.
  • So gradient = −r → r = −gradient = −(−1.20) = 1.20 Ω.
  • And y-intercept = ε = 1.50 V.

Step 2 — Calculate the uncertainty in r (the gradient magnitude):

  • The two worst gradients are m_max = −1.00 V/A (the shallowest, which algebraically is the largest because it is closest to zero — gradients here are negative) and m_min = −1.40 V/A (the steepest, which is the most negative — algebraically the smallest).
  • |m_max| = 1.00 Ω, |m_min| = 1.40 Ω.
  • Δr = ½ × (|m_min| − |m_max|) = ½ × (1.40 − 1.00) = ½ × 0.40 = 0.20 Ω.
  • Result for internal resistance: r = 1.20 ± 0.20 Ω (or r = 1.2 ± 0.2 Ω to 2 sig figs in the value, matching the 1 sig fig in the uncertainty)

Step 3 — Calculate the uncertainty in ε (the intercept):

  • The two worst intercepts are c_max = 1.65 V (highest) and c_min = 1.35 V (lowest).
  • Δε = ½ × (c_max − c_min) = ½ × (1.65 − 1.35) = ½ × 0.30 = 0.15 V ≈ 0.2 V (1 sig fig)
  • Result for EMF: ε = 1.50 ± 0.15 V or rounded to 1 sig fig in uncertainty: ε = 1.5 ± 0.2 V

Step 4 — Sig fig matching:

  • For r: Δr = 0.20 Ω. The best-fit value r = 1.20 Ω already has the same decimal place (2 decimal places — but Δr = 0.2 Ω is 1 decimal place, so r should be quoted to 1 decimal place: r = 1.2 ± 0.2 Ω).
  • For ε: Δε = 0.2 V. The best-fit ε = 1.50 V should be rounded to 1 decimal place: ε = 1.5 ± 0.2 V.

Common errors (examiner reports, Oct 2022 / Jan 2023 / Oct 2024):

  1. Reporting a negative uncertainty or negative r — writing r = −1.20 ± 0.20 Ω because the gradient was negative. r is the magnitude; the negative sign on the gradient tells you the line slopes down, not that resistance is negative. Always take the modulus: r = +1.2 Ω.
  2. Drawing only one worst line — students draw only the steepest and use Δr = |m_max − m_best| instead of ½|m_max − m_min|. This overstates the uncertainty and loses 2 marks (one for not drawing the second line, one for the wrong formula).
  3. Using the full range instead of half — writing Δr = |m_max − m_min| = 0.40 Ω instead of 0.20 Ω. Always use half the range.
  4. Confusing gradient and intercept — assigning the gradient value to ε and the intercept to r. This is a misreading of y = mx + c; check the equation form (V = ε − Ir has ε as the constant term = intercept).
  5. Not drawing the worst lines through ALL error bars — the worst acceptable line must pass through every error bar on the graph, not just the end ones. A line that clips one error bar but misses another is not a valid worst line.

Why the worst-line method works here: The V–I data are inherently scattered because the ammeter and voltmeter each contribute ±0.01 (in their respective units) of random reading uncertainty, plus the contact resistance at the variable resistor changes slightly between readings. The error bars capture this scatter. The two worst lines represent the extreme slopes that remain consistent with the data, so half their difference is the standard uncertainty in the gradient.

10. WPH12 Calculation Flowchart (CP-Specific)

For each WPH12 CP calculation:

1. Identify the formula and isolate the unknown.
   ├── CP4: v = d / t
   ├── CP5: f = (1/2L)√(T/μ)
   ├── CP6: λ = d sin θ / n
   ├── CP7: ρ = R A / L  with A = πd²/4
   └── CP8: r = −gradient, ε = y-intercept from V-I graph

2. List each input with its value AND absolute uncertainty.
   ├── Single reading: ΔX = ± resolution (digital) or ± ½ resolution (analogue)
   ├── Repeats: ΔX = ½ range (exclude anomalies, subtract zero error first)
   └── Difference of two readings: double the single-reading uncertainty

3. Convert every input to % uncertainty.
   └── %X = (ΔX / X) × 100 — denominator is the MEASURED VALUE, not the calculated result

4. Apply the propagation rule:
   ├── CP4: %v = %d + %t  (division rule)
   ├── CP5: %f = %L + ½%T + ½%μ  (L to power 1, T and μ to power ½)
   ├── CP6: %λ = %d + %sin θ  where Δ(sin θ) = cos θ × Δθ_RADIANS
   ├── CP7: %ρ = %R + 2×%d + %L  (d is squared in A — THE doubling rule)
   └── CP8: Δr = ½|m_max − m_min|;  Δε = ½|c_max − c_min|  (worst-line method)

5. Convert % back to absolute: ΔX = %X × X / 100

6. Quote result: value ± uncertainty, same decimal place, correct units.
   └── The magnitude r is positive (modulus of the negative gradient) in CP8.

7. Improvement answer (2 marks): name the specific change + state which
   uncertainty it reduces and by how much. Vague = 0 marks.

2. CP-by-CP Question Patterns

2.1 CP4 — Speed of Sound (Oscilloscope)

Typical question shape (4–6 marks):

  1. State the time-base setting shown on the oscilloscope (1 mark).
  2. Read the time delay t from the trace and convert from divisions to ms or s (1 mark).
  3. Calculate v = d / t (1 mark).
  4. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in v (2 marks — separate %d and %t contributions).
  5. Suggest one improvement that reduces %t (1 mark).

Mark-scheme phrasing that scores:

  • "Time-base = 0.1 ms/div" (must include units)
  • "t = 3 div × 0.1 ms/div = 0.3 ms = 3 × 10⁻⁴ s" (must show the division-to-time conversion)
  • "%t = (0.1 / 3.0) × 100 = 3.3%" (or whatever the question's value is — denominator must be the measured value, not the calculated v)
  • "%v = %d + %t = 0.05% + 3.3% = 3.4%" (must show both contributions and addition)
  • "Increase d so t is larger, reducing %t" OR "Use a finer time-base (e.g. 0.05 ms/div) so the same delay occupies more divisions"

Common pitfall: Confusing ms and µs prefixes when converting the time-base. A 0.1 ms/div setting is 100 µs/div — using 0.1 µs/div gives %t wrong by 1000×. Examiner reports (Oct 2023) flag this on 4 of the last 6 CP4 papers.

2.2 CP5 — Vibrating String

Typical question shape (4–6 marks):

  1. Calculate T from the hanging mass (1 mark).
  2. Calculate f from f = (1/2L)√(T/μ) (2 marks — must substitute correctly).
  3. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in f (2 marks — the ½ multiplier on %T and %μ is the key).
  4. Plot or interpret a graph of f vs 1/L and find μ from the gradient (1–2 marks).

Mark-scheme phrasing that scores:

  • "T = mg = 0.200 × 9.81 = 1.96 N" (must show substitution)
  • "f = (1 / (2 × 0.450)) × √(1.96 / 6.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 64 Hz" (must show every step including the ½ inside the formula)
  • "%f = %L + ½%T + ½%μ" (the ½ applies ONLY to T and μ because of the square root — not to L)
  • "Use a longer sample of the same string to determine μ, reducing %μ" (improvement)

Common pitfall: Applying the ½ to %L. Examiner reports (Oct 2021, Jan 2025) note that ~30% of students write %f = ½%L + ½%T + ½%μ — this is wrong because L is in 1/(2L) (power 1), not inside the square root. The ½ exponent applies only to T and μ.

2.3 CP6 — Diffraction Grating

Typical question shape (4–6 marks):

  1. Calculate the grating spacing d from the lines-per-mm N (1 mark).
  2. Calculate λ from nλ = d sin θ (2 marks).
  3. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in λ (2 marks — the angle-to-sin conversion is the key).
  4. Comment on whether the result agrees with the manufacturer's stated wavelength (1 mark — comparing within combined uncertainties).

Mark-scheme phrasing that scores:

  • "d = 1/N = 1/(300 mm⁻¹) = 3.33 × 10⁻&sup6; m" (must convert mm⁻¹ to m)
  • "λ = d sin θ / n = 3.33 × 10⁻&sup6; × sin 25° / 1 = 1.41 × 10⁻&sup6; m" (must show sin θ substitution)
  • "Δθ_rad = 0.5 × π/180 = 8.73 × 10⁻³ rad" (must convert degrees to radians)
  • "Δ(sin θ) = cos θ × Δθ_rad = cos 25° × 8.73 × 10⁻³ = 7.9 × 10⁻³"
  • "%sin θ = (7.9 × 10⁻³ / sin 25°) × 100 = 1.9%"
  • "%λ = %d + %sin θ = 0% + 1.9% = 1.9%"
  • "Use a grating with more lines per mm (smaller d) to increase θ for the same λ, giving a larger sin θ and so reducing %sin θ" (improvement)

Common pitfall: Using degrees directly in Δ(sin θ) = cos θ × Δθ. Examiner reports (Jan 2024) show ~40% of students write Δ(sin θ) = cos 25° × 0.5 = 0.45 — wrong by a factor of 180/π ≈ 57. Always convert Δθ to radians first.

2.4 CP7 — Resistivity (THE Doubling-Trap CP)

Typical question shape (8–12 marks — the largest CP question on most papers):

  1. Measure the wire diameter from a micrometer reading, including the zero error correction (1–2 marks).
  2. Calculate the mean diameter and its uncertainty from repeat readings (2 marks — ½ range, exclude anomalies, subtract zero error).
  3. Calculate the cross-sectional area A from A = πd²/4 (1 mark).
  4. Calculate the resistivity ρ from ρ = RA/L (2 marks — R from V/I, A from step 3, L from metre rule).
  5. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in ρ, showing each contribution (3 marks — the doubling rule is the third mark).
  6. Suggest one improvement that reduces the dominant uncertainty (1–2 marks).

Mark-scheme phrasing that scores:

  • "Zero error = +0.01 mm; corrected readings are 0.37, 0.38, 0.37, 0.39, 0.37, 0.38 mm" (systematic correction)
  • "Mean d = 0.38 mm; range = 0.02 mm; Δd = ½ × 0.02 = 0.01 mm" (half the range)
  • "%d = (0.01 / 0.38) × 100 = 2.6%"
  • "%A = 2 × %d = 5.3%" (THE doubling rule — d is squared in A = πd²/4)
  • "%ρ = %R + %A + %L = 1.9% + 5.3% + 0.1% = 7.3%" (must add all three contributions, with %A already doubled)
  • "Take more repeat readings of d at different points and orientations, reducing the range and so %d (and so %A by the doubling rule)" (improvement)

Common pitfall (THE trap): Writing %ρ = %R + %d + %L — forgetting to double %d. Examiner reports (Oct 2023, Jan 2024, Oct 2024, Jan 2025) flag this on 4 of the last 4 CP7 papers. ~50–60% of students make this error. The single mark lost is the difference between an A and A* on most CP7 questions.

8. Practice Question Answers

7.1 CP4 Answer

(a) t = 2.0 div × 0.2 ms/div = 0.4 ms = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ s. Absolute uncertainty = ±0.2 ms = ±2 × 10⁻⁴ s (one division on the time-base, since the reading is to the nearest division). So t = (4.0 ± 2.0) × 10⁻⁴ s.

(b) v = d / t = 0.800 / (4.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 2000 m/s = 2.0 × 10³ m/s.

(c) %d = (0.001 / 0.800) × 100 = 0.125%. %t = (2.0 / 4.0) × 100 = 50%. %v = %d + %t = 0.125% + 50% = 50.1% ≈ 50%. The %t dominates overwhelmingly — improvement would be to increase d (e.g. to 4 m) so that t = 2 ms and %t = 10%, OR use a finer time-base.

7.2 CP5 Answer

(a) f = (1 / (2 × 0.500)) × √(1.96 / 5.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 1.0 × √3920 = 1.0 × 62.6 = 62.6 Hz ≈ 63 Hz.

(b) %L = (0.001 / 0.500) × 100 = 0.20%. %T = (0.01 / 1.96) × 100 = 0.51%. %μ = (1.0 × 10⁻&sup5; / 5.0 × 10⁻⁴) × 100 = 2.0%. %f = %L + ½%T + ½%μ = 0.20% + 0.255% + 1.0% = 1.46% ≈ 1.5%. The %μ dominates because of the square root (½ × 2.0% = 1.0%) — improvement would be to use a longer string sample to reduce %μ.

7.3 CP6 Answer

(a) d = 1/N = 1/(600 mm⁻¹) = (1/600) × 10⁻³ m = 1.667 × 10⁻&sup6; m. λ = d sin θ / n = (1.667 × 10⁻&sup6; × sin 30°) / 2 = (1.667 × 10⁻&sup6; × 0.500) / 2 = 4.17 × 10⁻&sup7; m = 417 nm.

(b) Δθ = 0.5° (single protractor reading, half the 1° resolution). Δθ_rad = 0.5 × π/180 = 8.73 × 10⁻³ rad. Δ(sin θ) = cos 30° × 8.73 × 10⁻³ = 0.866 × 8.73 × 10⁻³ = 7.56 × 10⁻³. %sin θ = (7.56 × 10⁻³ / sin 30°) × 100 = (7.56 × 10⁻³ / 0.500) × 100 = 1.51%. %d = 0% (manufacturer's value, treated as exact). %λ = %d + %sin θ = 0% + 1.51% = 1.51% ≈ 1.5%. So λ = (4.17 ± 0.06) × 10⁻&sup7; m, or 417 ± 6 nm.

7.4 CP7 Answer

(a) Corrected readings are already given: 0.24, 0.25, 0.24, 0.25, 0.24, 0.25 mm. Mean d = (3×0.24 + 3×0.25)/6 = (0.72 + 0.75)/6 = 1.47/6 = 0.245 mm. Range = 0.25 − 0.24 = 0.01 mm. Δd = ½ × 0.01 = 0.005 mm. Compare with micrometer resolution (0.01 mm): take the larger, so Δd = ±0.01 mm. So d = 0.25 ± 0.01 mm (rounding the mean to the same decimal place as the uncertainty).

(b) A = πd²/4 = π × (0.25 × 10⁻³)² / 4 = π × 6.25 × 10⁻&sup8; / 4 = 4.91 × 10⁻&sup8; m².

(c) ρ = RA/L = (4.50 × 4.91 × 10⁻&sup8;) / 0.800 = 2.76 × 10⁻&sup7; Ω·m.

(d) %d = (0.01 / 0.245) × 100 = 4.08%. %A = 2 × %d = 8.16%. %L = (0.001 / 0.800) × 100 = 0.125%. %R = (0.10 / 4.50) × 100 = 2.22%. %ρ = %R + %A + %L = 2.22% + 8.16% + 0.125% = 10.5%. So ρ = (2.76 ± 0.29) × 10⁻&sup7; Ω·m, or to 1 sig fig in uncertainty: ρ = (2.8 ± 0.3) × 10⁻&sup7; Ω·m. The %A dominates (8.16%) because of the doubling rule — improvement: take more repeat diameter readings and use a digital micrometer with finer resolution.

7.5 CP8 Answer

(a) r = magnitude of the best-fit gradient = 1.50 Ω. Δr = ½ × |m_max − m_min| = ½ × |−1.30 − (−1.70)| = ½ × 0.40 = 0.20 Ω. So r = 1.5 ± 0.2 Ω.

(b) ε = best-fit y-intercept = 4.50 V. Δε = ½ × |c_max − c_min| = ½ × |4.70 − 4.30| = ½ × 0.40 = 0.20 V. So ε = 4.5 ± 0.2 V.

(c) Sig figs: both uncertainties are 0.2 (1 sig fig). So r = 1.5 ± 0.2 Ω and ε = 4.5 ± 0.2 V — both values are quoted to 1 decimal place to match the uncertainty. Note r is positive (modulus of the negative gradient) — the negative sign tells you the line slopes down, not that resistance is negative.
