## Overview

The page opens by listing the most common English punctuation marks: capital letters and full stops, question marks, commas, colons and semi-colons, exclamation marks, and quotation marks. It frames punctuation as the written counterpart to the pauses and voice pitch we use in speech, making text easier to read. It distinguishes between hard **rules** that must be followed and **conventions** that give writers choice.

## 1. Capital letters (B, D) and full stops (.)

- Capital letters open sentences; full stops close them.
- Capitals also mark proper nouns: personal names (with titles), nationalities and languages, days and months, public holidays, and geographical names.
- Capitals are used for titles of books, magazines, newspapers, plays, and music.
- Full stops also appear in initials (e.g., "J. A. Smith") and after abbreviations (Dr., Prof., etc.), though the latter is described as becoming less common.

## 2. Question marks (?) and exclamation marks (!)

- Question marks signal that a sentence is a question; a full stop is not used together with one.
- Exclamation marks mark exclamative clauses or expressions in informal writing, and informal writing sometimes repeats them for emphasis.

## 3. Commas (,)

- Commas separate items in a list of similar words or phrases.
- British English normally omits a comma before the final *and* in a list of single words; American English typically keeps it (the serial comma).
- Commas mark slight pauses: asides, qualifying phrases, and appositive-style interruptions naming or describing a noun.

### Separating clauses with commas

- With main clauses joined by *and*, *or*, or *but*, a comma is normally avoided when both clauses share the same subject, but is often used when the subjects differ.
- A fronted subordinate clause is usually followed by a comma, though short sentences often skip it.
- Comment clauses (subordinate or non-finite) that add extra information are commonly set off with commas.

### Commas and relative clauses

- Commas mark **non-defining** relative clauses (and non-finite equivalents) that add non-essential information.
- **Defining** clauses take no commas — the page flags this with an explicit "warning" and a not/correct contrast.

### Commas and speech forms

- Question tags and yes/no responses are separated by commas.
- Vocatives (direct address), discourse markers, and interjections are usually set off with commas.
- Commas introduce following direct speech (or a colon may be used); when speech comes first, a comma goes before the closing quotation mark.

## 4. Colons (:) and semi-colons (;)

- Colons introduce lists, subtitles or topic subdivisions, direct speech, and a second sentence that explains or justifies the first.
- Semi-colons join two grammatically separate but meaningfully related main clauses as an alternative to full stops.
- The page notes semi-colons are not common in contemporary English, where full stops and commas dominate.

## 5. Quotation marks ('…' or "…")

- Direct speech is enclosed in single or double quotation marks (single ones increasingly common), usually opening with a capital and preceded by a comma or colon.
- The reporting clause can sit before, after, or mid-speech; the page shows the comma and full stop placement for all three positions.
- Quotes inside quotes alternate between double and single marks (either order).
- Question marks stay inside the quotation marks unless the question belongs to the reporting clause.
- Single quotation marks also serve as "scare quotes" questioning a word's exact meaning, and appear in headlines.
- Titles of books, newspapers, films, songs, etc. may take quotation marks or italics; chapters and short story titles normally take single quotation marks.

## 6. Dashes (–) and other punctuation marks

- Dashes are informal; they work like commas or semi-colons and may be used singly or in pairs.
- Brackets behave similarly, adding extra non-essential information, and are standard around dates and page numbers in academic writing.
- Forward slashes appear in internet addresses and mean *and/or* in academic references.

## 7. Numerals and punctuation

- British dates run day–month–year and are written with full stops, slashes, or dashes (e.g., 1.8.1985 / 1/8/1985 / 1–8–1985).
- American English reverses day and month, so the same date reads differently.
- Weights, measures, and number references are normally unpunctuated (4kg, 10m, 5m dollars).
- Commas mark thousands and millions in numbers; full stops (never commas) mark decimals; times take full stops or colons (9.30 or 9:30).

## 8. Saying email and internet addresses

- Spoken convention: read addresses word by word, spelling out letters when needed — "dot" for periods, "at" for @, "underscore" for _, "forward slash" for /, with multi-word domain parts spelled out or said as one word.
