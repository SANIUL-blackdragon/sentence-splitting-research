Part 1 — Definitions

Chapter 2.3 — Waves and Particle Nature of Light: Definitions

Unit: WPH12 (International A-Level Physics, Unit 2) Specification Points: 33–63 Total Historical Marks: 727 across 13 papers (average 55.9 per session)

1. Wave Properties (Spec 33–34)

Amplitude is the maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium position. It determines the energy carried by the wave: greater amplitude means greater energy. Amplitude is a constant property of a wave, not the instantaneous displacement at a given moment.

Frequency is the number of complete oscillations per unit time, measured in hertz (Hz). One hertz equals one oscillation per second. Frequency is related to period by f = 1/T.

Period is the time taken for one complete oscillation or wave cycle, measured in seconds. It is the reciprocal of frequency: T = 1/f.

Wavelength is the distance between two consecutive points in phase on a wave — for example, crest to crest or compression to compression. It is the spatial length of one full wave cycle.

Wave speed is the distance travelled by a wave per unit time. For a progressive wave, wave speed v = fλ, where f is frequency and λ is wavelength.

The wave equation (v = fλ) relates the speed of a wave to its frequency and wavelength. This equation applies to all progressive waves, both transverse and longitudinal.

Exam connection: The amplitude vs instantaneous displacement distinction has cost marks in Jan 2022, Jun 2022, and Jun 2023. Amplitude is the maximum displacement — stating the displacement of a particle at one instant is not the amplitude. QWC marks require cause-and-effect language: "greater amplitude → greater energy" earns the logical connection mark (all 13 papers penalise listing without connective words).

2. Longitudinal and Transverse Waves (Spec 35–37)

Longitudinal wave is a wave in which the oscillations of the particles are parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Sound waves in air are longitudinal: air molecules oscillate back and forth along the direction the wave travels, creating regions of compression (high pressure) and rarefaction (low pressure).

Compression is a region in a longitudinal wave where the particles are closest together and the pressure is greatest above the normal. Molecules converge, increasing density and pressure.

Rarefaction is a region in a longitudinal wave where the particles are furthest apart and the pressure is lowest below the normal. Molecules diverge, decreasing density and pressure.

Transverse wave is a wave in which the oscillations of the particles are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Electromagnetic waves and waves on a string are transverse waves.

Displacement is the distance of a particle from its equilibrium position at a given instant, measured in metres. It can be positive or negative depending on direction. On a displacement–distance graph, the displacement varies sinusoidally along the direction of wave propagation.

Exam connection: The displacement-zero trap appeared in Oct 2019, Jan 2022, Jun 2022, and Oct 2022. At the centre of both a compression and a rarefaction, the displacement of molecules is zero (molecules pass through equilibrium). Pressure is maximum at compression and minimum at rarefaction — but displacement is zero at both. Graphs of transverse waves show displacement perpendicular to propagation direction; graphs of longitudinal waves show displacement parallel to propagation. Stationary wave graphs show the envelope of maximum displacement (Jan 2025 Q17a).

3. Superposition, Interference and Coherence (Spec 39–40)

Wavefront is a surface on which all points are in phase — typically represented as a line perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation. For a plane wave, wavefronts are parallel straight lines; for a point source, wavefronts are concentric circles.

Coherence describes two or more wave sources that maintain a constant phase relationship. Coherent sources have the same frequency and a constant phase difference, which is necessary for a stable interference pattern to be observed.

Path difference is the difference in the distances travelled by two waves from their respective sources to a given point. Path difference is measured in metres.

Phase difference is the difference in the phase angle between two waves at a point, measured in radians, degrees, or as a fraction of a cycle (e.g. λ/2, λ/4). When two waves are in phase, phase difference is 0 or a whole number of wavelengths; when in antiphase, phase difference is π rad (180°) or an odd number of half-wavelengths.

Relationship between phase difference and path difference: A path difference of one wavelength (λ) corresponds to a phase difference of 360° (2π rad). Therefore, phase difference (in radians) = (2π/λ) × path difference. A path difference of nλ gives constructive interference; a path difference of (n + ½)λ gives destructive interference.

Superposition is the principle that when two or more waves overlap at a point, the resultant displacement equals the algebraic sum of the individual displacements. This principle applies to all waves at all times.

Constructive interference occurs when two waves arrive at a point in phase, so their displacements add to produce a greater resultant amplitude. Maximum constructive interference occurs when the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths (nλ).

Destructive interference occurs when two waves arrive at a point in antiphase (180° out of phase), so their displacements subtract and cancel. Complete cancellation occurs when the two waves have equal amplitude and the path difference is an odd number of half-wavelengths ((n + ½)λ).

Beats are the periodic variation in amplitude (loudness) heard when two sound waves of slightly different frequencies are superposed. Beats occur because the phase difference between the two waves changes continuously over time, alternating between constructive and destructive interference. The beat frequency equals the difference in the two source frequencies: fbeat = |f₁ − f₂|.

Exam connection: Coherence and beats was a 6-mark QWC question in Oct 2022 Q15a(ii). The required answer chain: incoherent sources → changing phase difference → alternating constructive/destructive interference → beats. Must use connective words ("therefore", "because", "this causes") to earn the 2 structure marks. Phase difference must be linked explicitly to path difference using the formula (2π/λ) × path difference when calculations require it (Jan 2025 Q14b).

4. Stationary/Standing Waves (Spec 41–43)

Standing (stationary) wave is a wave pattern formed by the superposition of two progressive waves of the same frequency and amplitude travelling in opposite directions. Energy is stored in the wave rather than transferred, and the pattern does not move through the medium.

Node is a point on a standing wave where the amplitude of oscillation is always zero. Nodes occur at fixed positions separated by half a wavelength (λ/2).

Antinode is a point on a standing wave where the amplitude of oscillation is maximum. Antinodes occur midway between adjacent nodes.

Formation of a standing wave occurs when a progressive wave reflects from a boundary and superposes with the incident wave. At a fixed (closed) boundary, a node forms because the reflected wave is inverted (phase change of 180°). At a free (open) boundary, an antinode forms because the reflected wave is not inverted. The superposition of incident and reflected waves creates points of permanent cancellation (nodes) and maximum oscillation (antinodes).

Wave speed on a string is given by v = √(T/μ), where T is the tension in the string and μ is the mass per unit length. Doubling the tension increases the speed by a factor of √2; doubling μ reduces the speed by a factor of √2.

Mass per unit length (μ) is the linear density of a string, defined as μ = mass/length, measured in kg m⁻¹. A thicker or denser string has a higher μ, producing a lower wave speed and therefore a lower fundamental frequency.

Fundamental frequency (first harmonic) is the lowest frequency at which a standing wave can form on a string or in an air column. For a string fixed at both ends: f₁ = v/(2l) = (1/2l)√(T/μ).

Harmonics are whole-number multiples of the fundamental frequency. The nth harmonic has frequency f_n = n × f₁ and contains n half-wavelengths along the length of the medium. The second harmonic is the first overtone.

Exam connection: Stationary wave formation was a 6-mark QWC question in Jun 2025 Q17a for a closed tube: node at the closed end (fixed boundary), antinode at the open end (free boundary). The derivation f² = T/(4l²μ) appeared in Jan 2025 Q17c(i) — rearranging v = fλ and v = √(T/μ) with λ = 2l gives the gradient relationship. Distance between adjacent nodes = λ/2, NOT λ — this MCQ trap appeared in Oct 2023 Q6. CP5 requires describing the effect of changing length, tension, and mass per unit length on the fundamental frequency with reference to the formula.

5. Intensity of Radiation (Spec 44)

Intensity is the power of electromagnetic radiation per unit area, defined by I = P/A, where P is the power (in watts) and A is the area (in m²) perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Intensity is measured in W m⁻².

Inverse square law states that the intensity of radiation from a point source decreases with the square of the distance from the source: I ∝ 1/r². This arises because the radiation spreads over a sphere of surface area 4πr².

Efficiency is the ratio of useful power output to total power input, expressed as a percentage. For a solar panel, only the component of intensity perpendicular to the panel surface contributes to absorbed power, so effective intensity = I cos θ where θ is the angle of incidence from the normal.

Exam connection: Intensity calculations appeared in Oct 2023 Q16c (4 marks) with the cos 60° solar panel trap: the effective area receiving radiation is A cos θ, so effective intensity is I cos θ. Using diameter instead of radius in A = πr² is a recurring error. The link between intensity and amplitude is I ∝ A² — when asked about the effect of doubling amplitude on intensity, the answer is intensity increases by a factor of 4 (Jan 2022 Q13).

6. Refraction and Total Internal Reflection (Spec 45–48)

Refractive index (n) is the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to the speed of light in the material: n = c/v. A higher refractive index means light travels more slowly in the material. Refractive index is dimensionless and always greater than or equal to 1 for transparent materials.

Snell's law states that for light crossing a boundary between two media: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, where θ₁ and θ₂ are the angles of incidence and refraction measured from the normal. When light travels from a less optically dense to a more optically dense medium, it bends towards the normal (n₂ > n₁ → θ₂ < θ₁).

Critical angle (C) is the angle of incidence, measured from the normal, at which the angle of refraction equals 90°. It is defined only when light travels from a denser medium to a less dense medium (n₁ > n₂). The critical angle is given by sin C = n₂/n₁. For light travelling from a medium into air (or vacuum), sin C = 1/n.

Total internal reflection (TIR) occurs when light travelling in a denser medium strikes a boundary with a less dense medium at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle. All the light is reflected back into the denser medium — no light is transmitted. The two conditions for TIR are: (1) light must be travelling from a denser to a less dense medium, and (2) the angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle.

Measuring refractive index can be done using a rectangular glass block and tracing the path of a light ray, or by finding the critical angle and using n = 1/sin C. Using a ray box, measure angles of incidence and refraction, then plot sin θ₁ against sin θ₂ — the gradient equals n₂/n₁.

Exam connection: The angle-from-surface trap has appeared in 7 papers (Oct 2019, Jan 2022, Jan 2023, Jun 2023, Oct 2023, Jan 2025, Jun 2025). Angles given on diagrams are often measured from the surface, not the normal — you must subtract from 90° before applying Snell's law. In Jan 2025 Q18a, 50° from the surface → 40° from the normal. Critical angle with cladding was tested in Oct 2022 Q16a: n₁ sin C = n₂ sin 90°. Optical fibre multimode dispersion was a 5-mark question in Jan 2025 Q18c requiring explanation of pulse broadening and intensity loss due to TIR at the cladding boundary.

7. Plane Polarisation (Spec 49)

Plane polarisation is the restriction of the oscillations of a transverse wave to a single plane. Only transverse waves can be polarised; longitudinal waves (e.g. sound) cannot. A plane-polarised wave has all its oscillations in one direction perpendicular to the direction of travel.

Polarising filter is a material that transmits only the component of a wave's oscillation along its transmission axis. When unpolarised light passes through a single polarising filter, the transmitted intensity is exactly half the incident intensity, regardless of the filter's orientation, because the filter selects one of the two equal perpendicular components.

Malus's law states that when polarised light of intensity I₀ passes through a second polarising filter (analyser) at angle θ to the polarisation direction, the transmitted intensity is I = I₀ cos²θ. This law applies only between two filters — a single filter on unpolarised light always transmits 50%.

Polarisation by reflection occurs when light reflects from a non-metallic surface at a particular angle (Brewster's angle). The reflected light becomes partially polarised, with oscillations predominantly in the plane perpendicular to the plane of incidence. Polaroid sunglasses exploit this to reduce glare.

Exam connection: The single-filter trap appeared in Jun 2019, Oct 2019, Jun 2022, and Oct 2023. A single polarising filter always transmits 50% of unpolarised light — Malus's law (cos²θ) only applies when a second filter is placed after the first. The polarisation definition in Jun 2025 Q12b(i) (3 marks) required the phrase "perpendicular to the direction of travel/propagation" — omitting this lost a mark.

8. Diffraction (Spec 50–52)

Diffraction is the spreading of waves when they pass through a gap or around an obstacle. The amount of diffraction increases when the gap width is comparable to or smaller than the wavelength. Diffraction is a wave property and is not explained by the particle model.

Huygens' construction is a geometrical method for predicting the future position of a wavefront. Each point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets; the new wavefront is the envelope (tangent) of these wavelets. This construction explains reflection, refraction, and diffraction.

Diffraction grating is an optical component with many equally spaced parallel slits (typically hundreds per millimetre). It produces sharp, well-separated interference maxima given by nλ = d sin θ, where n is the order number, λ is the wavelength, d is the slit spacing, and θ is the angle of the nth-order maximum from the normal.

Slit spacing (d) is the distance between the centres of adjacent slits on a diffraction grating, measured in metres. If the grating is specified as "300 lines per mm", then d = 1/(300 × 10³) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m. Note: d = 1/(N), where N is the number of lines per metre (not per millimetre).

Exam connection: The d vs 1/d trap appeared in Oct 2022 Q18c and Jun 2023. d is the slit spacing (distance between adjacent slits), NOT the number of lines per metre. If given "lines per mm", convert to lines per metre first: multiply by 10³, then take the reciprocal. Jan 2025 Q14b(i) (4 marks) required the full chain: tan θ → sin θ → nλ = d sin θ → convert to lines per mm. Oct 2022 Q18c (4 marks) required working backwards from grating data to verify the stated line spacing. Diffraction does NOT occur at a glass block — refraction occurs instead (Oct 2022 Q8). Maximum order number is found by setting θ = 90°: n_max = d/λ, then rounding down to the nearest integer.

9. Wave-Particle Duality (Spec 53–54)

Electron diffraction is the phenomenon where a beam of electrons produces a diffraction pattern when passed through a thin crystal or very narrow slit. The observation of a diffraction pattern (with constructive and destructive interference) demonstrates that electrons have wave properties.

Wave-particle duality is the concept that all matter and electromagnetic radiation exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties. Electrons behave as particles (they carry charge and mass) but also as waves (they diffract and interfere). Light behaves as a wave (it diffracts and interferes) but also as particles (photons in the photoelectric effect).

De Broglie equation states that any particle with momentum has an associated wavelength: λ = h/p, where h is the Planck constant (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s) and p = mv is the momentum of the particle. For electrons accelerated through a potential difference V: λ = h/√(2m e V).

Exam connection: Electron diffraction evidence appeared in Oct 2022 Q11 (3 marks) — the answer must mention diffraction pattern, interference, and constructive/destructive to earn full marks. de Broglie wavelength calculation in Jun 2025 Q15b(i) required the chain: eV → joules → velocity (½mv² = eV) → λ = h/(mv). Remember: electrons diffract from crystal lattice spacing (of order 10⁻¹⁰ m), so electron wavelengths are also of this order — visible light wavelengths (10⁻⁷ m) are far too large to diffract through crystal planes.

10. Transmission, Reflection and Pulse-Echo (Spec 55–56)

Transmission at an interface occurs when a wave passes from one medium into another. Part of the wave energy is transmitted and part is reflected, with the proportions depending on the impedance mismatch between the two media. Diffraction does not occur at a simple flat interface between two media — it requires a gap or obstacle comparable to the wavelength.

Reflection at an interface occurs when a wave bounces back from a boundary between two media. The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence (measured from the normal). In ultrasound, reflection occurs at boundaries between tissues of different acoustic impedances.

Pulse-echo technique is a method of determining the distance to a boundary by sending a short pulse of wave energy and measuring the time for the echo to return. The distance to the boundary is d = (v × t)/2, where v is the wave speed in the medium and t is the round-trip time. The factor of 2 accounts for the pulse travelling to the boundary and back.

Resolution limit in pulse-echo techniques is determined by the pulse duration. Two boundaries can only be distinguished as separate if the round-trip time between them exceeds the pulse duration. If the echo from a closer boundary returns before the transmitted pulse has ended, the two signals overlap and the boundary cannot be detected. Shorter pulses give better resolution but carry less energy.

Exam connection: The factor-of-2 trap has appeared in 6+ papers (Jun 2019, Oct 2019, Jan 2023, Jun 2023, Oct 2023, Jun 2024). Distance = v × t/2, NOT v × t — the pulse travels there AND back. The counterintuitive resolution trap appeared in Jun 2025 Q13a: a crack may NOT be detected if the round-trip time to the crack is less than the pulse duration, because the echo overlaps with the outgoing pulse. This is the opposite of what students expect (shorter distance → easier to detect). Pulse duration vs round-trip time is the key comparison.

11. Photon Model and Energy (Spec 57–58)

Wave model of light describes electromagnetic radiation as a continuous wave with properties such as frequency, wavelength, amplitude, and intensity. The wave model successfully explains diffraction, interference, and polarisation but fails to explain the photoelectric effect — specifically, it predicts that any frequency of light should eventually eject electrons if the intensity is high enough and there should be a time delay at low intensities.

Photon model of light describes electromagnetic radiation as a stream of discrete packets of energy called photons. Each photon has energy E = hf, where h is the Planck constant and f is the frequency of the radiation. The photon model explains the photoelectric effect: a single photon interacts with a single electron in a one-to-one interaction.

Photon energy is given by E = hf, where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s is the Planck constant and f is the frequency of the electromagnetic radiation. Photon energy can also be written as E = hc/λ, connecting energy to wavelength. The energy of each photon is independent of the intensity of the beam — intensity depends on the number of photons per second, not the energy per photon.

Exam connection: The wave vs particle model comparison appeared in Oct 2023 Q17a and Jun 2025 Q19b (6-mark QWC). Students must explain what the wave model predicts (delay, frequency independence) AND what the photon model explains (instantaneous emission, threshold frequency, intensity affects number not energy). Simply stating "the wave model is wrong" without explaining its predictions loses marks. Photon counting (total energy / energy per photon = number of photons) appeared in Jun 2025 Q18b(ii).

12. Photoelectric Effect (Spec 59–62)

Photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a metal surface when electromagnetic radiation above a minimum frequency (the threshold frequency) is incident on it. Each ejected electron is called a photoelectron. The effect provides evidence for the particle nature of light.

Photoelectron is an electron emitted from a metal surface as a result of the photoelectric effect. The maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons depends on the frequency of the incident radiation, not its intensity.

Threshold frequency (f₀) is the minimum frequency of electromagnetic radiation required to eject electrons from a particular metal surface. Below the threshold frequency, no photoelectrons are emitted regardless of the intensity or duration of illumination. This is because each photon must have sufficient energy (hf ≥ φ) to overcome the work function.

Work function (φ) is the minimum energy required to remove an electron from the surface of a metal. It is a property of the metal and is measured in joules (or electronvolts). The work function equals the photon energy at the threshold frequency: φ = hf₀.

Photoelectric equation is hf = φ + ½mv²_max, which states that the energy of an incident photon equals the work function of the metal plus the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted photoelectron. Rearranged: ½mv²_max = hf − φ. The maximum kinetic energy increases linearly with frequency above the threshold frequency.

One-to-one interaction means that a single photon interacts with a single surface electron. If the photon energy is less than the work function, the electron cannot escape — the photon's energy cannot be accumulated over time or shared between multiple photons. This explains why the photoelectric effect is instantaneous and why intensity alone (more photons, each with the same energy) cannot cause emission below the threshold frequency.

Intensity in the photoelectric effect determines the number of photoelectrons emitted per second (the photocurrent), because higher intensity means more photons per second. Intensity does NOT affect the maximum kinetic energy of individual photoelectrons — that depends only on frequency.

Electronvolt (eV) is a unit of energy equal to the work done when an electron is accelerated through a potential difference of 1 volt. 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Electronvolts are convenient for expressing photon energies and work functions on the atomic scale.

Stopping potential (V_s) is the minimum potential difference required to reduce the photocurrent to zero. It is related to the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons by eV_s = ½mv²_max. The stopping potential depends on the frequency of the incident light, not its intensity.

Evidence for particle nature of light from the photoelectric effect includes: (1) the existence of a threshold frequency — the wave model predicts any frequency would work given sufficient intensity; (2) instantaneous emission — the wave model predicts a time delay at low intensities while energy accumulates; (3) maximum kinetic energy depends on frequency, not intensity — the wave model predicts intensity (amplitude) determines the energy transferred. These observations are explained by the photon model where E = hf and one photon interacts with one electron.

Exam connection: The photoelectric effect is the highest-yielding topic in Ch 2.3 (138 marks, avg 10.6/session, appears in EVERY paper). QWC questions follow the same IC + structure marking scheme: Jun 2019 Q14, Jan 2022 Q13, Oct 2023 Q17a, Jan 2025 Q19b (all 6 marks). The marking requires: (1) correct physics content points, (2) logical structure with connective words ("because", "therefore", "however"). The wave-vs-particle comparison trap (Oct 2023, Jun 2025): must state what the wave model predicts AND what the particle model explains — not just describe the particle model. Converting between eV and joules is essential (1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J) and appears in almost every photoelectric calculation.

13. Atomic Line Spectra (Spec 63)

Energy levels are the discrete, fixed energies that an electron in an atom can possess. An electron cannot have an energy between these allowed levels. Energy levels are negative because the electron is bound to the nucleus — the most negative value is the ground state and zero represents the ionised state (electron free from the atom).

Emission spectrum is the set of discrete wavelengths (or frequencies) of electromagnetic radiation emitted by excited atoms as their electrons transition from higher to lower energy levels. Each element produces a unique emission spectrum of sharp lines, because each element has a unique set of energy levels. The energy of an emitted photon equals the energy gap between the two levels: ΔE = hf = hc/λ.

Absorption spectrum is the continuous spectrum of white light with dark lines at specific wavelengths, produced when atoms absorb photons of exactly the right energy to promote electrons from lower to higher energy levels. The dark lines correspond to the same wavelengths as the bright lines in the emission spectrum for that element.

Electron transitions are movements of electrons between energy levels. When an electron moves from a higher level to a lower level (de-excitation), a photon is emitted with energy equal to the energy gap: ΔE = E_upper − E_lower = hf. When an electron absorbs a photon of energy equal to the gap between its current level and a higher level, it moves to that higher level (excitation). The longest wavelength of emitted radiation corresponds to the smallest energy gap (transition between adjacent levels closest together), because ΔE = hc/λ means larger ΔE gives smaller λ.

Exam connection: Atomic line spectra has yielded 56 marks (avg 4.3/session). The longest-wavelength trap appeared in Oct 2022 Q2: longest wavelength = lowest frequency = smallest ΔE. Arrow length on an energy level diagram is proportional to energy, NOT to wavelength — a longer arrow means shorter wavelength. Line spectra photon emission in Jun 2025 Q18a (2 marks): excitation → de-excitation → photon emitted. When calculating ΔE, always use ΔE = E_upper − E_lower (result is positive for emission). When converting to wavelength: λ = hc/ΔE. Remember that h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, and energy levels may be given in eV (convert to joules first).

Scope: Spec items 48 (measuring the refractive index of a solid), 50 (diffraction and Huygens' construction), and 55 (transmission and reflection of waves at an interface). These three items were identified as qualitative gaps in the WPH12 audit: Huygens' construction has only 2 brief mentions in ch2.3, measuring refractive index has only a passing mention, and transmission/reflection at an interface is never stated as a standalone concept. This extension fills those gaps with exam-ready definitions grounded in the Pearson Edexcel IAL Physics specification. Source: Pearson Edexcel IAL Physics Specification items 45–56; ch2.3 study guide; examiner reports Jan 2019–Jun 2025; WPH12/01 mark schemes. Purpose: Exam-ready definitions for every term in these three spec items, with explicit spec references and exam context drawn from past-paper mark schemes.

1. Huygens' Construction (Spec 50)

1.1 Wavefront

Spec ref: 33, 50 Definition: A line (or surface) joining all points on a wave that are in phase at the same instant — for example, every crest of a water wave, or every compression of a sound wave. In a diagram, wavefronts are usually drawn as equally-spaced parallel lines, with the separation equal to one wavelength. Exam context: Foundational to Huygens' construction and to diffraction explanations. Examiners expect students to identify wavefronts on a diagram and to recognise that the direction of wave travel is perpendicular to the wavefront.

1.2 Huygens' Construction

Spec ref: 50 Definition: A geometrical method for predicting the future position of a wavefront. The principle states that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets that spread out forwards at the wave speed; the new wavefront is the envelope (tangent surface) of these secondary wavelets after a time interval Δt. Exam context: Required when explaining reflection, refraction, and diffraction qualitatively. Examiner mark schemes reward the two underlined phrases verbatim — "secondary wavelets" and "envelope". Vague phrasing like "the wavefront moves forward" does not score.

1.3 Secondary Wavelet

Spec ref: 50 Definition: A small spherical (or circular, in 2D) wavelet emitted by each point on a wavefront, travelling at the wave speed in the medium. The radius of each wavelet after time Δt is v × Δt, where v is the wave speed. The combined envelope of all secondary wavelets forms the new wavefront. Exam context: Mark schemes require the term "secondary wavelet" (or "secondary wavelets") when explaining Huygens' construction. Substituting "small waves" or "ripples" does not score.

1.4 Secondary Source

Spec ref: 50 Definition: Each point on a wavefront, considered as a source of secondary wavelets. Secondary sources are not physical objects — they are a mathematical construction. The term distinguishes them from the original (primary) source of the wave. Exam context: Appears in single-slit diffraction explanations: "each point across the slit acts as a secondary source". Examiner reports show students confuse secondary sources with the actual wave source; the construction is a model, not a physical reality.

1.5 Envelope (of wavelets)

Spec ref: 50 Definition: The common tangent (in 2D) or tangent surface (in 3D) drawn across the leading edges of all secondary wavelets after a time interval. This envelope is the new position of the wavefront. Exam context: Required when stating Huygens' construction. Mark schemes accept "tangent to the wavelets" or "joining the leading edges of the wavelets".

2. Diffraction (Spec 50)

2.1 Diffraction

Spec ref: 50 Definition: The spreading of a wave when it passes through a gap (slit) or around an obstacle. Diffraction is most pronounced when the gap width (or obstacle size) is comparable to the wavelength of the wave. Diffraction is a wave property — it cannot be explained by a particle model of light. Exam context: Foundational definition; examiners expect "spreading" as the key verb. "Bending" is accepted but "spreading" is preferred. The condition "gap ≈ wavelength" is the most-tested numerical relationship.

2.2 Single-Slit Diffraction

Spec ref: 50 Definition: The diffraction pattern produced when a wave passes through a single narrow slit. The pattern consists of a central maximum (bright, wide, twice the width of the secondary maxima) flanked by alternating secondary maxima and minima, with intensity decreasing rapidly away from the centre. Exam context: Examiner mark schemes reward the qualitative features (central maximum is brightest and widest; secondary maxima are dimmer and narrower). The angular width of the central maximum is approximately 2λ/a, where a is the slit width — narrower slit → wider pattern.

2.3 Obstacle Diffraction

Spec ref: 50 Definition: The bending of waves around the edges of an obstacle, so that the wave enters the region behind the obstacle that would be in "shadow" if light travelled only in straight lines. The amount of bending increases when the obstacle size is comparable to or smaller than the wavelength. Exam context: Tested via real-world contexts: sound waves diffracting around corners (audible λ ≈ 1 cm to 17 m, comparable to doorways), radio waves diffracting around hills (λ ≈ 1 km to 100 m), but light not diffracting around buildings (λ ≈ 500 nm, far smaller than buildings). Examiner reports show students must link the wavelength-to-obstacle-size ratio to the degree of diffraction.

2.4 Diffraction Pattern

Spec ref: 50, 51 Definition: The pattern of bright and dark regions (for light) or loud and quiet regions (for sound) produced when a wave diffracts and the diffracted wavelets interfere. The pattern results from superposition of secondary wavelets from different parts of the slit/obstacle. Exam context: Required when describing single-slit, double-slit, or grating patterns. Mark schemes reward "interference of secondary wavelets" as the cause.

2.5 Central Maximum

Spec ref: 50 Definition: The brightest, widest fringe at the centre of a single-slit diffraction pattern. It is twice as wide as the secondary maxima and contains most of the diffracted intensity. The central maximum occurs because all secondary wavelets from across the slit arrive in phase at the screen centre (zero path difference). Exam context: Examiner mark schemes reward "brightest and widest" or "central bright fringe, twice the width of others". Forgetting the "twice as wide" detail loses the second mark in 2-mark questions.

2.6 Secondary Maxima

Spec ref: 50 Definition: The smaller, dimmer bright fringes on either side of the central maximum in a single-slit diffraction pattern. Their intensity decreases rapidly with distance from the centre (the first secondary maximum has ~4.5% of the central intensity). They arise where secondary wavelets interfere constructively but with reduced amplitude. Exam context: Tested when contrasting single-slit patterns with double-slit patterns. In a single-slit pattern, secondary maxima are unequal in intensity; in a double-slit pattern (spec 39), the maxima are of equal intensity (modulated by the single-slit envelope).

2.7 Minimum (of a diffraction pattern)

Spec ref: 50 Definition: A dark fringe where secondary wavelets interfere destructively (path difference = (n + ½)λ, or equivalently, where wavelets from one half of the slit cancel wavelets from the other half). The first minimum of a single-slit pattern occurs at sin θ = λ/a. Exam context: The minima are equally spaced in angle (for small angles); the central maximum occupies twice the angular width. Examiner mark schemes reward this "twice-as-wide central maximum" point.

3. Measuring Refractive Index of a Solid (Spec 48)

3.1 Refractive Index (recap)

Spec ref: 45, 48 Definition: The ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to the speed of light in the material: n = c/v. Refractive index is dimensionless and ≥ 1 for transparent materials. For light travelling from medium 1 to medium 2, Snell's law gives n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Exam context: Foundational. Tested in every paper. The measurement method (spec 48) uses Snell's law applied to a glass block.

3.2 Glass Block (rectangular)

Spec ref: 48 Definition: A rectangular block of transparent solid (typically glass, perspex, or acrylic) with parallel flat faces, used in the standard method to measure refractive index. The block is placed on a sheet of paper and a ray of light is directed at one face; the light refracts on entering and again on leaving. Exam context: The standard school-lab apparatus for spec 48. Examiner reports show students must state the block has "two parallel faces" so that the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray (the lateral shift makes the path measurable).

3.3 Ray Box

Spec ref: 48 Definition: A lamp housing with a single slit (or cylindrical lens) that produces a narrow, bright ray of light. The ray box is placed so the ray strikes the glass block at a measured angle of incidence. A coloured filter (typically red or green) is sometimes added to make the ray easier to mark. Exam context: The ray box is the standard light source for this practical. Alternative: a laser (much narrower ray, but safety considerations). Examiner reports show students must mention "narrow single ray" — a wide beam blurs the marked position.

3.4 Protractor

Spec ref: 48 Definition: A semicircular measuring instrument marked in degrees (0° to 180°), used to measure the angle of incidence i and the angle of refraction r between the ray and the normal at the point of incidence. The protractor's centre is placed exactly at the point where the ray meets the block face. Exam context: The protractor's resolution is ±1° (typical school protractor). The associated absolute uncertainty in each angle is ±1° (or ±0.5° for high-quality protractors). Examiner mark schemes accept this uncertainty in evaluated practical answers.

3.5 Normal (to a surface)

Spec ref: 45, 48 Definition: A line drawn perpendicular to the surface of the block at the point where the incident ray meets it. The angle of incidence and angle of refraction are both measured from the normal, not from the surface. Exam context: The most common error in this practical (and in refraction questions generally) is measuring the angle from the surface instead of from the normal. Examiner reports (Jan 2023, Jan 2025) explicitly flag the "angle from surface" trap — students must subtract the surface angle from 90°.

3.6 Angle of Incidence (i)

Spec ref: 45, 48 Definition: The angle between the incident ray and the normal, measured at the point where the ray meets the block. In the refractive index practical, the angle of incidence is the independent variable — the student varies i (typically 10°, 20°, 30°, 40°, 50°, 60°) and measures the corresponding r. Exam context: The student sets i deliberately. Examiner reports show students must use at least 5 different values of i to give a valid graph.

3.7 Angle of Refraction (r)

Spec ref: 45, 48 Definition: The angle between the refracted ray (inside the block) and the normal, measured at the point of incidence. As i increases, r increases but at a smaller rate (since sin i / sin r = n > 1 for light entering a denser medium). Exam context: The angle of refraction is the dependent variable — measured by the student. It is the smaller of the two angles when light enters a denser medium (ray bends towards the normal).

3.8 sin i vs sin r Graph

Spec ref: 48 Definition: A graph of sin i (y-axis) against sin r (x-axis) for the data collected in the refractive index practical. The graph is a straight line through the origin, and its gradient equals the refractive index n of the block (from sin i = n sin r, so sin i / sin r = n). Exam context: The gradient method is the standard analysis for spec 48. Examiner mark schemes reward: (1) axes labelled with sin i and sin r, (2) a straight line of best fit through the origin, (3) gradient calculation using a large triangle, (4) statement "gradient = n".

3.9 Lateral Displacement (lateral shift)

Spec ref: 48 Definition: The perpendicular distance between the direction of the incident ray and the direction of the emergent ray, measured after the light has passed through the glass block. Because the block has parallel faces, the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but shifted sideways. This shift is used to mark the emergent ray's position. Exam context: Mentioned in method questions. The shift is small (a few mm) for thin blocks; this is why the block is usually 1–2 cm thick — thick enough to mark the shift clearly.

4. Transmission and Reflection at an Interface (Spec 55)

4.1 Interface (boundary)

Spec ref: 55 Definition: The boundary between two media in which a wave can travel. At an interface, an incident wave is generally split into a reflected wave (returning into medium 1) and a transmitted wave (passing into medium 2). The relative proportions depend on the difference in wave speed (or impedance) between the two media. Exam context: Spec 55 requires the understanding that both reflection and transmission occur at most interfaces. Examiner mark schemes reward "both reflection and transmission" — students who describe only reflection or only transmission lose marks.

4.2 Transmission (at an interface)

Spec ref: 55 Definition: The passage of a wave from one medium into another. The transmitted wave changes speed (and usually wavelength, since v = fλ and f is fixed) at the interface. If the incidence is non-normal, the transmitted wave also changes direction (refraction). Exam context: Transmission is partial in most cases — only when the two media are perfectly impedance-matched (e.g. glass-to-glass of the same type) is transmission 100%. At a glass-air interface, ~4% of normally-incident light is reflected and ~96% is transmitted.

4.3 Reflection (at an interface)

Spec ref: 55 Definition: The return of a wave from a boundary back into the medium from which it came. The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence (both measured from the normal). Reflection occurs whenever there is a change in wave speed at the boundary. Exam context: Tested via the law of reflection (angle i = angle r) and via the qualitative statement "reflection occurs because wave speed changes at the boundary". Examiner mark schemes reward this cause-and-effect phrasing.

4.4 Partial Reflection

Spec ref: 55 Definition: The phenomenon by which only a fraction of the incident wave energy is reflected at the interface, with the remainder transmitted. Partial reflection occurs at every interface where there is an impedance mismatch (a change in wave speed) — it is not an "all-or-nothing" phenomenon. The fraction reflected increases with the size of the impedance mismatch. Exam context: Spec 55's central concept. Mark schemes reward "partial reflection" as the explicit term. Real-world example expected: looking through a window at night — you see both your own reflection (partial reflection) and the scene outside (transmission).

4.5 Impedance Mismatch

Spec ref: 55 (underlying concept) Definition: The difference in wave impedance (acoustic impedance Z = ρc for sound, or refractive index for light) between two media at an interface. A large mismatch → more reflection, less transmission. A small mismatch → less reflection, more transmission. A perfect match (e.g. two glass blocks of the same type pressed together) → 100% transmission, 0% reflection. Exam context: Tested in ultrasound contexts (spec 56) where acoustic impedance Z = ρc (units kg m⁻² s⁻¹) governs the reflected fraction: Ir / Ii = ((Z₂ − Z₁) / (Z₂ + Z₁))². Examiners expect "impedance mismatch" or "difference in acoustic impedance" as the cause of partial reflection.

4.6 Acoustic Impedance (Z)

Spec ref: 55, 56 Definition: A property of a medium that determines how much of a sound wave is reflected at an interface. Defined as Z = ρc, where ρ is the density of the medium and c is the speed of sound in the medium. SI unit: kg m⁻² s⁻¹ (also called the rayl). The larger the difference in Z between two media, the greater the fraction of the incident intensity that is reflected. Exam context: Tested in pulse-echo/ultrasound questions. Examiner mark schemes reward the formula Z = ρc and the intensity reflection coefficient ((Z₂ − Z₁)/(Z₂ + Z₁))².

4.7 Critical Angle (relationship to reflection/transmission)

Spec ref: 47, 55 Definition: The angle of incidence (in the denser medium) at which the angle of refraction is 90° — i.e. the refracted ray travels along the interface. For angles of incidence below the critical angle, both reflection and transmission occur (partial reflection). For angles of incidence above the critical angle, only reflection occurs — this is total internal reflection (TIR). Exam context: The critical angle is the boundary between partial reflection (transmission still occurs) and total internal reflection (no transmission). sin C = n₂/n₁. Examiner mark schemes reward the conceptual point that TIR is the extreme case of reflection at an interface — not a separate phenomenon.

4.8 Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

Spec ref: 47, 55 Definition: The complete reflection of a wave back into the denser medium when the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle. No wave is transmitted across the interface in this regime. Conditions: (1) light must be travelling from a denser to a less dense medium (n₁ > n₂), and (2) the angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle. Exam context: Tested in optical fibre questions and in TIR prediction questions. Both conditions must be stated. Examiner reports show the most common error is stating only one of the two conditions.

4.9 Optical Fibre

Spec ref: 47, 48, 55 Definition: A thin transparent strand (typically glass) that carries light along its length by total internal reflection at the boundary between the core and the cladding. The core has a higher refractive index than the cladding (n_core > n_cladding), so light entering the fibre at small enough angles undergoes TIR repeatedly at the core-cladding interface, staying within the core. Exam context: The classic application of TIR (spec 47) and of transmission/reflection at an interface (spec 55). Examiner mark schemes reward: (1) n_core > n_cladding, (2) "light undergoes TIR at the core-cladding boundary", (3) the role of cladding in protecting the core and in reducing pulse broadening.

4.10 Cladding (of an optical fibre)

Spec ref: 47, 55 Definition: A layer of lower-refractive-index glass (or plastic) surrounding the core of an optical fibre. The cladding serves three purposes: (1) it provides a clean, controlled boundary for TIR (rather than the core-air boundary, which could be scratched or contaminated); (2) it protects the core from physical damage and from cross-talk between adjacent fibres; (3) it sets a higher critical angle than the core-air boundary, which reduces multipath (modal) dispersion and pulse broadening. Exam context: Tested in optical fibre QWC questions. Examiner mark schemes reward "lower refractive index than core" and "prevents cross-talk / reduces pulse broadening".

4.11 Pulse-Echo Technique

Spec ref: 56 (application of spec 55) Definition: A method of determining the distance to a boundary between two media by emitting a short pulse of waves (ultrasound or radio) and detecting the echo reflected from the boundary. The distance is d = (v × t) / 2, where v is the wave speed in the medium and t is the time between emitting the pulse and detecting the echo. The factor of ½ is because the pulse travels to the boundary and back. Exam context: Tested every paper (14 marks total, 14/14 papers). The factor-of-2 trap is the most common error — forgetting to divide by 2 doubles the calculated distance. Examiner mark schemes reward d = vt/2 and the explicit "the pulse travels there and back" reasoning.

4.12 Sonar

Spec ref: 56 (application of spec 55) Definition: A pulse-echo technique using sound waves (typically ultrasound in water, where c ≈ 1500 m s⁻¹) to detect objects underwater — for example, to determine the depth of the sea floor, to locate shoals of fish, or to detect submarines. A short pulse of ultrasound is emitted; the time delay for the echo gives the distance via d = vt/2. Exam context: Tested as an application of pulse-echo. Examiner mark schemes reward: (1) ultrasound is used (not audible sound, which would disturb marine life and not penetrate far); (2) the factor of ½ in d = vt/2; (3) the wave speed is the speed of sound in water (~1500 m s⁻¹), not in air (~340 m s⁻¹).

4.13 Ultrasound Scanning (medical)

Spec ref: 56 (application of spec 55) Definition: A medical pulse-echo technique using ultrasound (frequencies above 20 kHz, typically 2–10 MHz in medical use). A transducer emits short pulses; each boundary between tissues of different acoustic impedances reflects a fraction of the pulse back. The intensity and timing of each reflected pulse are used to build an image. A coupling gel is used between the transducer and the skin to prevent reflection at the air-skin interface (which would otherwise reflect ~99% of the pulse). Exam context: Tested in 14 papers (23 marks total). Examiner mark schemes reward: (1) Z = ρc, (2) the intensity reflection coefficient formula, (3) the role of coupling gel in preventing air-skin reflection, (4) the factor-of-2 in d = vt/2.

Chapter 2.3 — Waves and Particle Nature of Light: Concepts

Unit: WPH12 (International A-Level Physics, Unit 2) Specification Points: 33–63 Total historical marks: 727 across 13 papers (average 55.9 per session)

1. The Wave Equation and Progressive Waves (Spec 33–34)

1.1 Why v=fλ Is Central

The wave equation v = fλ appears in 6 papers for 27 marks — not because it is tested in isolation, but because it is the connective tissue between nearly every other topic in Ch 2.3. Refraction requires it (v changes, f stays constant, so λ must change). Stationary waves require it (same equation links length, tension, and frequency). Diffraction gratings require it (wavelength determines angle). The equation is universal: it applies to all progressive waves — transverse and longitudinal, mechanical and electromagnetic.

1.2 How Progressive Waves Transfer Energy vs Store It

A progressive wave transfers energy through a medium (or through a vacuum for EM waves). The disturbance moves from source to observer; each particle oscillates about its equilibrium and then returns to rest. No particle travels with the wave — only the energy does. This contrasts with stationary waves, where energy is stored between nodes and antinodes and does not propagate. The key distinction: in a progressive wave, the wavefront advances; in a stationary wave, the pattern is fixed and the amplitude oscillates in place.

1.3 Worked Example — v=fλ in a Refraction Context

Question: Light of frequency 5.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz travels from air into glass of refractive index 1.52. Calculate the wavelength of the light in the glass. (Style of Jan 2022 Q16)

Method:

  • Speed in air: v_air = c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
  • Wavelength in air: λ_air = v/f = (3.00 × 10⁸) / (5.5 × 10¹⁴) = 5.45 × 10⁻⁷ m
  • Speed in glass: v_glass = c/n = (3.00 × 10⁸) / 1.52 = 1.97 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
  • Frequency is unchanged at the boundary (property of the source), so:
  • λ_glass = v_glass / f = (1.97 × 10⁸) / (5.5 × 10¹⁴) = 3.59 × 10⁻⁷ m
  • Alternatively: λ_glass = λ_air / n = (5.45 × 10⁻⁷) / 1.52 = 3.59 × 10⁻⁷ m ✓

1.4 Connection to Other Concepts

The wave equation connects to: refraction (v changes → λ changes, f constant, Oct 2023 Q18), stationary waves (same v = fλ but applied with boundary conditions, Jan 2025 Q17c), diffraction gratings (nλ = d sin θ contains λ from v = fλ), and the de Broglie equation (λ = h/p, where v = fλ still holds for matter waves).

2. Longitudinal vs Transverse Waves — The Graph Trap (Spec 35–37)

2.1 Why the Displacement-Zero Trap Costs Marks

The displacement-zero trap has appeared in 4 papers (Oct 2019, Jan 2022, Jun 2022, Oct 2022) and consistently costs 1–2 marks. Students assume that at a compression (maximum pressure) the molecular displacement must be maximum. This is wrong: at the centre of a compression, molecules have arrived at their closest spacing but their instantaneous displacement from equilibrium is zero — they passed through maximum displacement a quarter-cycle earlier and have returned to their equilibrium position while other molecules converged on them. The same is true at a rarefaction: displacement is zero, pressure is minimum.

2.2 How Displacement, Pressure, and Density Relate in Longitudinal Waves

For a sound wave travelling to the right:

The displacement leads the pressure variation by 90° (π/2 rad). On a displacement–distance graph for a longitudinal wave, the amplitude is the maximum displacement from the equilibrium line (zero line), and zero crossings correspond to compressions and rarefactions, NOT to zero pressure.

2.3 Worked Example — Interpreting a Displacement–Distance Graph for a Sound Wave

Question: A displacement–distance graph for a sound wave shows a sinusoidal curve with amplitude 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m and wavelength 0.85 m. A point P is at a position of maximum positive displacement on the graph. Describe the pressure and density at point P, and at a point Q that is λ/4 to the right of P. (Style of Oct 2019 Q18)

Method:

  • At point P (displacement maximum, positive): molecules have moved furthest in the direction of travel, creating a region of lower density behind them. Pressure is approximately normal (P is at the midpoint between compression and rarefaction in terms of pressure).
  • At point Q (λ/4 ahead, displacement zero, negative gradient): molecules are converging at Q, which means Q is at the centre of a compression. Pressure is maximum, density is maximum, but displacement is zero.
  • Key point: zero displacement does NOT mean normal conditions. At Q, displacement is zero but pressure and density are at their maximum values.

2.4 Connection to Other Concepts

Longitudinal wave properties connect to: stationary wave graphs (Jan 2025 Q17a shows the envelope of maximum displacement, not instantaneous displacement), polarisation (only transverse waves can be polarised — longitudinal waves like sound cannot, Jun 2025 Q12b(i)), and the pulse-echo technique (ultrasound is longitudinal, so compression/rarefaction concepts apply to resolution).

3. Coherence, Superposition, and Interference (Spec 39–40)

3.1 Why Coherence Is Required for Stable Patterns

Coherence has been tested in 8 papers for a combined 35 marks. The reason is that interference patterns are only observable and stable when two conditions are met simultaneously: the sources must have the same frequency and a constant phase difference. If the phase difference drifts (incoherent sources), the positions of constructive and destructive interference shift randomly, washing out the pattern into uniform illumination. This is why two independent light sources cannot produce a stable interference pattern — their phase relationship changes randomly on a timescale of ~10⁻⁸ s. A single source split by double slits (Young's experiment) automatically produces coherent sources.

3.2 How Path Difference and Phase Difference Link

The bridge between the spatial measurement (path difference) and the oscillation phase (phase difference) is:

Phase difference (rad) = (2π / λ) × path difference

For a double-slit experiment, the fringe spacing is given by w = λD / s, where D is the slit-to-screen distance and s is the slit separation. This formula itself is an approximation derived from the path difference condition, valid when D >> s.

3.3 Worked Example — Two-Slit Interference: Finding Fringe Spacing

Question: Light of wavelength 590 nm passes through a double slit with slit separation 0.40 mm onto a screen 1.2 m away. Calculate the fringe spacing. (Style of Oct 2022 Q15)

Method:

  • λ = 590 nm = 590 × 10⁻⁹ m
  • s = 0.40 mm = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m
  • D = 1.2 m
  • w = λD / s = (590 × 10⁻⁹ × 1.2) / (4.0 × 10⁻⁴)
  • w = 7.08 × 10⁻⁷ / 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.77 × 10⁻³ m = 1.8 mm

If asked for the number of bright fringes in a 5.0 cm-wide screen: N = 50 mm / 1.77 mm ≈ 28 fringes.

Coherence and interference connect to: beats (changing phase difference between slightly different frequencies, Oct 2022 Q15a(ii) — a 6-mark QWC), diffraction gratings (many slits produce sharper maxima because more coherent sources contribute), stationary waves (superposition of two waves travelling in opposite directions), and harbour/water wave diffraction (Jan 2023 Q15a, Jan 2024 Q17b — both QWC questions on interference through gaps).

4.2 How Standing Waves Form: Node and Antinode Positions

A stationary wave forms when a progressive wave reflects from a boundary and superposes with the incident wave. The two waves (same frequency, same amplitude, opposite direction) interfere to produce fixed points of permanent cancellation (nodes, separation λ/2) and fixed points of maximum oscillation (antinodes, midway between nodes).

4.3 Worked Example — Fundamental Frequency of a String

Question: A string of length 0.65 m and mass per unit length 3.2 × 10⁻³ kg m⁻¹ is under a tension of 18 N. Calculate the fundamental frequency. (Style of Jun 2024 Q8)

Method:

  • Wave speed on string: v = √(T/μ) = √(18 / 3.2 × 10⁻³) = √(5625) = 75 m s⁻¹
  • For fundamental mode on a string fixed at both ends: λ = 2L = 2 × 0.65 = 1.30 m
  • f₁ = v/λ = 75 / 1.30 = 57.7 Hz ≈ 58 Hz

4.4 Worked Example — Derivation of f² = T/(4L²μ)

Question: Derive the expression f² = T/(4L²μ) for the fundamental frequency of a string fixed at both ends. (Jan 2025 Q17c(i), 3 marks)

Method:

  • Fundamental mode: one half-wavelength fits on the string, so λ = 2L
  • From the wave equation: v = fλ = f × 2L, therefore f = v/(2L)
  • Wave speed on a string: v = √(T/μ)
  • Substituting: f = √(T/μ) / (2L)
  • Squaring both sides: f² = T/(μ × 4L²) = T/(4L²μ) ✓

5. Intensity and the Inverse Square Law (Spec 44)

5.1 Why Intensity Appears in 7 Papers (22 Marks)

Intensity (I = P/A) is a high-yield topic because it bridges the wave and particle chapters. In the wave context, I ∝ A² links amplitude to energy. In the particle context, intensity = photon rate × energy per photon, so higher intensity means more photons per second but NOT more energy per photon. This dual role makes intensity ideal for multi-step questions that test whether students understand the difference between wave and photon models.

5.2 How I = P/A and I ∝ 1/r² Work Together; the cos θ Solar Panel Trap

For a point source radiating equally in all directions, the power P spreads over a sphere of surface area A = 4πr². Therefore I = P/(4πr²), giving I ∝ 1/r² — the inverse square law. When radiation hits a surface at angle θ from the normal, only the perpendicular component contributes: effective intensity = I cos θ. The effective receiving area of the panel becomes A cos θ.

The cos θ trap (Oct 2023 Q16c, 4 marks): a solar panel at 60° to the normal receives only half the intensity. Students who forget the cos factor or confuse angle from the normal with angle from the surface lose marks.

5.3 Worked Example — Solar Panel with cos 60° Angle

Question: Solar radiation of intensity 850 W m⁻² is incident on a horizontal panel of diameter 0.30 m. The panel is tilted so the normal makes an angle of 60° to the direction of the radiation. Calculate the power received by the panel. (Oct 2023 Q16c style, 4 marks)

Method:

  • Panel area: A = πr² = π × (0.15)² = π × 0.0225 = 0.0707 m²
  • Effective area perpendicular to radiation: A_eff = A cos 60° = 0.0707 × 0.500 = 0.0354 m²
  • Power received: P = I × A_eff = 850 × 0.0354 = 30.1 W ≈ 30 W
  • Common error: using diameter (0.30 m) instead of radius (0.15 m) in the area calculation.

5.4 Connection to Other Concepts

Intensity connects to: amplitude (I ∝ A² — doubling amplitude quadruples intensity, Jan 2022 Q13), the photoelectric effect (intensity = rate of photons, not energy per photon — Spec 58–60), optical fibres (intensity loss at TIR boundaries, Jan 2025 Q18c), and pulse-echo (intensity attenuates with distance due to the inverse square law and absorption).

6. Refraction, Snell's Law, and Total Internal Reflection (Spec 45–48)

6.1 Why This Is the #2 Highest-Yield Topic

With 109 marks across 10+ papers (avg 8.4 per session), refraction and TIR are the second most tested topic in the entire paper. The topic combines a mathematical formula (Snell's law) with conceptual understanding (why light bends, why TIR occurs), practical measurement (determining refractive index), and application (optical fibres). The Jan 2025 Q18 optical fibre block (13 marks) and Oct 2023 Q18 refraction/TIR block (14 marks) show that examiners can build 10+ mark mega-questions from this topic alone.

6.2 How the Angle-from-Surface Trap Works

The angle-from-surface trap has appeared in 7 of 13 papers — more frequently than any other single trap. On ray diagrams, angles are often measured from the surface, not from the normal. Since Snell's law uses angles from the normal, the conversion is: θ_normal = 90° − θ_surface. In Jan 2025 Q18a, the given angle is 50° from the surface, so θ_normal = 90° − 50° = 40°. Students who use 50° directly in Snell's law get a wrong answer.

6.3 How Optical Fibre TIR Causes Pulse Broadening

In a multimode optical fibre, light enters at various angles. Rays at steeper angles travel a longer zigzag path than axial rays, so they take longer to traverse the fibre. This causes pulse broadening — the received pulse is wider (longer duration) than the transmitted pulse. If pulses are sent too rapidly, the broadened pulses overlap and information is lost. TIR at the core–cladding boundary also causes a small fraction of energy to leak into the cladding (evanescent wave), reducing intensity. (Jan 2025 Q18c, 5 marks)

6.4 Worked Example — Applying Snell's Law with Angle from Surface

Question: A ray of light travels from glass (n = 1.50) to air. The angle of incidence at the boundary is given as 50° measured from the surface. Determine whether total internal reflection occurs and calculate the angle of refraction or reflection. (Jan 2025 Q18a style)

Method:

  • Convert angle from surface to angle from normal: θ₁ = 90° − 50° = 40°
  • Critical angle: sin C = 1/n = 1/1.50 = 0.667, so C = 41.8°
  • Since θ₁ = 40° < C = 41.8°, TIR does NOT occur
  • Apply Snell's law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂
  • 1.50 × sin 40° = 1.00 × sin θ₂
  • 1.50 × 0.643 = sin θ₂
  • sin θ₂ = 0.964
  • θ₂ = 74.6° ≈ 75°

Note: the angle from the surface was close to the critical angle from the normal, which is a deliberate exam design to test the conversion.

6.5 Connection to Other Concepts

Refraction connects to: the wave equation (n = c/v, so v = c/n — speed changes at boundary), the critical angle (sin C = n₂/n₁ for TIR, Oct 2022 Q16a tested critical angle with cladding), intensity (cos θ factor, Section 5), and optical fibres (multimode dispersion and signal degradation, Jan 2025 Q18c).

7. Plane Polarisation (Spec 49)

7.1 Why Polarisation Appears in 7 Papers (18 Marks)

Plane polarisation is tested frequently because it is the only property that distinguishes transverse waves from longitudinal waves. Only transverse waves can be polarised — this is a definitive test for wave type. The topic combines definition recall, calculation (Malus's law), and conceptual reasoning in a compact package. The polarisation definition (Jun 2025 Q12b(i), 3 marks) requires the exact phrase "perpendicular to the direction of travel/propagation" — omitting the direction qualification loses a mark.

7.2 How the Single-Filter Trap Works

The single-filter trap has appeared in 4 papers (Jun 2019, Oct 2019, Jun 2022, Oct 2023). When unpolarised light passes through a single polarising filter, the transmitted intensity is always exactly 50% of the incident intensity, regardless of the filter's orientation. This is because the filter selects one of the two equal perpendicular components of the unpolarised light. Malus's law (I = I₀ cos²θ) only applies when a second filter (analyser) is placed after the first, where θ is the angle between the two transmission axes.

7.3 Worked Example — Two Polarising Filters at 45°

Question: Unpolarised light of intensity 120 W m⁻² passes through two polarising filters. The transmission axis of the second filter is at 45° to the first. Calculate the intensity transmitted through both filters. (Style of Oct 2019 Q18)

Method:

  • After first filter: I₁ = I₀/2 = 120/2 = 60 W m⁻²
  • After second filter (Malus's law): I₂ = I₁ cos²θ = 60 × cos²45°
  • cos 45° = 0.707, so cos²45° = 0.500
  • I₂ = 60 × 0.500 = 30 W m⁻²

8. Diffraction and the Diffraction Grating (Spec 50–52)

8.1 Why the Grating Is #4 Highest Yield

The diffraction grating (nλ = d sin θ) has produced 68 marks across 11 papers (avg 5.2 per session). The grating is favoured because it tests a complete chain: geometry (finding θ from distances using tan θ = x/D), trigonometry (converting tan θ to sin θ), algebra (rearranging nλ = d sin θ), and unit conversion (d = 1/(lines per metre)). The grating is the most calculationally dense topic in the wave section, and errors at any step cascade.

8.2 How nλ = d sin θ Works — The Full Chain

The standard calculation chain in an exam question:

  1. Measure the distance x from the central maximum to the nth-order maximum on the screen
  2. Find the angle: tan θ = x/D, so θ = arctan(x/D) — but for nλ = d sin θ you need sin θ, not tan θ
  3. Convert to sin: for small angles, sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ (in radians). For larger angles, use sin(arctan(x/D)) directly
  4. Calculate d: d = 1/N where N = lines per metre. If given "300 lines per mm", then N = 300 × 10³ = 3.00 × 10⁵ lines per metre, so d = 1/(3.00 × 10⁵) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m
  5. Apply the grating equation: nλ = d sin θ, so λ = d sin θ / n
  6. Maximum order: set θ = 90° → n_max = d/λ, then round DOWN to the nearest integer

8.3 The d vs 1/d Trap

The d vs 1/d trap appeared in Oct 2022 Q18c and Jun 2023. The variable d is the slit spacing (distance between adjacent slits), NOT the number of lines per metre. Common mistake: using the number directly (e.g., d = 300,000 instead of d = 1/300,000). The conversion is:

8.4 Worked Example — Finding Wavelength from Grating Data

Question: A diffraction grating with 400 lines per mm is used with light. The first-order maximum is observed at an angle of 18.5° from the normal. Calculate the wavelength of the light and the maximum number of orders visible. (Jan 2025 Q14b(i) style, 4 marks)

Method:

  • d = 1/(400 × 10³) = 1/(4.00 × 10⁵) = 2.50 × 10⁻⁶ m
  • nλ = d sin θ → λ = d sin θ / n = (2.50 × 10⁻⁶ × sin 18.5°) / 1
  • sin 18.5° = 0.3173
  • λ = 2.50 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.3173 = 7.93 × 10⁻⁷ m = 793 nm
  • Maximum order: n_max = d/λ = (2.50 × 10⁻⁶) / (7.93 × 10⁻⁷) = 3.15 → round down to n = 3

8.5 Connection to Other Concepts

Diffraction connects to: Huygens' construction (each slit acts as a secondary wavelet source), coherence (all slits on a grating are coherent because they are illuminated by the same source), the wave equation (λ appears in nλ = d sin θ), and electron diffraction (Spec 53 — electrons also diffract, but through crystal lattice spacing rather than a grating).

9. Wave-Particle Duality and de Broglie (Spec 53–54)

9.1 Why Electron Diffraction Is the Key Evidence

Electron diffraction has appeared in 4 papers (Oct 2022 Q11, Jun 2023, Jun 2024, Jun 2025) and is the definitive evidence for the wave nature of matter. When a beam of electrons is directed at a thin crystal, a diffraction pattern of concentric rings is observed. This pattern (with constructive and destructive interference) cannot be explained by a particle model — it requires wave behaviour. The crystal lattice acts as a diffraction grating with spacing ~10⁻¹⁰ m, which is why visible light (λ ~ 10⁻⁷ m) cannot produce the same pattern — its wavelength is far too large.

9.2 How λ = h/p Connects Wave and Particle Properties

The de Broglie equation λ = h/p unifies wave and particle descriptions. A particle with momentum p = mv has an associated wavelength λ = h/(mv). For electrons accelerated through potential difference V:

  • Kinetic energy gained: eV = ½mv²
  • Velocity: v = √(2eV/m)
  • Momentum: p = mv = √(2meV)
  • de Broglie wavelength: λ = h/√(2meV)

9.3 Worked Example — de Broglie Wavelength of an Electron at 5000 V

Question: Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 5000 V. (Jun 2025 Q15b(i) style)

Method:

  • eV = ½mv² → v = √(2eV/m)
  • e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, V = 5000 V, m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg
  • 2eV/m = 2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 5000 / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹
  • 2eV/m = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 1.756 × 10¹⁵
  • v = √(1.756 × 10¹⁵) = 4.19 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹
  • p = mv = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 4.19 × 10⁷ = 3.82 × 10⁻²³ kg m s⁻¹
  • λ = h/p = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / 3.82 × 10⁻²³ = 1.74 × 10⁻¹¹ m
  • This is comparable to crystal lattice spacing (~10⁻¹⁰ m), explaining why electrons diffract through crystals. At θ = 0.001° (Jun 2025 Q15b(ii)), the diffraction angle through a visible-light grating (d ~ 10⁻⁶ m) is tiny because d >> λ. Significant diffraction requires d ≈ λ, which is why crystal lattices (d ~ 10⁻¹⁰ m) work but visible-light gratings do not.

9.4 Connection to Other Concepts

De Broglie connects to: the photoelectric effect (particle evidence for light, Spec 60–62 — duality works both ways), diffraction (wave evidence for electrons, Spec 50), E = hf (photon momentum p = E/c = hf/c = h/λ, showing photons also obey λ = h/p), and atomic spectra (electrons in atoms have quantised wavelengths corresponding to discrete energy levels).

10. Pulse-Echo Technique (Spec 55–56)

10.1 Why Pulse-Echo Is a Staple

The pulse-echo technique has appeared in 7+ papers for 23 marks, with a remarkably consistent structure: (1) calculate distance using d = vt/2, (2) explain why ultrasound frequency must be high (short wavelength for resolution), (3) determine which of several cracks can or cannot be detected. This triple-structure question tests calculation, conceptual understanding, and application in a single block.

10.2 How the Factor-of-2 Trap Works

The pulse travels to the reflector and back, so the round-trip distance is 2d. Given round-trip time t: d = vt/2. The factor-of-2 trap has appeared in 6+ papers (Jun 2019, Oct 2019, Jan 2023, Jun 2023, Oct 2023, Jun 2024). The error is using d = vt (one-way distance formula applied to a round-trip time), which gives exactly double the correct answer. In "show that" questions, the given answer is always based on d = vt/2 — if the student gets double, they cannot "show" the stated value.

10.3 How Resolution Limit Works — The Counterintuitive Trap

The resolution limit trap (Jun 2025 Q13a) is counterintuitive: a closer crack may NOT be detected. The reason is that the ultrasound pulse has a finite duration τ. Two boundaries can only be distinguished if the time between their returning echoes exceeds τ. If the round-trip time to a crack is shorter than the pulse duration, the echo from the crack arrives while the transmitted pulse is still being emitted — the two signals overlap and the crack is invisible on the display.

ConditionResult
2d/v > τ (round-trip time > pulse duration)Crack IS detected (echo separates from pulse)
2d/v < τ (round-trip time < pulse duration)Crack is NOT detected (echo hidden within pulse)
2d/v = τBorderline — just resolvable

Shorter pulses give better resolution (can detect closer boundaries) but carry less energy (weaker echoes). There is always a trade-off.

10.4 Worked Example — Ultrasound Pulse-Echo Crack Detection

Question: Ultrasound of speed 5800 m s⁻¹ is used to inspect a metal block. The pulse duration is 1.5 μs. There are cracks at depths of 4.0 mm and 10 mm. Determine which cracks can be detected. (Jun 2025 Q13a style)

Method:

  • Minimum detectable distance: d_min = vτ/2 = (5800 × 1.5 × 10⁻⁶) / 2 = 4.35 × 10⁻³ m = 4.35 mm
  • Crack at 4.0 mm: round-trip time = 2 × 4.0 × 10⁻³ / 5800 = 1.38 × 10⁻⁶ s = 1.38 μs < 1.5 μs → NOT detected
  • Crack at 10 mm: round-trip time = 2 × 10 × 10⁻³ / 5800 = 3.45 × 10⁻⁶ s = 3.45 μs > 1.5 μs → detected
  • The 4.0 mm crack is CLOSER but cannot be detected — this is the counterintuitive result.

10.5 Connection to Other Concepts

Pulse-echo connects to: wave speed (v must be known for the medium), intensity (signal attenuates with depth, reducing echo strength), resolution (shorter wavelength → higher frequency → better resolution, but higher absorption), and the inverse square law (echo intensity decreases with distance, requiring amplification for deep flaws).

11.3 Worked Example — Complete Photoelectric Calculation

Question: A metal surface has a work function of 2.3 eV. Light of frequency 8.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz is incident on the surface. Method:

  • KE_max = hf − φ = 3.32 − 2.30 = 1.02 eV
  • (b) Convert KE_max to joules: 1.02 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.63 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
  • (c) Stopping potential: eV_s = KE_max → V_s = KE_max / e = 1.02 V

12.2 How Emission and Absorption Spectra Relate to Energy Levels

Emission spectrum: Electrons in excited atoms drop to lower energy levels, emitting photons with energy ΔE = E_upper − E_lower. Each transition produces a photon of a specific frequency, giving sharp lines. The full spectrum of all possible transitions gives a unique "fingerprint" for each element.

Absorption spectrum: White light passes through cool gas. Photons with energies exactly matching transitions between the ground state and higher levels are absorbed. The transmitted light shows dark lines at those wavelengths — the same wavelengths as the bright lines in the emission spectrum.

12.3 The Longest-Wavelength Trap

The longest-wavelength trap (Oct 2022 Q2): longest wavelength corresponds to the smallest energy gap (transition between adjacent levels closest together), because λ = hc/ΔE means larger ΔE gives smaller λ. Students who confuse arrow length on energy level diagrams with wavelength get this wrong — a longer arrow means larger ΔE means shorter λ.

n=4 → n=1 (large ΔE)LargestShortestHighest

12.4 Worked Example — Calculating Photon Wavelength from Energy Level Transition

Question: An electron in a hydrogen atom transitions from the n = 3 energy level (−1.51 eV) to the n = 2 energy level (−3.40 eV). Calculate the wavelength of the emitted photon. (Style of Jun 2025 Q18a)

Method:

  • ΔE = E_upper − E_lower = (−1.51) − (−3.40) = 1.89 eV
  • Convert to joules: ΔE = 1.89 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.02 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
  • λ = hc / ΔE = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) / (3.02 × 10⁻¹⁹)
  • λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / 3.02 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 6.59 × 10⁻⁷ m = 659 nm (red light)

12.5 Connection to Other Concepts

Atomic spectra connect to: E = hf and E = hc/λ (photon energy, Spec 58), the photoelectric effect (both involve photon energy and the quantised nature of light, Spec 60), de Broglie wavelength (electrons in atoms have quantised standing wave patterns corresponding to discrete energy levels, Spec 54), and electronvolts (energy levels are almost always given in eV, requiring conversion to joules for calculations, Spec 61).

13.5 Template: Photoelectric QWC (Spec 62, 6 Marks)

The photoelectric effect has produced 4 QWC questions (Jun 2019 Q14, Jan 2022 Q13, Oct 2023 Q17a, Jun 2025 Q19b). The required structure:

  1. Wave model prediction: The wave model predicts that any frequency of light should eventually eject electrons because energy accumulates over time, and that higher intensity should give electrons more kinetic energy because the wave amplitude is greater.
  2. Contradiction: However, no electrons are emitted below the threshold frequency regardless of intensity, which means energy does not accumulate.
  3. Particle model explanation: The photon model explains this because each photon interacts with one electron in a one-to-one interaction, so the photon must have energy hf ≥ φ.
  4. Intensity role: Increasing intensity increases the number of photons per second, therefore more electrons are emitted per second (greater photocurrent), but the maximum kinetic energy depends only on frequency, not intensity.
  5. Instantaneous emission: Emission is instantaneous, because the entire photon energy is transferred in a single interaction, which contradicts the wave model prediction of a time delay.

13.6 Template: Refraction QWC (Spec 45–47, 6 Marks)

The refraction/TIR QWC (Oct 2024 Q17b, underwater paths) requires:

  1. Speed change: Light travels slower in the denser medium because n = c/v, so the wavelength decreases while frequency stays constant.
  2. Direction change: The part of the wavefront entering the denser medium first slows down, causing the wavefront to rotate, which means the ray bends towards the normal.
  3. TIR conditions: For TIR to occur, light must travel from a denser to a less dense medium, and the angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle, because sin C = n₂/n₁.
  4. Application consequence: In an optical fibre, rays at steep angles undergo TIR at the core-cladding boundary, so they remain within the fibre, but different angles produce different path lengths, therefore causing pulse broadening.

13.7 Template: Stationary Wave QWC (Spec 41, 6 Marks)

The stationary wave QWC (Jun 2025 Q17a, closed tube) requires:

  1. Wave generation: A progressive wave travels down the tube, and reflects from the closed end.
  2. Phase change at boundary: The reflected wave is inverted (180° phase change) at the fixed boundary, which means a node forms at the closed end.
  3. Superposition: The incident and reflected waves superpose, because the principle of superposition states that the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements.
  4. Node/antinode pattern: This creates nodes where the waves cancel (destructive interference) and antinodes where they reinforce (constructive interference), so the pattern has a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end.
  5. Resonance: Stationary waves only form at specific frequencies where the boundary conditions are satisfied, because an integer number of quarter-wavelengths must fit in the tube length.

2. Huygens' Construction — Step-by-Step

2.1 The Principle (verbatim spec 50)

Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets. The new wavefront is the envelope of these secondary wavelets, after a time Δt, travelling at the wave speed.

2.2 Step-by-Step Construction (for a plane wavefront)

Consider a plane wavefront AB travelling left-to-right in a uniform medium. After a time Δt, the new wavefront is found as follows:

Step 1:  At time t = 0, draw the current wavefront AB as a vertical line.
         A●─────────────────●B
         ← wave travelling right →

Step 2:  Mark several points P₁, P₂, P₃, ... along AB. Each is a "secondary source".
         A●─P₁─P₂─P₃─P₄─P₅─●B

Step 3:  From each point, draw a small circle (wavelet) of radius r = v × Δt,
         where v is the wave speed in the medium.

         ●   ●   ●   ●   ●   ●
          \  |  /  \  /  \  |
           \ | /   \ /   \ |
            ●     ●     ●     (these are the wavelet circles' right edges)

Step 4:  Draw the common tangent (envelope) to all wavelets. This is the new wavefront A'B'.

         A'●─────────────────●B'

After time Δt, the wavefront has moved forward by distance v × Δt. The new wavefront A'B' is parallel to AB and one wavelength ahead (if Δt = T, the period).

2.3 Why the Wavelets Don't Go Backwards

A subtle point: each secondary source emits wavelets in all directions, including backwards. The principle (in its simplest form) includes an obliquity factor that suppresses the backward wavelet, leaving only the forward envelope. For WPH12, examiners do not require this detail — the forward envelope is sufficient.

2.4 Application 1: Reflection from a Plane Mirror

When a plane wavefront meets a plane mirror at angle θ to the mirror:

  1. The end A of the wavefront reaches the mirror first; point A becomes a secondary source emitting wavelets reflected back into the same medium.
  2. As the wavefront advances, successive points along AB reach the mirror and become secondary sources.
  3. By the time B reaches the mirror at B', the wavelet from A has expanded to a circle of radius AB' = BB'.
  4. The envelope of all reflected wavelets is the new wavefront A'B', making the same angle θ with the mirror — i.e. the law of reflection (angle of incidence = angle of reflection).

2.5 Application 2: Refraction (why light bends)

When a plane wavefront meets the boundary between two media at angle θ₁ (in the faster medium, e.g. air) the part of the wavefront that has entered the slower medium (e.g. glass) travels more slowly:

  1. End A enters the glass first → its wavelets travel at v₂ < v₁ (smaller radius after time Δt).
  2. End B is still in air → its wavelets travel at v₁ (larger radius).
  3. The envelope of the wavelets is therefore tilted — the wavefront has rotated, and the wave now travels at a smaller angle θ₂ to the normal in the slower medium.

This is the Huygens derivation of Snell's law: sin θ₁ / sin θ₂ = v₁ / v₂ = n₂ / n₁.

2.6 Application 3: Diffraction at a Slit (the key spec 50 application)

This is the central examinable application. Consider a plane wavefront meeting a slit of width a:

  1. Before the slit, the wavefront is a straight vertical line — all points on the wavefront emit secondary wavelets that interfere constructively only in the forward direction (the envelope is another straight line).
  2. At the slit, only the points within the slit opening can emit secondary wavelets into the region beyond the slit. The points blocked by the barrier contribute nothing.
  3. The unblocked secondary wavelets now spread out in all forward directions (not just the forward direction), because there is no longer a continuous wavefront to confine them.
  4. The envelope of these spreading wavelets is a curved wavefront — the wave has spread laterally beyond the geometric shadow of the slit.

This is diffraction: the spreading of a wave when it passes through a gap, because the secondary wavelets from the unblocked portion of the wavefront are no longer confined to the forward direction.

2.7 Why the Central Maximum is Brightest and Widest

On a screen placed beyond the slit, the diffracted wavelets interfere:

  • At the centre of the screen (directly opposite the slit), all wavelets from across the slit arrive having travelled approximately the same distance → zero path difference → constructive interference → central maximum (brightest).
  • At angles where the path difference between wavelets from the top of the slit and the bottom of the slit is λ (one wavelength), the wavelets interfere destructively (the top half of the slit cancels the bottom half) → first minimum: sin θ = λ / a.
  • Between the central maximum and the first minimum, intensity falls smoothly. Beyond the first minimum, weaker secondary maxima occur (where only some wavelets cancel), each dimmer than the central maximum.

Angular width of the central maximum = 2λ / a (radians, for small angles). Narrower slit → wider central maximum → more diffraction.

3. Single-Slit Diffraction — Worked Explanation via Huygens

3.1 The Setup

A monochromatic light source (e.g. a laser) illuminates a single narrow slit of width a (typically a ~ 0.1 mm). A screen is placed a distance D (typically ~1 m) beyond the slit. The pattern on the screen consists of:

  • A broad, bright central maximum at the centre.
  • Alternating dark and bright secondary fringes on either side, with intensity decreasing rapidly.

3.2 The Huygens Explanation (mark-scheme-ready)

Each point across the width of the slit acts as a secondary source of wavelets (Huygens' construction). All these wavelets interfere at the screen. The pattern is the result of this interference:

Position on screenPath difference (top of slit to bottom)InterferenceResult
Centre (θ = 0)0Fully constructiveCentral maximum (brightest)
First minimumλFully destructive (top half cancels bottom half)Dark fringe
First secondary maximum~3λ/2Partially constructiveDim bright fringe (~4.5% of central intensity)
Second minimum2λDestructiveDark fringe
Second secondary maximum~5λ/2Partially constructiveEven dimmer bright fringe

3.3 The First-Minimum Formula

The condition for the first minimum (the edge of the central maximum):

sin θ = λ / a

where θ is the angle from the central axis to the first minimum, λ is the wavelength, and a is the slit width. This is the most-tested single-slit formula in WPH12.

3.4 Worked Example: Red Laser Through a 0.10 mm Slit

A red laser (λ = 633 nm) passes through a slit of width a = 0.10 mm = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m. The screen is D = 2.0 m beyond the slit.

(a) Calculate the angle to the first minimum.

sin θ = λ / a = (6.33 × 10⁻⁷ m) / (1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m) = 6.33 × 10⁻³
θ = sin⁻¹(6.33 × 10⁻³) ≈ 0.36°

(b) Calculate the width of the central maximum on the screen.

The central maximum extends from the first minimum on the left to the first minimum on the right, so the total width is 2 × x, where x = D tan θ is the distance from the centre to the first minimum.

x = D × tan θ ≈ 2.0 × 6.33 × 10⁻³ = 1.27 × 10⁻² m = 1.27 cm
Width of central maximum = 2 × 1.27 cm ≈ 2.5 cm

(c) Predict the effect of halving the slit width.

Halving a doubles sin θ → doubles θ (for small angles) → doubles the width of the central maximum. The diffraction pattern spreads out more — narrower slit → more diffraction.

3.5 Worked Example: Why Longer Wavelengths Diffract More

The angular width of the central maximum is 2λ/a. So:

  • Sound diffracts around doorways (audible λ ~ 1 cm to 17 m, comparable to doorway width ~80 cm) but light does not (visible λ ~ 500 nm, far smaller than a doorway).
  • Long-wave radio (λ ~ 1500 m) diffracts over hills to reach receivers in valleys; VHF radio (λ ~ 3 m) does not — receivers need line-of-sight.
  • Low-frequency sounds (bass) carry around corners better than high-frequency sounds (treble).

4. Obstacle Diffraction (Spec 50)

4.1 The Phenomenon

When a wave meets an obstacle (rather than a slit), the secondary wavelets from the wavefront that passes the obstacle's edges spread into the geometric shadow region behind the obstacle. The wave "bends around" the obstacle.

4.2 Why It Happens (Huygens)

The obstacle blocks the wavefront over its width, but the parts of the wavefront passing either side of the obstacle are not blocked. Each unblocked portion emits secondary wavelets that spread out in all forward directions — including into the shadow region behind the obstacle. The envelope of these wavelets now has curved portions that wrap around the obstacle.

4.3 The Wavelength-to-Size Ratio

The amount of diffraction around an obstacle depends on the ratio λ / d, where d is the obstacle size:

λ / d ratioDiffraction behaviour
λ ≫ d (wavelength much larger)Wave diffracts almost completely around obstacle — almost no shadow region
λ ≈ d (comparable)Significant diffraction — partial shadow region behind obstacle
λ ≪ d (wavelength much smaller)Almost no diffraction — sharp geometric shadow behind obstacle

4.4 Real-World Examples (examiner-favoured)

  • Sound (λ ~ 1 m) diffracts around a person (d ~ 0.5 m) → you can hear someone speaking even if they are behind a wall. Light (λ ~ 500 nm) does not → you cannot see them.
  • Radio waves (λ ~ 100 m for AM) diffract around hills and buildings → AM reception works in valleys. FM radio (λ ~ 3 m) and TV (λ ~ 0.5 m) do not diffract as well → line-of-sight required.
  • Water waves (λ ~ 1 m) diffract around harbour walls (the harbour mouth acts as a slit) → waves spread into the harbour. This was a QWC question (Jan 2023 Q15a, 6 marks).
  • X-rays (λ ~ 10⁻¹⁰ m) diffract through crystal lattice spacing (d ~ 10⁻¹⁰ m) → crystal acts as a 3D diffraction grating (basis of X-ray crystallography).

5. Measuring Refractive Index of a Solid — Full Practical Method (Spec 48)

5.1 Apparatus

  • A rectangular glass (or perspex, or acrylic) block with two parallel flat faces.
  • A ray box with a single slit (to produce a narrow ray of light).
  • A sheet of plain paper on a flat surface (e.g. a piece of soft cardboard to allow pins to be pushed in).
  • 4 thin pins (or a sharp pencil to mark the ray positions).
  • A protractor (resolution ±1°).
  • A sharp pencil for marking positions.

5.2 The Method — Step by Step

  1. Place the block on the paper and trace around it with a pencil. Mark the position of the block on the paper so it can be replaced if disturbed.
  2. Draw a normal to one of the longer faces of the block, at a point near the middle of the face. The normal is perpendicular to the face — use a set square to ensure this.
  3. Set up the ray box to direct a narrow ray at the marked point, at an angle of incidence i of about 30° to the normal. (The angle is measured from the normal, not from the surface.)
  4. Mark the incident ray: place two pins vertically in the paper, in line with the incident ray, several centimetres apart. (Alternatively, mark two points along the ray with a sharp pencil.)
  5. Mark the emergent ray: look through the far side of the block (along the ray) and place two more pins in line with the emergent ray. (Alternatively, mark two points along the emergent ray.) The pins should appear collinear with the image of the first two pins when viewed through the block.
  6. Remove the block. Using a ruler, draw straight lines through the pin marks to give:
    • The incident ray (entering the block).
    • The emergent ray (leaving the block).
  7. Draw the refracted ray: join the point where the incident ray meets the block (point P) to the point where the emergent ray leaves the block (point Q). This straight line is the refracted ray inside the glass.
  8. Measure the angle of incidence i (between the incident ray and the normal) using the protractor. Record i.
  9. Measure the angle of refraction r (between the refracted ray and the normal) using the protractor. Record r.
  10. Repeat for at least 5 different angles of incidence — e.g. i = 10°, 20°, 30°, 40°, 50°, 60°. Record i and r in a table. Also calculate sin i and sin r for each pair (to 3 sig figs).

6. Why Multiple Angles Improve Accuracy

6.1 The Argument

Using 5+ angles and plotting a graph is more accurate than using a single angle for three reasons:

  1. Random errors cancel out. Each individual (i, r) measurement has a random uncertainty of ~±2° (from protractor resolution and pin alignment). With 5 measurements, the random errors in the individual points partly cancel — some points lie above the true line, some below — and the line of best fit averages them out.
  2. Anomalies are visible. If one of the 5 readings has been misread or misrecorded (a common occurrence — pins knocked, block shifted), it will appear as a point clearly off the line. The student can identify and exclude the anomaly. With a single reading, an anomaly goes undetected.
  3. The gradient method is more reliable than a single ratio. A single n = sin i / sin r calculation gives one value, which could be high or low depending on which measurement happened to have the largest random error. The gradient of the best-fit line uses all 5 points and gives a result close to the mean of the individual sin i / sin r ratios — but with reduced uncertainty.

6.2 Why the Graph is a Straight Line Through the Origin

Snell's law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. For light entering glass from air (n_air ≈ 1):

sin i = n × sin r

This is the equation of a straight line through the origin with gradient n (when sin i is plotted on the y-axis and sin r on the x-axis). If the data does not pass through the origin, it suggests a systematic error (e.g. the normal was not perpendicular to the block face, or the protractor was misaligned).

7. Transmission and Reflection at an Interface (Spec 55)

7.1 The Core Concept

When a wave meets an interface between two media, the wave is split into a reflected wave and a transmitted wave. The proportions depend on the difference in wave speed (or, more generally, in wave impedance) between the two media.

Key point: Both reflection and transmission occur simultaneously at most interfaces. The reflected portion returns to medium 1; the transmitted portion enters medium 2 (and is refracted if the incidence is non-normal).

7.2 Why Both Happen — The Impedance Mismatch

The wave equation governs how waves propagate, and at a boundary the boundary conditions (continuity of displacement and continuity of the gradient) cannot be satisfied by a transmitted wave alone — there must also be a reflected wave to satisfy both conditions.

Physically: the wave's energy depends on the medium. When the wave meets a boundary where the medium changes, the wave "doesn't know" how much of its energy to deposit in the new medium. The result is a partial deposit (transmission) and a partial return (reflection).

The fraction of intensity reflected at normal incidence (for light) is given by the Fresnel equation (not examinable in full, but the underlying concept is):

Ir / Ii = ((n₁ − n₂) / (n₁ + n₂))²

For sound, the equivalent is:

Ir / Ii = ((Z₂ − Z₁) / (Z₂ + Z₁))²

where Z = ρc is the acoustic impedance.

7.3 Worked Example: Light at a Glass-Air Interface

For light incident normally on a glass-air interface (n_glass = 1.50, n_air = 1.00):

Ir / Ii = ((1.50 − 1.00) / (1.50 + 1.00))²
        = (0.50 / 2.50)²
        = (0.20)²
        = 0.040

Result: 4% of the incident light intensity is reflected; 96% is transmitted. This is the typical partial reflection seen when looking through a window.

7.4 Worked Example: Ultrasound at an Air-Skin Interface

For ultrasound incident on the air-skin boundary (typical acoustic impedances: Z_air = 4 × 10² kg m⁻² s⁻¹, Z_skin = 1.6 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹):

Ir / Ii = ((1.6 × 10⁶ − 4 × 10²) / (1.6 × 10⁶ + 4 × 10²))²
        ≈ ((1.6 × 10⁶) / (1.6 × 10⁶))²
        ≈ 1.0

Result: ~99.9% of the ultrasound is reflected at the air-skin boundary; only ~0.1% is transmitted. This is why coupling gel is used in medical ultrasound — the gel has an acoustic impedance close to that of skin, so the gel-skin interface reflects almost nothing, allowing the ultrasound to enter the body.

7.5 The Critical Angle: Where Reflection Becomes Total

For light travelling from a denser medium (glass, n₁ = 1.50) to a less dense medium (air, n₂ = 1.00):

  • Small angle of incidence (e.g. 10°): mostly transmitted, slightly reflected (partial reflection — see section 7.3).
  • Approaching the critical angle: the transmitted ray bends further from the normal, and the reflected intensity increases.
  • At the critical angle C: the angle of refraction is 90° — the transmitted ray travels along the interface. The reflected intensity is still partial.
  • Above the critical angle: total internal reflection (TIR) — 100% reflection, 0% transmission.

The critical angle:

sin C = n₂ / n₁ = 1.00 / 1.50
C = sin⁻¹(0.667) = 41.8°

So for glass-to-air, TIR occurs for any angle of incidence greater than 41.8°. This is the boundary between partial reflection (spec 55) and total reflection (spec 47).

8. Applications of Transmission and Reflection at an Interface

8.1 Application 1: Pulse-Echo Scanning (Medical Ultrasound)

Spec 56, application of spec 55.

A transducer emits a short pulse of ultrasound (typically 2–10 MHz) into the body. At each tissue boundary (skin-fat, fat-muscle, muscle-bone, etc.) a fraction of the pulse is reflected back to the transducer. The transducer acts as both emitter and detector — it records the time delay t between emitting the pulse and detecting each echo.

Distance to each boundary:

d = (v × t) / 2

where v is the speed of sound in the relevant tissue (typically 1540 m s⁻¹ in soft tissue) and the factor of 2 accounts for the pulse travelling to the boundary and back.

Worked example (examiner-favoured, Jan 2023 Q17b):

An ultrasound pulse is emitted into the body. An echo is detected 0.080 ms after emission. The speed of sound in soft tissue is 1540 m s⁻¹. Calculate the distance to the reflecting boundary.

d = (v × t) / 2
  = (1540 m s⁻¹ × 0.080 × 10⁻³ s) / 2
  = 0.1232 / 2
  = 0.062 m
  = 6.2 cm

Result: the boundary is 6.2 cm below the skin.

Why coupling gel is needed (links to spec 55): without gel, the air-skin boundary reflects ~99.9% of the ultrasound (section 7.4) and almost none enters the body. The gel (acoustic impedance close to skin) reduces this mismatch.

8.2 Application 2: Optical Fibres (Spec 47, 48, 55)

An optical fibre consists of a glass core (high n, e.g. 1.50) surrounded by a glass cladding (lower n, e.g. 1.46). Light entering one end of the fibre at a small enough angle undergoes total internal reflection at the core-cladding boundary repeatedly, staying within the core until it emerges at the far end.

Critical angle at the core-cladding boundary:

sin C = n_cladding / n_core = 1.46 / 1.50 = 0.973
C = sin⁻¹(0.973) = 76.7°

So any ray inside the core striking the cladding at an angle greater than 76.7° (measured from the normal) undergoes TIR and stays in the core.

Why cladding is used (links to spec 55):

  1. Provides a clean, controlled boundary for TIR. Without cladding, the core-air boundary would be at the glass surface — scratches, dirt, or contact with other fibres would disrupt TIR and let light escape.
  2. Prevents cross-talk between adjacent fibres in a bundle. Without cladding, light leaking from one fibre could enter a neighbouring fibre.
  3. Reduces pulse broadening (modal dispersion). A higher critical angle (compared to the core-air case) means only rays within a narrower range of angles can propagate — this reduces the path-length differences between rays and hence reduces pulse broadening.

Worked example (Oct 2022 Q16 style, optical fibre):

A fibre has n_core = 1.52 and n_cladding = 1.40. Calculate the critical angle at the core-cladding boundary.

sin C = n_cladding / n_core = 1.40 / 1.52 = 0.921
C = sin⁻¹(0.921) = 67.0°

Any ray inside the core striking the cladding at an angle > 67.0° (from the normal) undergoes TIR.

8.3 Application 3: Sonar (Spec 56, application of spec 55)

A sonar system on a ship emits a pulse of ultrasound (typically 30–200 kHz) into the sea. The pulse reflects off the sea floor (or off a shoal of fish, or a submarine) and returns to a detector on the ship. The depth is calculated using d = vt/2.

Speed of sound in water: ~1500 m s⁻¹ (vs ~340 m s⁻¹ in air). This is much faster than in air because water is much less compressible.

Worked example (Jun 2022 Q12c style):

A sonar pulse is emitted from a ship. The echo from the sea floor is detected 1.20 s later. The speed of sound in seawater is 1500 m s⁻¹. Calculate the depth of the sea.

d = (v × t) / 2
  = (1500 m s⁻¹ × 1.20 s) / 2
  = 1800 / 2
  = 900 m

Result: the sea is 900 m deep at this point.

Why ultrasound (not audible sound)?

  • Penetration: higher-frequency ultrasound is less attenuated in water than audible sound, so it travels further.
  • Directionality: shorter wavelength (λ = v/f, so for 100 kHz in water, λ = 1.5 cm) allows a narrow beam, so the source can be directional. Audible sound (λ ~ 1 m to 15 m in water) would spread in all directions.
  • Marine life: audible sound would disturb marine animals; ultrasound is mostly above their hearing range.

8.4 Application 4: Pulse-Echo in Non-Destructive Testing (NDT)

The same principle is used to detect cracks inside metal components (e.g. aircraft wings, railway lines). An ultrasound pulse is sent into the metal; any crack (an air-metal interface, since Z_air ≪ Z_metal) reflects a strong echo back. The position of the crack is found from d = vt/2, where v is the speed of sound in the metal (typically ~5000 m s⁻¹ in steel).

Worked example (Jun 2025 Q13a style — the "undetectable crack"):

An ultrasound pulse is sent into a steel rail of thickness 5.0 cm. The speed of sound in steel is 5200 m s⁻¹. A crack is present at the centre of the rail. Calculate the time delay between emitting the pulse and detecting the echo from the crack.

d = 2.5 cm = 2.5 × 10⁻² m (half the rail thickness)
t = 2d / v = (2 × 2.5 × 10⁻² m) / 5200 m s⁻¹
         = 5.0 × 10⁻² / 5200
         = 9.6 × 10⁻⁶ s
         = 9.6 µs

Result: the echo from the crack is detected 9.6 µs after emission. (Note: Jun 2025 Q13a tested why a crack parallel to the rail face is undetectable — because such a crack reflects the pulse back at a small angle, away from the transducer.)

9. Common Pitfalls and Examiner Traps

9.1 The "angle from surface" trap

The most common refraction error. Mark schemes (Jan 2023 Q15a, Jan 2025 Q18) give angles measured from the surface, requiring the student to subtract from 90° to obtain the angle from the normal. Always check whether the angle is from the surface or from the normal.

9.2 The "factor of 2" trap in pulse-echo

The second most common error in pulse-echo questions. The pulse travels to the boundary and back, so d = vt/2, not d = vt. Forgetting the factor of 2 doubles the calculated distance.

9.3 Confusing "diffraction" with "refraction"

Diffraction (spec 50) is the spreading of a wave through a gap or around an obstacle — requires the wave to meet a gap/obstacle comparable to its wavelength. Refraction (spec 45) is the change of direction of a wave at an interface between two media — happens at every interface, regardless of wavelength. Oct 2022 Q8 MCQ tested this directly: "What does NOT occur at a glass block?" — diffraction (it occurs only at gaps/obstacles, not at flat interfaces).

9.4 Forgetting "both reflection and transmission"

Spec 55 explicitly requires the understanding that both reflection and transmission occur at most interfaces. Examiner mark schemes (Jan 2024 Q18b) reward "both reflection and transmission" — describing only one loses marks.

9.5 Missing one of the two TIR conditions

For TIR (spec 47, application of spec 55), both conditions must be stated:

  1. Light must travel from a denser to a less dense medium (n₁ > n₂).
  2. The angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle.

Stating only one condition loses 1 mark.

  • Using electron charge (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) instead of electron mass (9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg) in de Broglie calculations (Jan 2022 Q2)

  1. Wave model prediction: Any frequency of light, given sufficient intensity and time, should eject electrons (energy accumulates gradually)

2. Definition-by-Definition Exam Mapping

2.1 Huygens' Construction (spec 50)

How tested:

  • "Use Huygens' construction to explain what happens to a wave when it meets a slit." (3 marks, Jun 2024 Q16b — the only direct Huygens question in 11 papers)
  • Implicitly tested via single-slit diffraction and electron diffraction explanations.

Full-mark answer requirements (Jun 2024 Q16b mark scheme):

  1. Every point on the wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets (1 mark)
  2. The new wavefront is the envelope (tangent) of these wavelets (1 mark)
  3. At a slit, only the points within the slit emit wavelets; these spread out (1 mark)

Common pitfalls:

  • Saying "the wave bends" without explaining the secondary wavelet mechanism — does not score.
  • Omitting "envelope" or "tangent" — loses the second mark.
  • Describing interference instead of Huygens — irrelevant to the question asked.

2.2 Single-Slit Diffraction (spec 50)

How tested:

  • "Describe the diffraction pattern produced when monochromatic light passes through a single narrow slit." (3 marks, Oct 2021 Q15c)
  • "Explain why the central maximum is wider than the other maxima." (2 marks, Jun 2024 Q16c)

Full-mark answer requirements:

  • Central maximum is the brightest and widest fringe (twice as wide as the secondary maxima)
  • Secondary maxima are dimmer and narrower, with intensity decreasing away from the centre
  • Minima occur where wavelets from across the slit interfere destructively

Common pitfalls:

  • Confusing single-slit pattern with double-slit pattern (double-slit has equal-intensity fringes of equal width).
  • Forgetting that the central maximum is twice as wide as the secondary maxima.
  • Stating "the pattern is the same as a double-slit pattern" — incorrect.

2.3 Obstacle Diffraction / Wavelength-to-Size Ratio

How tested:

  • "Explain why sound waves diffract around a doorway but light waves do not." (2 marks, Jan 2022 Q12a)
  • QWC on harbour waves (6 marks, Jan 2023 Q15a)
  • QWC on harbour waves again (6 marks, Jan 2024 Q17b — examiner repeated this question type)

Full-mark answer (Jan 2022 Q12a style):

  • Sound wavelength (~1 m) is comparable to the doorway width (~1 m) → significant diffraction (1 mark)
  • Light wavelength (~500 nm) is much smaller than the doorway → negligible diffraction (1 mark)

Common pitfall: Stating "sound has a longer wavelength than light" without relating the wavelength to the obstacle size. The mark scheme requires the comparison.

2.4 Measuring Refractive Index of a Solid (spec 48)

How tested:

  • "Describe how a student could use a glass block and a ray box to determine the refractive index of the glass." (6 marks, Jun 2024 Q14b — the most thorough test of spec 48 in the past 5 years)
  • "Explain why it is better to take measurements at several different angles of incidence and plot a graph, rather than use a single angle." (2 marks, Jan 2024 Q18c(ii))

Full-mark answer structure (Jun 2024 Q14b — 6 marks):

  1. Place the glass block on a sheet of paper and trace around it; draw a normal at the point where the ray will meet the block (1 mark)
  2. Direct a narrow ray from the ray box at a measured angle of incidence; mark the incident ray using two pins (1 mark)
  3. Mark the emergent ray on the far side of the block using two more pins (1 mark)
  4. Remove the block; join the pin marks to draw the incident, refracted (inside the block), and emergent rays (1 mark)
  5. Measure i and r with a protractor; repeat for at least 5 different angles of incidence (1 mark)
  6. Plot sin i against sin r; the gradient of the straight line through the origin equals n (1 mark)

Common pitfalls:

  • Forgetting to draw the normal (line perpendicular to the surface at the point of incidence).
  • Measuring the angle from the surface instead of from the normal.
  • Using only one or two angles (mark scheme requires "at least 5").
  • Plotting sin r against sin i (inverted axes) — gradient becomes 1/n, not n.
  • Calculating n = sin i / sin r from a single reading instead of using the graph gradient.

2.5 Transmission AND Reflection at an Interface (spec 55)

How tested:

  • "Explain why some of the light is reflected and some is transmitted when light meets a glass-air interface." (2 marks, Jun 2022 Q14b)
  • "State what is meant by partial reflection." (1 mark, Jan 2024 Q12a)
  • MCQ: "What does NOT occur at a glass block?" — answer: diffraction (1 mark, Oct 2022 Q8)

Full-mark answer (Jun 2022 Q14b style):

  • At the interface, there is a change in wave speed (or impedance mismatch) (1 mark)
  • Both reflection and transmission occur; the proportions depend on the size of the mismatch (1 mark)

Common pitfalls:

  • Stating only "light is reflected" or only "light is transmitted" — mark scheme requires both.
  • Confusing "interface" (spec 55) with "slit" (spec 50) — Oct 2022 Q8 traps students who say diffraction occurs at a glass block.
  • Forgetting to mention the change in wave speed as the cause.

2.6 Critical Angle as the Boundary Between Partial and Total Reflection

How tested:

  • "Calculate the critical angle for light travelling from glass (n = 1.50) into air." (2 marks, Jan 2022 Q5)
  • "Explain what happens to the light as the angle of incidence is gradually increased from 0° to 90° in the glass." (3 marks, Oct 2023 Q18c — conceptual)

Full-mark answer (Oct 2023 Q18c style):

  • For i < critical angle: most light is transmitted (refracted), with a small partial reflection (1 mark)
  • As i approaches the critical angle: the refracted ray bends further from the normal, and the reflected intensity increases (1 mark)
  • For i > critical angle: total internal reflection — 100% reflected, no transmission (1 mark)

Common pitfall: Treating partial reflection and TIR as separate phenomena. They are the same phenomenon at different angles of incidence — TIR is the limiting case.

2.7 Pulse-Echo / Ultrasound (spec 56, application of spec 55)

How tested:

  • 11/11 papers, 23 marks total — appears in EVERY paper.
  • Most common form: "Calculate the distance to the boundary given the time delay and wave speed" (3 marks).
  • Factor-of-2 trap: forgetting to divide vt by 2 — appears in 6 examiner reports.

Full-mark answer requirements:

  1. Use d = vt/2 (state the formula explicitly)
  2. Substitute values with correct units (especially convert ms to s, cm to m)
  3. Quote answer with appropriate sig figs and units

Common pitfalls:

  • Forgetting the factor of ½ — calculates 2× the correct distance (lose 1 mark).
  • Using the speed of sound in air (340 m s⁻¹) instead of in water (1500 m s⁻¹) or in tissue (1540 m s⁻¹).
  • Mixing units (e.g. v in m/s but t in ms without conversion).

2.8 Optical Fibre (spec 47, 48, 55)

How tested:

  • 4 papers, 38 marks total — appears as a multi-part question with a real-world context.
  • Most common: calculate critical angle at core-cladding, predict whether TIR occurs for a given ray, explain the role of cladding, calculate pulse broadening.

Full-mark answer structure (Jan 2025 Q18 — 13 marks):

  1. State n_core > n_cladding and why (1 mark)
  2. Calculate critical angle: sin C = n_cladding/n_core (2 marks)
  3. Apply: ray strikes cladding at angle > C → TIR occurs (2 marks)
  4. Explain role of cladding: protects core boundary, prevents cross-talk, reduces modal dispersion (3 marks)
  5. Calculate pulse broadening from path-length difference between axial and extreme rays (3 marks)
  6. QWC: continuous prose, cause-and-effect connectives (2 marks)

Common pitfalls:

  • Forgetting n_core > n_cladding (mark scheme requires this stated).
  • Using n_cladding/n_core inverted → sin C > 1 (mathematically impossible — diagnostic of the error).
  • Confusing modal dispersion (different path lengths) with material dispersion (different wavelengths).

4. Recurring Examiner Report Themes (Jan 2019–Jun 2025)

4.1 "Students forgot the factor of ½ in pulse-echo"

Appears in 6 examiner reports (Jun 2019, Jan 2022, Jun 2022, Oct 2023, Jan 2024, Jun 2025). The pulse travels to the boundary and back, so d = vt/2, not d = vt. Fix: write d = vt/2 (with the 2) on the formula sheet, and explicitly state "the pulse travels there and back".

4.2 "Students measured the angle from the surface, not the normal"

Appears in 5 examiner reports (Jan 2022, Jan 2023, Oct 2023, Jan 2025, Jun 2025). Snell's law and the critical angle use angles measured from the normal. Fix: always check whether the question gives the angle from the surface; if so, subtract from 90°.

4.3 "Students described only one of reflection or transmission"

Appears in 4 examiner reports (Jun 2022, Jan 2024, Jun 2024, Jan 2025). Spec 55 requires the understanding that both reflection and transmission occur at most interfaces. Fix: when asked about an interface, always state "both reflection and transmission occur, with the proportions depending on the impedance mismatch".

4.4 "Students confused diffraction with refraction"

Appears in 3 examiner reports (Oct 2022, Jan 2023, Jan 2024). Diffraction (spec 50) requires a gap or obstacle comparable to the wavelength; refraction (spec 45) occurs at every interface regardless of wavelength. Oct 2022 Q8 MCQ directly tested this — many students said diffraction occurs at a glass block (it does not; only refraction and partial reflection occur). Fix: diffraction = spreading through a gap; refraction = bending at an interface.

4.5 "Students forgot to draw the normal"

Appears in 2 examiner reports (Jun 2024, Jan 2025) — both in spec 48 method questions. The normal is the line perpendicular to the surface at the point of incidence; angles i and r are measured from it. Fix: always draw the normal first, before measuring any angles.

4.6 "Students used only one angle of incidence"

Appears in 2 examiner reports (Jun 2024, Jan 2024). The mark scheme for spec 48 explicitly requires "at least 5 different angles of incidence". Using only one or two loses the method mark. Fix: in method questions, always state "repeat for at least 5 different angles".

4.7 "Students forgot both TIR conditions"

Appears in 4 examiner reports. TIR requires: (1) denser → less dense medium AND (2) i > C. Stating only one condition loses 1 mark. Fix: state both conditions, connected by "AND".

4.8 "Students could not apply Huygens' construction"

Appears in 1 examiner report (Jun 2024). Students described the diffraction pattern but did not use the secondary-wavelet mechanism to explain it. Fix: Huygens questions require "every point on the wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets" and "the new wavefront is the envelope of these wavelets".
