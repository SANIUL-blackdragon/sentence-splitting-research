1. Energetics (Ch 2.6)

Formulas & Equations

Heat energy transferred: q = m × c × ΔT - q = heat energy transferred (J) - m = mass of SOLUTION being heated (g) — NOT the mass of the reactant/fuel - c = specific heat capacity (J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹); water = 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ - ΔT = temperature change (°C) - Use for calorimetry, combustion, neutralisation experiments

Enthalpy change per mole: ΔH = −q / n (exothermic = negative; drop negative for endothermic) - n = moles of limiting reactant (combustion: moles of fuel; neutralisation: moles of H₂O formed) - Final answer in kJ mol⁻¹, 2–3 SF

Hess's Law — Formation method: ΔHr = ΣΔHf(products) − ΣΔHf(reactants)

Hess's Law — Combustion method: ΔHr = ΣΔHc(reactants) − ΣΔHc(products) ← NOTE: reversed vs formation

Bond enthalpy calculation: ΔH = Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds formed) - Bonds broken = endothermic (+ve); bonds formed = exothermic (−ve) - For CₐH_b straight chain: C–C bonds = a − 1; C–H bonds = b

Percentage uncertainty (ΔT involves TWO readings): % uncertainty = (2 × individual uncertainty / measured ΔT) × 100 - Each thermometer reading has uncertainty (e.g. ±0.05 °C); total = 2× individual

Percentage error (comparing to data book): % error = |experimental value − data book value| / data book value × 100

Full-Mark Calorimetry Calculation Pattern (4–5 marks):

Step 1: q = m × c × ΔT (J)
Step 2: n = mass / Mᵣ  OR  n = c × V/1000
Step 3: ΔH = −q / n
Step 4: Convert J → kJ; apply sign; state units (kJ mol⁻¹); 2–3 SF

Thermometric Titration: - Draw two lines of best fit (pre-endpoint and post-endpoint), extrapolate to find true ΔT - Use TOTAL mass of both solutions: m = (V₁ + V₂) × 1.00 g cm⁻³ - Moles of water = moles of acid (or base) at endpoint

Mean bond enthalpy from atomisation data: Bond enthalpy = [Σ(atomisation values for all atoms) − ΔHreaction] / number of that bond type

Key Definitions

Standard conditions: 298 K (25 °C) and 100 kPa; 1 mol dm⁻³ for solutions; all substances in standard states. "Room temperature" NOT accepted.

Standard state: The physical state of a substance under standard conditions (e.g. H₂(g), H₂O(l), NaCl(s), Br₂(l), C(s) as graphite).

Exothermic reaction: Heat energy is transferred to the surroundings. Products have LESS enthalpy than reactants. ΔH is negative.

Endothermic reaction: Heat energy is absorbed from the surroundings. Products have MORE enthalpy than reactants. ΔH is positive.

Standard enthalpy change of formation (ΔH°f): The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states under standard conditions. - Must specify: (1) 1 mol of compound, (2) from elements, (3) in standard states

Standard enthalpy change of combustion (ΔH°c): The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a substance reacts completely with oxygen under standard conditions. - Always negative; per mole of substance combusted, NOT per mole of O₂

Standard enthalpy change of neutralisation (ΔH°neut): The enthalpy change when 1 mole of water is formed from the reaction between an acid and an alkali under standard conditions. - Key: per mole of water (not per mole of acid/base)

Standard enthalpy change of atomisation (ΔH°at): The enthalpy change when 1 mole of gaseous atoms is formed from an element in its standard state under standard conditions. - Always positive; product is always gaseous atom(s) - Example: ½Br₂(l) → Br(g) — Br₂ is LIQUID at standard conditions

Bond enthalpy (bond dissociation enthalpy): The enthalpy change when one mole of a specific covalent bond in a gaseous molecule is broken.

Mean bond enthalpy: The average enthalpy change for breaking one mole of a particular type of bond, averaged over many different compounds (Data Booklet values).

Hess's Law: The total enthalpy change for a chemical reaction is independent of the route by which the chemical change occurs, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.

Enthalpy level diagram rules: - Y-axis must be labelled "enthalpy" (NOT "energy", "ΔH", "energy change") - Exothermic: products BELOW reactants; arrow points DOWN - Endothermic: products ABOVE reactants; arrow points UP - Arrow is single-headed, from reactants to products - Do NOT draw activation energy on a simple level diagram - With intermediate: overall ΔH spans initial reactants to final products (NOT to/from intermediate)

Reaction profile: Includes activation energy "hump"; y-axis = "enthalpy", x-axis = "reaction progress"; uncatalysed peak higher than catalysed peak; ΔH same for both.

Key Facts & Constants

Specific heat capacity of water: 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ (most commonly used; for dilute aqueous solutions assume water's value)

Standard conditions: 298 K, 100 kPa

Common bond enthalpies (kJ mol⁻¹): | Bond | Value | |------|:-----:| | C–C | 347 | | C–H | 413 | | C–F | 485 | | C–Cl | 339 | | C–Br | 276 | | C–I | 238 | | C=O | 805 | | O–H | 464 | | O=O | 498 | | F–F | 151 | | H–H | 436 | | Cl–Cl | 242 | | Br–Br | 193 | | S–S (in S₈) | 268 |

Standard enthalpy definitions — sign convention: | Type | Sign | Per Mole of | |------|:----:|:-----------:| | ΔHf (formation) | Can be ± | 1 mol of compound | | ΔHc (combustion) | Always − | 1 mol of substance | | ΔHneut (neutralisation) | Always − | 1 mol of H₂O | | ΔHat (atomisation) | Always + | 1 mol of gaseous atoms |

Combustion increment per CH₂ group: ~630 kJ mol⁻¹ (used for homologous series predictions)

Bond enthalpy limitations (2 marks): 1. Mean bond enthalpies are averaged over many compounds (not specific to the reaction) 2. Bond enthalpies are for the gas phase only (solids/liquids not accounted for) 3. Enthalpies of vaporisation/fusion not included

Reasons experimental ΔH is LESS exothermic than data book (2 marks needed): - Heat loss to surroundings - Incomplete combustion - Evaporation of fuel (e.g. ethanol) - Heat absorbed by calorimeter (not accounted for) - NOT accepted: "not all fuel burned", "no lid", "water not stirred"

2. Kinetics (Ch 2.8)

Formulas & Equations

Rate from 1/time: Rate ∝ 1/time — valid ONLY for comparing initial rates of similar reactions (e.g., CP5 precipitate times)

Rate from graph (instantaneous): Rate = gradient of tangent at that point = Δy / Δx - Units depend on graph axes (e.g., cm³ min⁻¹, mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) - NOT average rate (total/total)

SN2 rate law: Rate = k[halogenoalkane][nucleophile] (second order)

Key Definitions

Collision theory: For a reaction to occur, particles must collide with sufficient energy (≥ activation energy, Eₐ) and with the correct orientation.

Activation energy (Eₐ): The minimum energy that particles must have for a collision to be successful (to result in a reaction). - Eₐ is an intrinsic property — does NOT change with temperature, pressure, or concentration - Always a positive value - Lower Eₐ = faster reaction

Rate of reaction: The change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time. Instantaneous rate = gradient of tangent on a concentration–time or volume–time graph.

Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution: A graph showing the distribution of kinetic energies among molecules in a gas at a given temperature. - Y-axis: number/fraction of molecules; X-axis: energy - Starts at the origin (no molecule has zero energy) - Peak = most probable energy (E_mode) - Asymmetric with long tail to right; asymptotic (never reaches x-axis) - Area = total number of molecules

Catalyst: A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. The catalyst is not consumed in the reaction and is regenerated at the end. - Does NOT change: equilibrium position, equilibrium constant, product yield, ΔH, average kinetic energy, M–B distribution curve - Does: lower Eₐ, increase rate of BOTH forward and backward reactions equally

Dynamic equilibrium: The state in a closed system where the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the backward reaction, and the concentrations of all reactants and products remain constant (not necessarily equal). - "Dynamic" = both reactions STILL occurring - Requires: closed system, reversible reaction, rate forward = rate backward, macroscopic properties constant

Le Chatelier's principle: If a system at dynamic equilibrium is subjected to a change, the equilibrium position shifts to counteract (oppose) the imposed change.

Compromise conditions: Industrial conditions (T, P) chosen as a compromise between maximum yield (from Le Chatelier) and acceptable rate (from kinetics) and economic viability.

Catalyst sustainability benefit: Catalysts allow lower operating temperatures → less energy/fuel consumed → reduced CO₂ emissions. NOT "catalyst is reused" or "lowers Eₐ" (those are mechanism, not sustainability).

Key Facts & Constants

Effects on rate and equilibrium: | Change | Collision Frequency | Proportion Successful | Rate | Equilibrium Shift | |--------|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---| | ↑ Concentration | ↑ | No change | ↑ | Right (products) | | ↑ Pressure (gas) | ↑ | No change | ↑ | Fewer gas moles side | | ↑ Temperature | ↑ | ↑ (more E ≥ Eₐ) | ↑↑ | Endothermic direction | | ↑ Surface area | ↑ | No change | ↑ | No effect | | Add catalyst | No change | ↑ (more molecules exceed the lowered Eₐ) | ↑ | No shift |

Maxwell–Boltzmann — Temperature increase: - Peak: lower and broader, shifts RIGHT - Area: same (same number of molecules) - Eₐ line: unchanged - More area to RIGHT of Eₐ (more molecules above Eₐ)

Maxwell–Boltzmann — Catalyst added: - Distribution curve: UNCHANGED - Eₐ line: shifts LEFT (lower value) - More molecules now have E ≥ new (lower) Eₐ

Maxwell–Boltzmann — Pressure decrease: - Area: decreases (fewer molecules) - Peak: lower (fewer at every energy) - Shape: same (per molecule) - Eₐ: unchanged

Le Chatelier — Temperature: - ↑T → favours endothermic direction - ↓T → favours exothermic direction

Le Chatelier — Pressure: - ↑P → favours side with fewer gas moles - ↓P → favours side with more gas moles

Le Chatelier — Concentration: - Adding reactant → shifts RIGHT - Removing product → shifts RIGHT - Adding product → shifts LEFT

Le Chatelier — Catalyst: - No shift (speeds up both directions equally)

Haber process compromise: | Factor | Le Chatelier (max yield) | Kinetics (max rate) | Compromise | |--------|:---:|:---:|:---:| | Temperature | Low T (exothermic fwd) | High T (more E ≥ Eₐ) | ~450 °C | | Pressure | High P (4→2 mol gas) | High P (more collisions) | ~200 atm | | Catalyst | No effect | Increases rate | Iron (Fe) |

Ethene hydration: C₂H₄(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ C₂H₅OH(g) — exothermic - Temperature compromise: lower T favoured (higher yield) but rate too slow - Pressure: 2 mol gas → 1 mol gas; >7 MPa → higher yield; >7 MPa = expensive equipment - Excess water: pushes equilibrium right (advantage); harder to separate (disadvantage) - Overall yield 95%: ethanol condenses/liquefies + unreacted ethene recycled

What you do NOT need for Unit 2: - Kc equilibrium constant calculations - Rate equations / rate-determining step - Arrhenius equation - Kp (gas equilibrium constant) - Half-life concepts

4. Physical Chemistry: IMF, Redox, Groups 1, 2, 7 (Ch 2.7)

Formulas & Equations

Titration calculation pattern:

Step 1: n(known) = c × V/1000
Step 2: Use balanced equation mole ratio
Step 3: Scale if aliquot (e.g., 25 cm³ of 250 cm³ → ×10)
Step 4: Convert to required units (g dm⁻³, mol dm⁻³, %)

Total volume trap: If 20 cm³ acid + 10 cm³ base → total = 30 cm³ for dilution calculations

Half-equation balancing (alkaline conditions): O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (n = 4 electrons)

Concentrated H₂SO₄ + halide reactions:

NaCl + conc H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl           (NOT redox — Cl⁻ too weak)
NaBr + conc H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + SO₂ + HBr + Br₂  (Br⁻ reduces H₂SO₄ to SO₂)
NaI  + conc H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + H₂S + HI + I₂    (I⁻ reduces H₂SO₄ to H₂S)

Thermal decomposition patterns:

Group 2 carbonates:   MCO₃ → MO + CO₂
Group 2 nitrates:     M(NO₃)₂ → MO + 2NO₂ + ½O₂
LiNO₃ (like Group 2): 4LiNO₃ → 2Li₂O + 4NO₂ + O₂
NaNO₃ (Group 1):      2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + ½O₂  (nitrite, NOT oxide)

Disproportionation equations:

Cl₂ + H₂O → HClO + HCl                    (Cl: 0 → +1 and 0 → −1)
Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O          (Cl: 0 → −1 and 0 → +1)
3NaClO → 2NaCl + NaClO₃                   (Cl: +1 → −1 and +1 → +5)

Group 2 + water:

Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂   (cold water; Ca(OH)₂ slightly soluble)
Mg + H₂O(steam) → MgO + H₂  (steam only → oxide, NOT hydroxide)
2Li + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂     (with state symbols required)

Reactions for CP4 qualitative tests:

CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)          (effervescence, limewater cloudy)
Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)         (white ppt, insoluble in acid)
NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃(g) + H₂O              (damp red litmus turns blue)

Key Definitions

London forces (induced dipole–dipole forces): Weak intermolecular forces caused by the movement of electrons creating an instantaneous/temporary dipole, which induces a dipole in a neighbouring atom/molecule. Exist between ALL atoms and molecules. - Key points: electron motion → temporary dipole → induces dipole in neighbour → attraction - Strength increases with: more electrons, larger electron cloud, greater surface area, less branching - "Van der Waals forces" — Edexcel uses "London forces"

Permanent dipole–dipole forces: Attractive forces between the permanent dipoles in polar molecules (molecules with an uneven charge distribution). Always present IN ADDITION to London forces in polar molecules.

Hydrogen bonding: A strong intermolecular force when H is covalently bonded to F, O, or N and is attracted to a lone pair on F, O, or N in a DIFFERENT molecule. - H must be on F, O, or N (NOT C, P, S, Cl) - Strongest type of intermolecular force (but NOT a covalent bond) - Requires: lone pair + δ⁺H interaction - HF, H₂O, NH₃ have anomalously high b.p. due to H-bonding

Ion-dipole interactions: Forces between an ion and the partial charges on a polar molecule (e.g., Na⁺ attracted to δ⁻O in water). Important for dissolving ionic compounds in water.

Oxidation: Loss of electrons; increase in oxidation number. Reduction: Gain of electrons; decrease in oxidation number. Oxidising agent: The species that is reduced (gains electrons). Reducing agent: The species that is oxidised (loses electrons).

Disproportionation: A redox reaction in which the SAME element is both oxidised AND reduced simultaneously. - Key phrase: "The same element undergoes both oxidation and reduction"

Standard electrode potential: Not required for Unit 2 (Unit 4).

Trends in Group 2 (down the group): - Atomic/ionic radius increases - 1st ionisation energy decreases - Reactivity with water/acid increases - Thermal stability of carbonates/nitrates INCREASES (harder to decompose) - Solubility of hydroxides INCREASES - Solubility of sulfates DECREASES

Thermal decomposition explanation (3-mark chain):

1. IONIC RADIUS: larger cation (e.g., Ca²⁺ > Mg²⁺)
2. POLARISING POWER: larger → LOWER polarising power → polarises anion LESS
3. BOND WEAKENING: C–O (or N–O) bonds are LESS weakened → harder to break → higher decomposition T

Diagonal relationship (Li/Mg): - LiNO₃ decomposes like Group 2 nitrates → Li₂O + NO₂ + O₂ (not NaNO₂ + ½O₂) - Both Li and Mg: carbonates decompose on heating; form normal oxides

Oxidation number rules: - Element = 0 - Simple ion = charge (Na⁺ = +1, O²⁻ = −2) - O = −2 normally; −1 in peroxides (H₂O₂, Na₂O₂); −½ in superoxides - H = +1 normally; −1 in hydrides (NaH, CaH₂) - F = −1 ALWAYS (most electronegative) - Sum of oxidation numbers = overall charge - Roman numeral = oxidation number (e.g., chlorate(V) = Cl at +5)

Key Facts & Constants

CRITICAL Group 2 solubility trends (OPPOSITE):

Hydroxide solubility:  Mg(OH)₂ < Ca(OH)₂ < Sr(OH)₂ < Ba(OH)₂  (INCREASES ↓)
Sulfate solubility:    MgSO₄  > CaSO₄  > SrSO₄  > BaSO₄      (DECREASES ↓)

Flame test colours: | Element | Colour | |---------|--------| | Li | Crimson | | Na | Yellow | | K | Lilac | | Ca | Orange-red / brick-red / red | | Sr | Red | | Ba | Green |

AgNO₃ precipitation test for halide ions (aqueous, acidified with dilute HNO₃): | Halide | Precipitate | Solubility in NH₃ | |--------|:-----------:|:------------------:| | Cl⁻ | White | Dissolves in excess | | Br⁻ | Cream | Partially dissolves | | I⁻ | Yellow | Does NOT dissolve |

Halogen displacement reactivity: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ (stronger oxidising agent displaces weaker from solution)

I₂ in solvent: - In cyclohexane (non-polar): purple - In aqueous solution: brown/yellow - With starch: blue-black

Group 7 trends down the group: - Boiling temperature ↑ - Electronegativity ↓ - Oxidising power ↓ - Reactivity ↓ - Atomic radius ↑ - Electron affinity ↓

HX boiling point trend: HCl < HBr < HI < HF - London forces increase Cl → I (more electrons) - HF anomaly: hydrogen bonding makes HF highest

Disproportionation examples tested: - Cl₂ + H₂O → HClO (+1) + HCl (−1) - Cl₂ + cold dilute NaOH → NaCl (−1) + NaClO (+1) - 3NaClO → 2NaCl (−1) + NaClO₃ (+5)

Methyl orange end-point: Yellow → ORANGE (NOT red — red = excess acid)

Peroxide oxidation numbers: - H₂O₂: O = −1, H = +1 - Na₂O₂: O = −1, Na = +1

Hydride oxidation numbers: - NaH: H = −1, Na = +1

5. Organic Chemistry: Alcohols, Haloalkanes & Analysis (Ch 2.9)

Formulas & Equations

SN2 mechanism — standard pattern:

Nucleophile (lone pair) attacks δ⁺ carbon from opposite side to leaving group
→ Curly arrow from lone pair to Cδ⁺
→ Curly arrow from C–X bond to X (both electrons go to X)
→ Products: R–Nu + X⁻

SN2 with NH₃ — TWO steps:

Step 1: NH₃ attacks → R–NH₃⁺ + Br⁻  (propylammonium ion)
Step 2: Second NH₃ removes H⁺ → R–NH₂ + NH₄⁺  (propylamine)

Halogenoalkane reactions:

SUBSTITUTION (aqueous):
  R–Br + NaOH(aq) → R–OH + NaBr        (heat under reflux)
  R–Br + 2NH₃(ethanol) → R–NH₂ + NH₄Br (heat under PRESSURE / sealed tube)
  R–Cl + KCN(ethanol) → R–CN + KCl      (heat)

ELIMINATION (ethanolic):
  R–CH₂–CHBr–R' + KOH(ethanol) → R–CH=CH–R' + KBr + H₂O  (heat)

Alcohol reactions:

PCl₅ test:    R–OH + PCl₅ → R–Cl + POCl₃ + HCl    (misty white fumes)
Dehydration:  R–OH + H₃PO₄ → alkene + H₂O          (170 °C)

Oxidation:
  Primary + distillation:  RCH₂OH + [O] → RCHO + H₂O      (aldehyde, orange→green)
  Primary + reflux:        RCH₂OH + 2[O] → RCOOH + H₂O     (carboxylic acid, orange→green)
  Secondary + reflux:      R₂CHOH + [O] → R₂C=O + H₂O       (ketone, orange→green)
  Tertiary:                NO REACTION (no colour change)

[O] counting:
  Primary alcohol → carboxylic acid: 2[O]
  Secondary alcohol → ketone: 1[O]

Ideal gas equation for Mᵣ: pV = nRT → n = pV/RT → Mᵣ = mass/n

Inorganic tests for organic compounds:

Br₂ water:     decolourised → C=C (alkene) present
Na₂CO₃(aq):   effervescence → –COOH (carboxylic acid) present
PCl₅:         misty white fumes → –OH (alcohol) present — works for ALL alcohol types
K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄: orange→green → primary or secondary alcohol (NOT tertiary)
AgNO₃(ethanol): precipitate → halogen present

Key Definitions

Substitution: A reaction where an atom or group is replaced by a different atom or group.

Elimination: A reaction where a small molecule (e.g., H₂O, HBr) is removed, forming a C=C double bond.

Addition: Two or more molecules combine to form a single larger molecule (across C=C).

Hydrolysis: A substitution reaction where a halogenoalkane reacts with water/aqueous hydroxide to form an alcohol.

Nucleophile: An electron-pair donor that attacks an electron-deficient (electrophilic) carbon atom. - Common: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O

SN2 (substitution nucleophilic bimolecular): One-step mechanism; nucleophile attacks from opposite side to leaving group; transition state is trigonal bipyramidal (5 groups); inversion of configuration at chiral centre. - Rate = k[halogenoalkane][nucleophile] - Favoured by: primary halogenoalkanes, good nucleophiles

SN1 (substitution nucleophilic unimolecular): Two-step mechanism; C–X bond breaks first forming carbocation intermediate; nucleophile then attacks; racemisation at chiral centre. - Rate = k[halogenoalkane] only - Favoured by: tertiary halogenoalkanes, polar protic solvents

Primary (1°) halogenoalkane: Carbon bearing halogen attached to one (or zero) other carbons. Secondary (2°): Attached to two other carbons. Tertiary (3°): Attached to three other carbons. - Cyclohexyl bromide = secondary (C–Br carbon bonded to 2 ring C atoms)

Primary alcohol: –OH on carbon attached to ≤1 other carbon → oxidises to aldehyde then carboxylic acid. Secondary alcohol: –OH on carbon attached to 2 other carbons → oxidises to ketone ONLY. Tertiary alcohol: –OH on carbon attached to 3 other carbons → NOT oxidised by K₂Cr₂O₇.

Molecular ion peak (M⁺): Peak in mass spectrum of the unchanged molecule (lost one electron). m/z = relative molecular mass.

Fragmentation: Breaking of M⁺ into smaller pieces (fragment ions) with characteristic m/z values.

Base peak: Tallest peak in mass spectrum (100% relative abundance) = most stable fragment.

M+1 peak: Caused by ¹³C natural abundance (~1.1%). Ratio M+1/M⁺ ≈ 1.1% × number of carbon atoms.

Bond STRENGTH (not polarity) determines hydrolysis rate. Weaker bond → lower Eₐ → faster.

Hydrolysis rate ranking (structural): Tertiary (SN1, fast) > Secondary > Primary (SN2, slow)

AgNO₃ test — precipitate speed (1° haloalkane): Cl⁻ (slowest, white) < Br⁻ (medium, cream) < I⁻ (fastest, yellow) Tertiary iodoalkane = absolute fastest (SN1 + weakest C–I bond compound)

SN2 Mechanism Marking Points Checklist (6–8 points for 3–4 marks)

  1. Lone pair shown on nucleophile (NOT from the negative charge symbol)
  2. δ⁺ on C and δ⁻ on X shown (dipole notation)
  3. Curly arrow from lone pair to Cδ⁺ (full-headed, NOT fishhook)
  4. Curly arrow from C–X bond to X (both electrons to leaving group)
  5. Organic product drawn correctly (structural formula, all atoms)
  6. Leaving group as anion (Br⁻, Cl⁻ — must have charge)
  7. For NH₃ reactions: Second step — deprotonation by second NH₃ molecule
  8. K⁺ (spectator ion) should be ignored

Penalties: Fishhook arrows penalised ONCE; missing lone pair penalised ONCE; fragment charge omission penalised ONCE.

Aqueous vs Ethanolic — The Critical Distinction

Solvent Reaction Type Product
Aqueous NaOH/KOH Substitution (SN2) Alcohol
Ethanolic NaOH/KOH Elimination Alkene
Ethanol + NH₃ (pressure) Substitution (SN2) Amine
Ethanol + KCN Substitution (SN2) Nitrile

NH₃ specifically: Heat under pressure / sealed tube in alcoholic solution. "Heat under reflux" is explicitly NOT awarded (NH₃ gas escapes through condenser).

Why amine yield is low: Excess ammonia leads to further substitution → secondary amine, tertiary amine, quaternary ammonium salt.

KCN importance in synthesis: CN⁻ substitution extends the carbon chain by one carbon (R–X → R–CN).

Alcohol Identification — Quick Decision

What type of alcohol?
├── Primary + K₂Cr₂O₇ (distillation) → Aldehyde (C=O at 1740–1720 cm⁻¹)
├── Primary + K₂Cr₂O₇ (reflux) → Carboxylic acid (broad O–H + C=O ~1700)
├── Secondary + K₂Cr₂O₇ (reflux) → Ketone (C=O at 1720–1700)
└── Tertiary + K₂Cr₂O₇ → NO REACTION (no colour change)

Best universal test for –OH: PCl₅ — works for primary, secondary, AND tertiary alcohols. Produces misty white fumes (HCl).

E/Z Isomerism from Elimination

  • Each alkene with different groups on both C=C sides has E and Z stereoisomers
  • Example: CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₂CH₂CH₃ → hex-2-ene (E + Z) + hex-3-ene (E + Z) = 4 products
  • Symmetrical halogenoalkanes → fewer products
  • Branching near halogen → fewer elimination sites

Mark Scheme Carry-Forward Rules

Where TE (transferred error) is applied: - Multi-step calculations: wrong q but correct ΔH = q/n → full marks for later steps - Hess's Law cycles: wrong value but correct calculation from it → score calculation marks - Bond enthalpy: one wrong value but correct total Σ → score for total

Where TE is NOT applied: - Signs (+/−): penalised independently - Significant figures: only 1 SF penalised; any reasonable SF otherwise accepted - Units: penalised once per paper (not per question)
