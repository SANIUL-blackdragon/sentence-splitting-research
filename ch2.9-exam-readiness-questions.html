Spec Point 10.1 — Substitution and Elimination Reactions

Q1 QFR §10.1 1 mark · ~2 min

Define the term hydrolysis as applied to halogenoalkanes.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

A substitution reaction in which a halogenoalkane reacts with water (or aqueous hydroxide) to form an alcohol. (1)

Q2 TDC §10.1 2 marks · ~4 min

Compare substitution and elimination reactions by completing the table below. | Feature | Substitution | Elimination | |---------|-------------|-------------| | What happens to atoms/groups | (a) | (b) | | Bond formed in organic product | (c) | (d) |

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) An atom/group is replaced by a different atom/group (½) (b) A small molecule (e.g. H₂O, HX) is removed (½) (c) No new C=C bond / single bonds only (½) (d) A C=C double bond is formed (½) (Award 1 mark for each correct pair: (a)+(b) = 1 mark; (c)+(d) = 1 mark)

Q3 EXP §10.1 3 marks · ~6 min

Classify each of the following reactions as substitution, elimination, addition, or oxidation. Give one reason for each classification. (i) CH₃CH₂Br + NaOH(aq) → CH₃CH₂OH + NaBr (ii) CH₃CHBrCH₃ + KOH(ethanol) → CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂O + KBr (iii) CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(i) Substitution — the Br atom is replaced by an OH group on the same carbon (1) (ii) Elimination — HBr is removed and a C=C double bond is formed (1) (iii) Oxidation — the functional group changes from alcohol to aldehyde (loss of H / gain of O) (1)

Spec Point 10.2 — Nucleophile Identity in the AgNO₃ Test

Q4 WTE §10.2 1 mark · ~2 min

A student observes a precipitate forming when 1-bromobutane is added to aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol. The student concludes: "The silver ion (Ag⁺) acted as the nucleophile in this reaction." State whether this conclusion is correct, and if not, identify the actual nucleophile.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Incorrect. The nucleophile is H₂O (water). Ag⁺ acts to precipitate the halide ion (Br⁻) as AgBr; it is not the nucleophile. (1)

Q5 EXP §10.2 3 marks · ~6 min

Reaction of a halogenoalkane with aqueous silver nitrate involves two distinct roles. (a) Identify the species that acts as the nucleophile and state what is formed when it attacks the halogenoalkane. (2 marks) (b) Identify the role of Ag⁺ in this reaction. (1 mark)

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) The nucleophile is H₂O (water) (1). It attacks the electrophilic carbon, forming an alcohol (1). (Accept: substitution product / hydroxyl group replaces halogen) (b) Ag⁺ reacts with the displaced halide ion (X⁻) to form an insoluble silver halide precipitate (AgX) (1), which provides the visible observation used to compare reactivity of different halogenoalkanes.

Spec Point 10.3 — Reactivity Trend: Bond Enthalpy, Not Polarity

Q6 WTE §10.3 2 marks · ~4 min

A student writes: "The hydrolysis of iodomethane is faster than the hydrolysis of fluoromethane because the C–I bond is more polar than the C–F bond." Identify the error in this statement and give the correct explanation.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Error: the C–I bond is the least polar, not the most polar (1). Correct explanation: the C–I bond has a lower bond enthalpy / is weaker than the C–F bond, leading to a lower activation energy for bond breaking (1).

Q7 EXP §10.3 3 marks · ~6 min

Explain why the rate of hydrolysis of halogenoalkanes increases in the order: RCl < RBr < RI. Your answer should include reference to bond enthalpy and activation energy.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Mean bond enthalpy decreases from C–Cl to C–Br to C–I / C–I bond is the weakest (1). The weaker the C–X bond, the lower the activation energy for the rate-determining step (1). A lower activation energy means a greater proportion of molecules have energy ≥ Eₐ (Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution), leading to more frequent successful collisions per second (1).

Q8 WTE §10.3 2 marks · ~4 min

A student explains the reactivity trend by saying "reactivity increases down Group 17 because the halogens become less electronegative." Explain why this is NOT the accepted explanation and state what should be said instead.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Electronegativity/polarity does not determine the rate of SN2 reactions (1). The correct explanation is that the C–X bond enthalpy decreases down the group (C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I), giving a lower activation energy and therefore a faster reaction (1).

Q9 EXP §10.3 4 marks · ~8 min

A student is asked to explain why fluoromethane is the least reactive halogenoalkane in hydrolysis. The student writes: "The C–F bond is the most polar, so the carbon is most electrophilic and most susceptible to nucleophilic attack, therefore fluoromethane should react fastest." Evaluate this explanation. In your answer you should: identify what is correct about the student's statement, identify what is incorrect or incomplete, and provide a complete and correct explanation.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Correct: the C–F bond is the most polar (largest electronegativity difference), so the carbon atom does carry the greatest δ⁺ charge and is the most electrophilic (1). Incorrect/incomplete: the student assumes that greater electrophilicity (from polarity) determines reactivity, but this is not the dominant factor in hydrolysis (1). Complete explanation: the C–F bond has the highest mean bond enthalpy (485 kJ mol⁻¹), requiring the most energy to break and giving the highest activation energy (1). Therefore, despite being the most polar, fluoromethane reacts slowest because bond enthalpy, not bond polarity, is the dominant factor controlling the rate of hydrolysis (1).

Spec Point 10.4 — Nucleophiles and the SN2 Mechanism

Q10 TDC §10.4 2 marks · ~4 min

(a) Define the term nucleophile. (1) (b) State the number of steps in an SN2 mechanism. (1)

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) An electron-pair donor (1) [accept: a species that donates a pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond] (b) One (1) [accept: one step / concerted / single step]

Q11 WTE §10.4 2 marks · ~4 min

A student draws a curly arrow from the negative charge symbol on OH⁻ towards the carbon in an SN2 mechanism. Explain why this is incorrect and state what should be drawn instead.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

The curly arrow must originate from a lone pair of electrons on the oxygen atom, not from the negative charge symbol (1). The negative charge is a consequence of the extra electron, not the electrons themselves — the arrow represents the movement of the electron pair (1).

Q12 DIA §10.4 3 marks · ~6 min

Draw the SN2 mechanism for the hydrolysis of bromoethane with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Your mechanism should include: curly arrows to show the movement of electron pairs, the transition state, and the products.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Curly arrow from lone pair on O of OH⁻ to the electrophilic carbon of bromoethane AND curly arrow from the C–Br bond to Br (1). Transition state drawn with the central carbon bonded to five groups in a trigonal bipyramidal arrangement, with partial/dashed bonds to both the incoming OH group and the outgoing Br (1). Products shown: ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) and Br⁻ (1).

Q13 TDC §10.4 2 marks · ~4 min

State TWO differences between the transition state and the products of an SN2 reaction.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Any two from: the transition state has five groups around the central carbon (trigonal bipyramidal) whereas the product has four groups (tetrahedral) (1); the transition state is not isolable whereas the products are stable, separate species (1); the transition state has a partial bond to both the nucleophile and the leaving group whereas the product has a complete bond to the nucleophile only (1).

Q14 WTE §10.4 2 marks · ~4 min

A student writing an SN2 mechanism uses a half-headed (fishhook) arrow from the C–Br bond to Br. Why would this be penalised in an Edexcel exam?

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

A half-headed (fishhook) arrow represents the movement of a single electron (homolytic fission) (1). The C–Br bond in an SN2 mechanism breaks by heterolytic fission (both electrons go to Br), so a full-headed curly arrow must be used (1).

Q15 DIA §10.4 3 marks · ~6 min

Draw a reaction profile diagram (potential energy vs reaction coordinate) showing two curves: one for the SN2 hydrolysis of 1-chlorobutane and one for 1-iodobutane. Label the activation energies and explain the difference.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:
  • Both curves start at the same (or similar) reactant energy level and end at the same product energy level
  • The 1-iodobutane curve has a lower activation energy (Eₐ) peak than the 1-chlorobutane curve (1)
  • Label Eₐ(I) < Eₐ(Cl) on the diagram (1)
  • Explanation: C–I bond has lower bond enthalpy than C–Cl, so less energy is required to break the C–X bond in the rate-determining step; the 1-iodobutane reaction is therefore faster at the same temperature (1)

Spec Point 10.5 — Reactions of Halogenoalkanes

Q16 QFR §10.5 2 marks · ~4 min

State the systematic name of the halogenoalkane with the formula (CH₃)₂CHCH₂Br.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

1-bromo-2-methylpropane (2)

Q17 TDC §10.5 2 marks · ~4 min

Compare the products of the reaction of 1-bromopropane with (i) NaOH(aq) and (ii) KCN(ethanol). In each case, state the product name and the number of carbon atoms in the product.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(i) NaOH(aq) → propan-1-ol, 3 carbon atoms (1). (ii) KCN(ethanol) → butanenitrile, 4 carbon atoms (the nitrile adds one carbon to the chain) (1).

Q18 EXP §10.5 3 marks · ~6 min

Explain why the reaction of a halogenoalkane with KCN is particularly useful in organic synthesis. Refer to the carbon chain length in your answer.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

The cyanide ion (CN⁻) acts as a nucleophile and substitutes the halogen on the halogenoalkane (1). The CN group contains a carbon atom, so the product (a nitrile) has one more carbon in the chain than the starting halogenoalkane (1). This extends the carbon chain by one carbon, which is valuable for building larger organic molecules (1).

Q19 WTE §10.5 2 marks · ~4 min

A student writes that the reaction of 1-chloropentane with KCN in aqueous solution produces pentanenitrile. Explain why this is incorrect and state the actual product under aqueous conditions.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Under aqueous conditions, water (or hydroxide from water) acts as the nucleophile, so the product is pentan-1-ol (via hydrolysis), not the nitrile (1). To obtain pentanenitrile, the reaction must be carried out in ethanol as the solvent, not aqueous solution (1).

Q20 QFR §10.5 1 mark · ~2 min

State the name of the organic product when 1-bromopropane reacts with excess ammonia.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Propylamine (CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂) (1).

Q21 EXP §10.5 2 marks · ~4 min

Explain why the reaction of 1-bromopropane with ammonia requires TWO steps in the mechanism, unlike the reaction with NaOH(aq) which occurs in one step.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Ammonia (NH₃) is a neutral nucleophile, so when it attacks the electrophilic carbon, it forms a positively charged intermediate (CH₃CH₂CH₂N⁺H₃) because nitrogen now has four bonds (1). A second NH₃ molecule is needed to remove a proton (H⁺) from this intermediate, forming the neutral amine product and NH₄⁺ (1). In contrast, OH⁻ is already negatively charged and the product (alcohol) is neutral directly.

Q22 WTE §10.5 2 marks · ~4 min

A student writes the product of Step 1 of the ammonia reaction as CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃ (without a positive charge on N). Explain why this would lose a mark.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

After the nitrogen of NH₃ forms a bond to carbon, it has four bonds and a positive charge (N⁺) — it is a propylammonium ion, not a neutral amine (1). Omitting the positive charge on nitrogen shows a failure to recognise that nitrogen now has four covalent bonds and carries a formal positive charge (1).

Q23 SYN §10.5 4 marks · ~8 min

Describe the full two-step mechanism for the reaction of 1-bromopropane with excess ammonia. In your answer, show all curly arrows, name all intermediates and products, and state the conditions.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Step 1: Lone pair on N of NH₃ attacks the electrophilic carbon (Cδ⁺) of 1-bromopropane; simultaneous curly arrow from C–Br bond to Br (1). Forms propylammonium ion (CH₃CH₂CH₂N⁺H₃) and Br⁻ (1). Step 2: A second NH₃ molecule acts as a base; curly arrow from lone pair on N to H⁺ on the ammonium ion; curly arrow from N–H bond to N⁺ (1). Forms propylamine (CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂) and NH₄⁺ (1). Conditions (required for full marks but no separate mark — counted within Step 1/2): ethanol as solvent, heat under pressure in a sealed tube, excess ammonia. (Any one of these conditions may be stated; the 4 marks are all awarded for the two-step mechanism above.)

Q24 EXP §10.5 3 marks · ~6 min

Explain TWO key distinctions between the conditions used for (R1) hydrolysis to form an alcohol and (R3) reaction with ammonia to form an amine.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Distinction 1: R1 uses aqueous solvent (water) because water is the nucleophile in hydrolysis; R3 uses ethanol as solvent because aqueous conditions would cause competing hydrolysis of the halogenoalkane (1). Distinction 2: R1 is carried out under reflux (heating with condenser, open to atmosphere); R3 requires heating under pressure in a sealed tube because ammonia is a gas and needs to be kept in contact with the halogenoalkane at elevated temperature (1). Both R1 and R3 are nucleophilic substitution but require different practical conditions (1).

Q29 EXP §10.7 3 marks · ~6 min

Explain why 1-chlorobutane would give the slowest precipitate in an AgNO₃ in ethanol test compared to (CH₃)₃CI, (CH₃)₂CHBr, and CH₃CH₂CH₂Br.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

1-Chlorobutane is primary (favouring the slower SN2 mechanism rather than SN1) (1) AND contains chlorine, which forms the strongest C–X bond (highest bond enthalpy, highest activation energy) among Cl, Br, and I (1). Both factors (primary structure AND strong C–Cl bond) result in the slowest rate of hydrolysis, hence the slowest precipitate formation (1).

Spec Point 10.8 — Ammonia Reaction Conditions and Elimination Products

Q30 TDC §10.8 3 marks · ~6 min

Compare the conditions required for the reaction of a halogenoalkane with aqueous sodium hydroxide (hydrolysis) with those required for reaction with ammonia to form a primary amine. Your answer should include the solvent, heating method and a reason for any difference.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Aqueous NaOH: dissolved in water (or aqueous ethanol), heated under reflux (1). Ammonia: dissolved in alcoholic ammonia (ethanol solvent), heated under pressure / in a sealed tube (1). Reason: ammonia is a gas and would escape from an open reflux system, so a sealed tube is required to maintain sufficient concentration / pressure of NH₃ (1).

Q31 EXP §10.8 2 marks · ~4 min

Even when a large excess of ammonia is used, the yield of the primary amine from the reaction of a halogenoalkane with ammonia is relatively low. Explain why.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

The primary amine product (R–NH₂) itself contains a lone pair on nitrogen and can act as a nucleophile (1). It can undergo further nucleophilic substitution with unreacted halogenoalkane to form a secondary amine, tertiary amine or quaternary ammonium salt, reducing the yield of the desired primary amine (1).

Q32 SYN §10.8 4 marks · ~8 min

A student carries out two experiments on 2-bromobutane. Experiment 1: 2-bromobutane is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Experiment 2: 2-bromobutane is warmed with potassium hydroxide dissolved in ethanol. (a) For each experiment, state the reaction type and name the nucleophile. (2 marks) (b) For Experiment 2, the student expects the major organic product to be but-1-ene but the major product is actually but-2-ene. Explain why but-2-ene is the major product. (2 marks)

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) Experiment 1: Substitution (1); nucleophile = OH⁻ (1) Experiment 2: Elimination (1); base = OH⁻ (1) (Note: in elimination, OH⁻ acts as a base (abstracting H⁺), not a nucleophile; accept "OH⁻" for both experiments) (b) But-2-ene is the more substituted alkene (it has more alkyl groups attached to the C=C double bond) (1), making it more stable than but-1-ene (1). (Accept: Zaitsev's rule — the alkene with the greater number of alkyl groups on the C=C bond is favoured)

Q33 SYN §10.8 4 marks · ~8 min

2-Bromobutane (CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃) undergoes elimination with hot, concentrated ethanolic KOH. (a) State the reaction type. (b) Name the alkene products formed. (c) For each product, state whether it shows E/Z stereoisomerism, giving a reason.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) Elimination (1). (b) But-1-ene and but-2-ene (1). (c) But-2-ene: YES, it shows E/Z isomerism because each carbon of the C=C has two different groups (CH₃ and H on each carbon) (1). But-1-ene: NO, one carbon of the C=C has two H atoms (CH₂=), so E/Z isomerism is not possible (1).

Q34 CAL §10.8 3 marks · ~6 min

CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₂CH₂CH₃ undergoes elimination with hot ethanolic KOH. (a) Name the two alkene products (without stereoisomers). (b) State the total number of alkene products including stereoisomers.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) Hex-2-ene and hex-3-ene (1 each, 2 marks). (b) 4 products total (hex-2-ene has E and Z stereoisomers; hex-3-ene has E and Z stereoisomers) (1).

Q81 CAL §10.24 3 marks · ~6 min

The mass spectrum of a compound shows a molecular ion peak at m/z 86. The empirical formula of the compound is C₂H₃O. Deduce the molecular formula of the compound.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Mᵣ from M⁺ peak = 86 (1). Empirical formula mass of C₂H₃O = (2 × 12) + (3 × 1) + 16 = 43 (1). Multiplier = 86 / 43 = 2, therefore molecular formula = C₄H₆O₂ (1).

Q82 EXP §10.24 3 marks · ~6 min

In a mass spectrum, the molecular ion peak (M⁺) has a relative abundance of 82% and the M+1 peak has a relative abundance of 3.3%. (a) Explain the origin of the M+1 peak. (b) Use the data to determine the number of carbon atoms in the molecule.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) The M+1 peak is caused by molecules containing one carbon-13 (¹³C) atom instead of carbon-12 (¹²C), due to the natural abundance of ¹³C (~1.1%) (1). (b) The ratio of M+1 to M⁺ is (3.3 / 82) × 100% ≈ 4.0%; number of carbon atoms ≈ (M+1/M⁺ ratio) / 1.1% = 4.0 / 1.1 ≈ 4 carbon atoms (2). (Methodology consistent with Q84: the M+1/M⁺ ratio is divided by 1.1% to estimate the carbon count.)

Q83 DIA §10.24 3 marks · ~6 min

The mass spectrum of a compound shows a molecular ion peak at m/z 60 and a prominent fragment ion peak at m/z 31. Identify the compound and explain how each piece of evidence supports your identification.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

Propan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂OH (1). The M⁺ peak at m/z 60 corresponds to C₃H₈O (Mᵣ = 60) (1). The peak at m/z 31 corresponds to CH₂OH⁺, which is characteristic of a primary alcohol formed by α-cleavage adjacent to the –OH group (1).

Q84 CAL §10.24 3 marks · ~6 min

A student analysing the mass spectrum of compound X sees a small peak at m/z 87, one mass unit higher than the M⁺ peak at m/z 86. Explain the origin of the m/z 87 peak, state the natural abundance of the isotope responsible, and explain how the M+1 peak can be used to estimate the number of carbon atoms in the molecule.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

The peak at m/z 87 is the M+1 peak, caused by the natural presence of carbon-13 (¹³C) — one ¹²C is replaced by ¹³C, increasing M⁺ by 1 (1). The natural abundance of ¹³C is approximately 1.1% (1). For a molecule with n carbon atoms, M+1/M⁺ ≈ n × 1.1%; so if the ratio is about 4.4%, the molecule contains approximately 4 carbon atoms (1).

Q86 SYN §10.24 5 marks · ~10 min

An organic compound X has Mᵣ = 60. The mass spectrum of X shows a base peak at m/z 31. The infrared spectrum of X shows a broad absorption covering 2500–3300 cm⁻¹. A student concludes that X is propan-1-ol. (a) Explain why the mass spectrum data is consistent with propan-1-ol. (2 marks) (b) Explain why the student's conclusion is incorrect based on the IR data, and suggest a more likely identity for X. (3 marks)

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) M⁺ at m/z 60 is consistent with C₃H₈O (Mᵣ = 60) (1). The base peak at m/z 31 corresponds to CH₂OH⁺, formed by α-cleavage characteristic of a primary alcohol (1). (b) The broad absorption at 2500–3300 cm⁻¹ is characteristic of the O–H stretch in a carboxylic acid, NOT an alcohol (1). An alcohol O–H stretch is a broad absorption in the range 3750–3200 cm⁻¹ (data-booklet value) and does not extend as far to lower wavenumbers (1). A more likely identity for X is ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH (Mᵣ = 60), which would show a broad carboxylic O–H stretch and a characteristic fragment at m/z 45 (COOH⁺) (1).

Q88 EXP §10.27 4 marks · ~8 min

Two isomeric compounds, ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) and methoxymethane (CH₃OCH₃), both have Mᵣ = 46. Describe a test-tube reaction that could distinguish between them and state the expected observations for each compound.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

  • Ethanol (a primary alcohol): orange solution turns green as Cr₂O₇²⁻ is reduced to Cr³⁺; ethanol is oxidised to ethanal/ethanoic acid (1)

Spec Point 10.28 — Determination of Mᵣ by Volatilisation

Q89 CAL §10.28 3 marks · ~6 min

A volatile liquid Y is vapourised to determine its relative molecular mass. The following data were obtained: pressure = 98 500 Pa, volume = 7.5 × 10⁻⁵ m³, temperature = 373 K, mass of vapour = 0.115 g. Calculate Mᵣ of Y and suggest its identity. (R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

n = pV / RT = (98 500 × 7.5 × 10⁻⁵) / (8.314 × 373) = 7.3875 / 3101.122 = 2.38 × 10⁻³ mol (1). Mᵣ = mass / n = 0.115 / 2.38 × 10⁻³ = 48.3 (1) (accept 47–49). Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH, Mᵣ = 46 is closest; methoxymethane (CH₃OCH₃, Mᵣ = 46) also possible — slightly high value suggests experimental error but identity is ethanol or methoxymethane (1).

Spec Point 10.24+ — Additional Spectroscopy & Mechanism Questions

Q90 EXP §10.24 3 marks · ~6 min

The mass spectrum of an unknown chlorinated compound shows a molecular ion cluster with two peaks at m/z 78 and m/z 80 in an approximate ratio of 3 : 1. (a) Explain the origin of the two peaks. (b) State how the mass spectrum of 1,2-dibromoethane would differ in the molecular ion region, and give the relative heights of the three M, M+2, and M+4 peaks.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) The two peaks arise from the two naturally occurring isotopes of chlorine — ³⁵Cl (~75%) and ³⁷Cl (~25%). Each molecule contains one Cl atom, so two molecular ion peaks appear at m/z = M and M+2 in an approximately 3 : 1 ratio matching the natural isotopic abundance of chlorine (1). (b) Bromine has two isotopes — ⁷⁹Br (~51%) and ⁸¹Br (~49%) of roughly equal abundance. With two bromine atoms in 1,2-dibromoethane (CH₂BrCH₂Br, M⁺ = 186), three peaks appear at m/z 186 (M), 188 (M+2) and 190 (M+4) (1). Their relative heights follow the binomial expansion (0.51 + 0.49)² → 0.26 : 0.50 : 0.24, i.e. approximately 1 : 2 : 1 (1).

Q91 EXP §10.24 4 marks · ~8 min

The Lucas test uses concentrated HCl with anhydrous ZnCl₂ (Lucas reagent) to distinguish between 1°, 2° and 3° alcohols by the rate of formation of the cloudy chloroalkane layer. (a) Write the equation for the reaction of butan-2-ol with HCl in the presence of ZnCl₂. (b) Explain why tertiary alcohols react fastest and primary alcohols react slowest (or not at all without heating). (c) State the visual observation that would distinguish each alcohol class.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ + HCl —(ZnCl₂)→ CH₃CHClCH₂CH₃ + H₂O (1). (b) The reaction proceeds via an SN1 mechanism through a carbocation intermediate. Tertiary alcohols form a tertiary carbocation (most stable, stabilised by three electron-donating alkyl groups via inductive effect and hyperconjugation), so the activation energy is lowest and the reaction is fastest (1). Primary alcohols would form a primary carbocation (least stable), so the SN1 pathway is disfavoured; the reaction is very slow at room temperature and may require heating (1). (c) Tertiary: cloudiness appears immediately (within ~1 minute); Secondary: cloudiness within 5–10 minutes; Primary: no observable reaction at room temperature (1).

Q92 SYN §10.24 4 marks · ~8 min

Account for the observation that 2-bromo-2-methylpropane (tertiary) undergoes hydrolysis via an SN1 mechanism, whereas 1-bromobutane (primary) undergoes hydrolysis via an SN2 mechanism. In your answer, refer to (a) carbocation stability, (b) steric hindrance, and (c) the rate equation expected for each substrate.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) 2-bromo-2-methylpropane forms a tertiary carbocation (CH₃)₃C⁺ that is stabilised by three electron-donating methyl groups (inductive effect + hyperconjugation), making the heterolytic C–Br bond cleavage energetically feasible — so the SN1 pathway is favoured (1). 1-bromobutane would form a primary carbocation (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂⁺) that is too unstable to form at an appreciable rate, so the concerted SN2 pathway (no carbocation) is favoured instead (1). (b) The tertiary carbon in 2-bromo-2-methylpropane is sterically hindered by three methyl groups, blocking back-side attack by the nucleophile required for SN2 — another factor favouring SN1 (1). (c) SN1 (tertiary): rate = k[halogenoalkane] (first-order, independent of [nucleophile]); SN2 (primary): rate = k[halogenoalkane][nucleophile] (second-order overall) (1).

Q93 EXP §10.24 3 marks · ~6 min

Sodium iodide reacts with concentrated sulfuric acid to give a mixture of products including hydrogen iodide, iodine, hydrogen sulfide and sulfur. (a) Write the equation for the primary reaction producing HI. (b) Explain why H₂SO₄ is not a suitable acid for producing pure HI from NaI, and identify the type of reaction that the acid undergoes. (c) State the observation that would indicate the formation of iodine.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Answer:

(a) NaI(s) + H₂SO₄(l) → NaHSO₄(s) + HI(g) (1). (b) HI is a strong reducing agent and reduces H₂SO₄ (S in +6 oxidation state) to lower sulfur species such as SO₂, S or H₂S — for example: 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O (S reduced from +6 to −2; I⁻ oxidised from −1 to 0) (1). Therefore the acid is itself reduced and the products are contaminated with iodine and sulfur-containing gases — this is a redox reaction (H₂SO₄ acting as an oxidising agent, not just an acid), so pure HI cannot be prepared this way. Phosphoric acid (a non-oxidising acid) is used instead (1). (c) A brown/black colouration or violet vapour indicates iodine formation (1).
