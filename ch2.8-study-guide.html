9.1 Collision Theory

Core Definition

Collision theory: For a reaction to occur, particles must collide with sufficient energy (equal to or greater than the activation energy) and with the correct orientation.

Key Sub-points

  • Not all collisions lead to reaction. Only those that satisfy BOTH the energy requirement AND the orientation requirement are "successful" or "effective" collisions.
  • The rate of reaction depends on: frequency of collisions and proportion of collisions that are successful.

Effects on Rate (Exam-Critical Table)

Change Effect on Collision Frequency Effect on Proportion of Successful Collisions Effect on Rate
Increase concentration (solution) ↑ (more particles per unit volume) No change ↑
Increase pressure (gas) ↑ (more particles per unit volume) No change ↑
Increase temperature ↑ (particles move faster) ↑ (more particles have E ≥ Eₐ) ↑↑
Increase surface area (solid) ↑ (more exposed particles) No change ↑

Exam Traps — Collision Theory

Trap Example from Papers What to Remember
"Increasing temperature increases the energy of collisions" Jun 2024 Q15(a) — Eₐ does NOT change Temperature does NOT change activation energy. It changes the proportion of molecules above Eₐ.
"Increasing pressure increases energy of particles" Jun 2024 Q15(b) (via MCQ) Pressure only increases collision frequency, not energy.
Confusing rate with equilibrium position Jan 2024 Q14 — catalysts affect rate only Increasing T/pressure may increase rate but DECREASE equilibrium yield (and vice versa).

Real Exam Questions

  • Jun 2024 Q15(a): "For which values does the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution change when temperature is increased?" Answer: E_mode and E_mean change; Eₐ stays the same.
  • Jun 2024 Q15(b): "For which values change when pressure is decreased?" Answer: None — Eₐ, E_mode, and E_mean are all unchanged. Decreasing pressure reduces the total number of molecules (lower concentration) but does NOT change Eₐ, E_mode, or E_mean — these depend only on temperature.

9.2 Activation Energy

Core Definition

Activation energy (Eₐ): The minimum energy that particles must have for a collision to be successful (to result in a reaction).

Essential Details

  • Eₐ is an intrinsic property of a reaction — it does not change with temperature, pressure, or concentration.
  • A lower activation energy means a faster reaction (more molecules exceed Eₐ at any given temperature).
  • Eₐ is always a positive value (energy barrier to be overcome).

Exam Facts from Mark Schemes

  • When asked "what happens to the activation energy when a catalyst is added?" → Eₐ decreases (the catalyst provides an alternative route with a lower activation energy).
  • When asked "what happens to the activation energy when temperature increases?" → Eₐ does not change. This is the single most common MCQ distractor across all 7 papers.

Real Exam Questions

  • Jan 2023 Q17(a): "Why does the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution start at the origin?" Answer: All molecules possess some (kinetic) energy — no molecule has zero energy.
  • Jan 2024 Q13: "Which statement correctly describes the effect of temperature on rate?" Correct: "More particles have energy ≥ Eₐ." Distractors: "Activation energy decreases" (WRONG), "Average kinetic energy increases alone is sufficient" (WRONG — must link to Eₐ).

9.3(i) Measuring Rate — Rate = 1/time

Core Definition

Rate of reaction (initial approximation): Rate ∝ 1/time, where time is measured from the start of the reaction to a fixed point (e.g., a colour change, a precipitate appearing, a fixed volume of gas produced).

When This Applies

  • This is an approximation valid for comparing rates of similar reactions under different conditions.
  • It assumes the reaction progress to the chosen endpoint is consistent across conditions.
  • Used extensively in Core Practical 5 (comparing rates of hydrolysis of halogenoalkanes using the silver nitrate test).

Exam Application

  • CP5 context (Oct 2023 Q15): "Why does C–Cl react slower than C–Br in SN2 hydrolysis?" Answer: The C–Cl bond is stronger than C–Br → higher activation energy → fewer molecules have sufficient energy → slower rate. (Bond enthalpy is the key factor, not solubility or polarity.)

Important Caveat

  • Rate = 1/time is only valid as a relative comparison. The actual instantaneous rate requires a gradient calculation (see 9.3(ii) below).

9.3(ii) Measuring Rate — Gradient of a Concentration–Time or Volume–Time Graph

Core Definition

Rate of reaction (from a graph): The instantaneous rate at any point is equal to the gradient (slope) of the tangent to the curve at that point.

How to Calculate

  1. Draw a tangent to the curve at the required point.
  2. Choose two well-separated points on the tangent line.
  3. Calculate: gradient = (change in y) / (change in x)
  4. Include correct units (e.g., cm³ min⁻¹, mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹).

Real Exam Questions

  • Jun 2023 Q15: "What is the rate at point X on the volume–time graph?" Answer: Draw tangent at X, calculate gradient = 1.3 cm³ min⁻¹. Common error: calculating the average rate (total volume / total time) instead of the instantaneous rate at X.
  • Jan 2023 Q22(e)(i): "Why is a high temperature used for NO production?" Answer: To increase the rate of reaction / more molecules have E ≥ Eₐ. (Note: this question is about rate, NOT about equilibrium yield.)

Graph Shapes to Know

Graph Type Shape What the Gradient Tells You
Concentration of reactant vs time Curve decreasing, flattening Gradient = rate of disappearance of reactant
Volume of gas vs time Curve increasing, flattening Gradient = rate of production of gas
Mass of product vs time Curve increasing, flattening Gradient = rate of formation of product

9.4 Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution

Core Definition

Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution: A graph showing the distribution of kinetic energies among molecules in a gas at a given temperature. The y-axis represents the number (or fraction) of molecules, and the x-axis represents energy.

Key Features of the Distribution

Feature Description
Starts at the origin No molecule has zero energy (Jan 2023 Q17(a))
Peak (mode) The most probable energy (E_mode) — shifts right with increasing T
Asymmetric Long "tail" to the right — a small fraction of molecules have very high energies
Never reaches the x-axis The curve is asymptotic — there is no maximum energy (theoretically)
Area under the curve Represents the total number of molecules (decreases if gas is removed)

Effect of Temperature (MOST TESTED TOPIC in 2.8)

When temperature increases: - The peak lowers (curve flattens) - The peak shifts to the right (higher average energy) - The curve becomes broader - The area stays the same (same number of molecules) - A larger proportion of molecules now have E ≥ Eₐ - Eₐ line is unchanged

Critical exam trap: Many students incorrectly state that the distribution "moves up" or that Eₐ changes. The peak ALWAYS lowers when T increases, and Eₐ NEVER changes with temperature.

Real Exam Questions

  • Jan 2023 Q17(b): "Effect of removing some gas molecules AND cooling on the M–B distribution." Answer: Area decreases (fewer molecules) AND peak shifts left (lower temperature). Both changes must be identified.
  • Jun 2024 Q15(a–c): Three-part MCQ on M–B:
  • (a) Temperature ↑: only E_mode and E_mean change (Eₐ unchanged) → C
  • (b) Pressure ↓: none of the three values change — Eₐ, E_mode, and E_mean depend only on temperature, not pressure. Only the total number of molecules (y-axis) decreases.
  • (c) Catalyst added: only Eₐ changes (decreases) → A
  • Oct 2024 Q1(a): "What happens to the M–B curve when temperature increases?" Answer: Peak becomes lower and further right.
  • Oct 2024 Q1(b): "How does the Eₐ line change when a catalyst is added?" Answer: Eₐ moves left; peak stays the same.
  • Jun 2023 Q21(a)(i): Sketch the M–B distribution at a higher temperature. Must start at origin, have lower peak shifted right, same asymptote. Do not let the curve drop below the original low-energy region.

9.5 Catalysts

Core Definition

Catalyst: A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. The catalyst is not consumed in the reaction and is regenerated at the end.

Key Properties (Exam-Ready)

Property Detail
Lowers activation energy Provides alternative route; Eₐ(cat) < Eₐ(uncat)
Not used up Regenerated at end of reaction
Does NOT change equilibrium position Speeds up both forward AND backward rates equally
Does NOT change the products formed Same products, same yield, just faster
Works by Providing a surface for adsorption (heterogeneous) or forming an intermediate (homogeneous)

Heterogeneous vs Homogeneous Catalysts

Heterogeneous catalyst: A catalyst in a different phase from the reactants (e.g. solid Fe in the Haber process with gaseous N₂ and H₂). The reaction occurs on the catalyst's surface.

Homogeneous catalyst: A catalyst in the same phase as the reactants (e.g. aqueous catalysts in solution reactions). It forms an intermediate species that then decomposes to release products and regenerate the catalyst.

Catalyst Effects on the M–B Distribution

  • The distribution curve itself does NOT change when a catalyst is added.
  • Only the Eₐ line shifts to the left (lower value).
  • This means a greater proportion of molecules now have E ≥ Eₐ(new), so more successful collisions occur per unit time.

Exam Facts from Mark Schemes

  • Jun 2023 Q14: Catalyst identification from rate data — Cu²⁺ reduced reaction time from 205 s to 75 s (catalyst), while Pb²⁺ increased it to 464 s (poison/inhibitor).
  • Jan 2023 Q22(d)(i): Reaction profile diagram — must show two profiles: uncatalysed (Eₐ = 290 kJ mol⁻¹) and catalysed (Eₐ = 70 kJ mol⁻¹), with correct scale and ΔH arrow.

Real Exam Questions

  • Oct 2023 Q21(b)(i): "Explain how a catalyst increases the rate of reaction, with reference to the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution." Three-mark answer: 1. Catalyst provides alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy 2. Greater proportion of molecules have energy ≥ (new, lower) Eₐ 3. Higher proportion of successful collisions per unit time

9.7 Catalysts in Industry — Sustainability

Core Definition

Catalyst sustainability benefit: Catalysts allow industrial processes to operate at lower temperatures, reducing energy consumption and fuel costs, which in turn reduces carbon dioxide emissions and makes the process more economically and environmentally sustainable.

Why This Matters in Exams

  • When asked "Suggest why using a catalyst is more sustainable" (Jan 2023 Q22(d)(ii)):
  • DO say: Less energy needed / lower temperature required / less fuel burned / reduced CO₂ emissions
  • DO NOT say: "The catalyst can be reused" (technically true but not a sustainability argument)
  • DO NOT say: "It lowers the activation energy" (this is HOW it works, not WHY it's sustainable)

Real Exam Questions

  • Jan 2023 Q22(d)(ii): "Suggest why the use of a catalyst is more sustainable." Acceptable answers: less energy needed / lower temperature / less fuel. Explicitly NOT accepted: "catalyst is not used up" or "lowers Ea."

9.8 Catalyst Action via Maxwell–Boltzmann

How to Answer (3-Mark Template)

When asked to explain catalyst action with reference to the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution:

Mark Required Content
1 Catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy
2 Greater proportion (or more) molecules now have energy ≥ Eₐ (new, lower value)
3 Therefore more frequent successful collisions (or higher rate of reaction)

Key Distinction

  • The distribution curve does not move or change shape when a catalyst is added.
  • Only the Eₐ vertical line shifts left.
  • This is a critical distinction from temperature changes, where the curve DOES change.

Common Error

  • Drawing a new, shifted distribution curve when a catalyst is added. This is incorrect — the curve stays the same; only the Eₐ marker moves.

9.9(i) Dynamic Equilibrium — Forward Rate = Backward Rate

Core Definition

Dynamic equilibrium: The state in a closed system where the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the backward reaction, and the concentrations of all reactants and products remain constant.

Essential Conditions for Dynamic Equilibrium

Condition Why It Matters
Closed system No reactants or products can enter or leave — matter is conserved
Reversible reaction Both forward and backward reactions must be possible
Rate forward = rate backward This is the defining feature of equilibrium
Macroscopic properties constant Concentration, pressure, colour, density remain steady

"Dynamic" vs "Static"

  • Dynamic means both reactions are STILL OCCURRING — particles are still colliding and reacting in both directions. It is NOT a state of no reaction.
  • Static equilibrium (which is NOT what we study) would mean both reactions have stopped.
  • The word "dynamic" is specifically tested — Jan 2024 Q14 asked students to identify the FALSE statement about equilibrium, and the correct answer (A) was that "catalysts affect the position of equilibrium" — which is false.

Real Exam Questions

  • Jan 2024 Q14: "Which statement about dynamic equilibrium is NOT true?" Answer: "A catalyst changes the position of equilibrium" — this is FALSE. Catalysts increase both rates equally; equilibrium position is unchanged.

9.9(ii) Constant Concentrations at Equilibrium

Core Definition

At equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant (not necessarily equal). This is because the forward and backward rates are equal, so any reactant consumed in the forward direction is immediately replaced by the backward reaction.

Key Exam Points

  • Constant ≠ Equal. The concentrations of reactants and products can be very different from each other — they just don't change over time.
  • "Position of equilibrium" describes where the equilibrium lies — if products are favoured, we say the equilibrium lies to the right; if reactants are favoured, it lies to the left.

9.10 Le Chatelier's Principle

Core Definition

Le Chatelier's principle: If a system at dynamic equilibrium is subjected to a change, the equilibrium position shifts to counteract (oppose) the imposed change.

Effects of Changes (Complete Table)

Change Applied Equilibrium Shift Effect on Yield Effect on Rate
Increase temperature Shifts in endothermic direction Depends on ΔH of forward reaction Rate ↑ (always)
Decrease temperature Shifts in exothermic direction Depends on ΔH of forward reaction Rate ↓ (always)
Increase pressure Shifts to side with fewer gas moles Depends on gas moles each side Rate ↑ (more collisions)
Decrease pressure Shifts to side with more gas moles Depends on gas moles each side Rate ↓
Increase [reactant] (add conc.) Shifts to right (products) Yield of products ↑ Rate ↑
Decrease [reactant] (remove) Shifts to left (reactants) Yield of products ↓ Rate ↓
Add catalyst No shift No change Rate ↑ (both directions)

JUSTIFICATION — The Key Exam Requirement

Edexcel mark schemes are extremely strict on Le Chatelier justifications. You must state: 1. What change is applied 2. Which direction the equilibrium shifts 3. Why (referencing the principle)

Template for full-mark answers:

"Increasing the temperature shifts the equilibrium to the left (endothermic direction) because the system opposes the increase in temperature by absorbing heat. This decreases the yield of products."

Real Exam Questions

  • Oct 2023 Q11(a): "Effect of decreasing temperature on SO₂ + ½O₂ ⇌ SO₃ (ΔH = −99 kJ mol⁻¹)." Answer: Equilibrium shifts right (exothermic direction is favoured) → yield ↑ but rate ↓ → D (rate decreases, yield of SO₃ increases).

  • Oct 2023 Q11(b): "Effect of increasing pressure on same system (3 mol gas → 2 mol gas)." Answer: Equilibrium shifts right (fewer gas moles on product side) → yield ↑ and rate ↑ → B.

  • Oct 2023 Q12: "Effect of adding NaOH to HCrO₄⁻ ⇌ CrO₄²⁻ + H⁺ equilibrium." Answer: NaOH removes H⁺ → equilibrium shifts left → more yellow CrO₄²⁻ → C.

  • Jan 2023 Q22(a)(i): "Effect of excess steam on methane reforming." Answer: Shifts equilibrium right / increases H₂ yield. Explicitly NOT accepted: "reaction goes to completion."

  • Jan 2023 Q22(c): "Pressure in Haber process — advantage and disadvantage." Advantage: higher yield / faster rate. Disadvantage: more energy needed / expensive equipment required.

  • Jan 2023 Q22(f): "Adding water removes NO₂ from equilibrium." Answer: equilibrium shifts left (to replace the removed reactant) → yield of products (HNO₃, HNO₂) decreases. Two marks: identify removal + state shift direction.

  • Oct 2024 Q2(a): "Effect of decreasing temperature (ΔH = +206 kJ mol⁻¹, endothermic forward)." Answer: Rate ↓, yield ↓ (D) — lower T disfavours the endothermic forward direction.

  • Oct 2024 Q2(b): "Effect of increasing pressure (more gas moles on product side)." Answer: Rate ↑, yield ↓ (B) — increasing pressure favours the side with fewer gas moles (reactants).

  • Oct 2024 Q19(b)(ii): "Adding NaOH to Cl₂ + H₂O equilibrium." Answer: NaOH removes H⁺ (reacts with it) → equilibrium shifts right (to replace H⁺) → produces more OCl⁻/HOCl.

  • Jan 2024 Q24(b)(ii): "Why does adding acid to bleach release toxic Cl₂?" Three-step answer: 1. Adding acid (H⁺) shifts Eq.1 left: H⁺ + ClO⁻ → HClO 2. This shifts Eq.2 right: HClO + Cl⁻ + H⁺ → Cl₂ + H₂O 3. Toxic Cl₂(g) is released

  • Jun 2023 Q22(d): "Adding H⁺ to chlorine disinfectant equilibrium." Answer: Equilibrium shifts left → toxic Cl₂ released.

Common Le Chatelier Pitfalls

Pitfall Papers Correct Approach
Saying equilibrium "moves to completion" Jan 2023 Equilibrium NEVER goes to completion — it shifts, not completes
Forgetting to mention rate effect Oct 2023 Q11 Some questions ask for BOTH yield AND rate effects
Confusing exo/endothermic direction Oct 2024 Q2(a) For endothermic forward: lower T → yield ↓ (shifts LEFT)

Ignoring intermediate equilibrium steps Jan 2024 Q24(b)(ii) Must trace shift through each linked equilibrium

9.11 Industrial Compromise Conditions — Yield vs Rate

Core Definition

Compromise conditions: In industrial processes, the conditions chosen (temperature, pressure) represent a compromise between the conditions that give the maximum yield (from Le Chatelier's principle) and the conditions that give an acceptable rate (from kinetics/collision theory) and are economically viable.

The Classic Example: Haber Process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹)

Factor Le Chatelier (max yield) Kinetics (max rate) Industrial Compromise
Temperature Low T (exothermic forward favoured) High T (more E ≥ Eₐ) ~450 °C — moderate T giving acceptable rate with reasonable yield
Pressure High P (4 mol → 2 mol gas) High P (more collisions) ~200 atm — high enough for good yield, but higher P = much more expensive equipment
Catalyst No effect on position Increases rate Iron (Fe) — allows lower T to be used while maintaining rate

Real Exam Questions

  • Jun 2024 Q19(c)(i): "Explain the temperature compromise for ethene hydration (C₂H₄ + H₂O ⇌ C₂H₅OH, exothermic)." Answer: Lower temperature favours the exothermic forward reaction (higher yield), but lower temperature gives a rate that is too slow (not economically viable). Both parts needed for 2 marks.

  • Jun 2024 Q19(c)(ii): "Why is >7 MPa pressure suggested?" Answer: Increasing pressure shifts equilibrium to give a greater yield (fewer moles of gas on product side).

  • Jun 2024 Q19(c)(iv): "Why not exceed 7 MPa?" Answer: Higher pressures require special/expensive plant or equipment.

  • Jun 2024 Q19(b): "How does overall yield reach 95% if equilibrium gives less?" Answer: Ethanol (and excess water) condenses/liquefies, and unused ethene/reactants are recycled.

  • Jan 2023 Q22(c): "Pressure advantage and disadvantage in Haber process." Advantage: higher yield / faster rate. Disadvantage: more energy needed / expensive equipment.

  • Jan 2023 Q22(h): "Suggest advantages of locating multiple processes on the same site." Any two from: cheaper to produce intermediates, transport savings, waste reduction, energy from exothermic steps used in endothermic steps, smaller workforce, less land.

The "Recycle" Concept

  • Many industrial processes achieve higher overall yields than predicted by equilibrium alone by continuously removing products and recycling unreacted reactants.
  • This is NOT a Le Chatelier shift — it is an engineering solution. But it often appears in equilibria questions.

1C: Combined Changes — Fewer Molecules + Lower Temperature

Question (Jan 2023 Q17(b)):

Some gas is removed from the container AND the temperature is decreased. What happens to the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution?

Answer: 1. Area decreases — fewer molecules in the container (total number reduced) 2. Peak shifts left (and becomes slightly higher relative to the reduced area) — lower average energy at lower temperature

Mark scheme: Both changes must be identified. Area decrease alone scores 1/2. Peak shift alone scores 1/2.

1D: Pressure Decrease

Question (Jun 2024 Q15(b)):

The pressure of a gaseous system is decreased at constant temperature. Which values change?

Answer: None of these values change. Decreasing pressure reduces the total number of molecules (lower concentration) but does NOT change Eₐ, E_mode, or E_mean — these depend only on temperature. The DISTRIBUTION SHAPE (per molecule) is unchanged; only the y-axis (number of molecules at each energy) decreases.

Mark scheme answer: Only the total number of molecules decreases; Eₐ, E_mode, and E_mean are unchanged.

2B: Identifying Rate from Catalyst Data

Question (Jun 2023 Q14):

Reaction time without catalyst = 205 s. Reaction time with Cu²⁺ = 75 s. Reaction time with Pb²⁺ = 464 s. Which ion acts as a catalyst?

Answer: - Cu²⁺: time decreased from 205 s to 75 s → rate increased → catalyst - Pb²⁺: time increased from 205 s to 464 s → rate decreased → poison / inhibitor

Since rate ∝ 1/time: - Without: rate ∝ 1/205 = 0.00488 - With Cu²⁺: rate ∝ 1/75 = 0.01333 (almost 3× faster) - With Pb²⁺: rate ∝ 1/464 = 0.00216 (less than half the original rate)

Mark scheme answer: Cu²⁺ is the catalyst → A.

Worked Example 3: Le Chatelier Predictions with Full Justification

This is the most important worked example section. Edexcel consistently awards marks for the reasoning chain, not just the prediction.

3A: Temperature Change — Exothermic Forward Reaction

Question (Oct 2023 Q11(a)):

For the reaction SO₂(g) + ½O₂(g) ⇌ SO₃(g), ΔH = −99 kJ mol⁻¹, what is the effect of decreasing the temperature on the rate and the yield of SO₃?

Full-mark answer (2 parts required):

Effect on rate: - Decreasing temperature decreases the rate of reaction because molecules have less kinetic energy and fewer molecules have energy ≥ Eₐ.

Effect on yield: - The forward reaction is exothermic. Decreasing temperature shifts the equilibrium to the right (in the exothermic direction) to oppose the temperature decrease. This increases the yield of SO₃.

Combined answer: Rate decreases, yield of SO₃ increases → D.

Mark scheme note: Students must give BOTH the rate effect AND the yield effect. Either alone scores 0/1 for a 1-mark MCQ.

3B: Temperature Change — Endothermic Forward Reaction

Question (Oct 2024 Q2(a)):

For a reaction where ΔH = +206 kJ mol⁻¹ (endothermic forward), what is the effect of decreasing the temperature?

Full-mark answer: - Rate decreases (lower T → fewer E ≥ Eₐ) - Yield decreases (lower T disfavours the endothermic forward reaction → equilibrium shifts LEFT, towards reactants)

Answer: Rate ↓, yield ↓ → D.

Common trap: Students assume "lower temperature always increases yield" — this is only true for EXOTHERMIC forward reactions. For endothermic forward reactions, lower T DECREASES yield.

3C: Pressure Change — Fewer Gas Moles on Product Side

Question (Oct 2023 Q11(b)):

For 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g) (3 mol gas → 2 mol gas), what is the effect of increasing the pressure?

Full-mark answer: - Rate increases (more molecules per unit volume → more frequent collisions) - Yield of SO₃ increases (increasing pressure favours the side with fewer gas moles → shifts right, from 4 mol to 2 mol)

Answer: Rate ↑, yield ↑ → B.

3D: Pressure Change — More Gas Moles on Product Side

Question (Oct 2024 Q2(b)):

For a reaction where the product side has MORE gas moles than the reactant side, what is the effect of increasing the pressure?

Full-mark answer: - Rate increases (always, with pressure) - Yield decreases (increasing pressure favours the side with FEWER gas moles → shifts LEFT, towards reactants)

Answer: Rate ↑, yield ↓ → B.

Common trap: Students always assume "higher pressure = higher yield" — this is only true if the product side has fewer gas moles. Always COUNT THE GAS MOLES on each side first.

3E: Concentration Change — Removing a Reactant

Question (Jan 2023 Q22(f)):

In the equilibrium 2NO₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HNO₃(aq) + HNO₂(aq), water is added (which removes NO₂ from the gas phase by dissolving it). What happens?

Full-mark answer (2 marks): 1. Adding water removes NO₂ (reactant) from the equilibrium mixture 2. The equilibrium shifts to the left (towards reactants) to oppose the removal (to replace the removed NO₂) 3. This decreases the yield of products (HNO₃ and HNO₂)

Mark scheme requirement: Must identify what is removed AND the direction of shift. Either alone = 1/2.

3F: Adding a Base to an Acid-Dependent Equilibrium

Question (Oct 2024 Q19(b)(ii), modelled on Jan 2024 Q24(b)(ii)):

The equilibrium is Cl₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HClO(aq) + HCl(aq). What happens when NaOH is added?

Full-mark answer: 1. NaOH reacts with / removes H⁺ (from HCl) from the equilibrium 2. The equilibrium shifts to the right (to replace the removed H⁺) 3. This produces more HClO (and more Cl⁻ as Cl⁻ is also in the system)

Extended version (Jan 2024 Q24(b)(ii), 3 marks): 1. Adding acid adds H⁺, shifting Eq.1 left: H⁺ + ClO⁻ → HClO 2. This causes Eq.2 to shift right: HClO + Cl⁻ + H⁺ → Cl₂ + H₂O 3. Toxic Cl₂(g) is released

3G: The Industrial Compromise — Full Worked Example

Question (Jun 2024 Q19(c), 7 marks total):

Ethene hydration: C₂H₄(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ C₂H₅OH(g), ΔH is negative (exothermic). (i) Explain the temperature compromise (2 marks) (ii) Why is a pressure > 7 MPa suggested? (2 marks) (iii) Why not exceed 7 MPa? (1 mark) (iv) Suggest one advantage and one disadvantage of using more water (2 marks)

(i) Temperature compromise (2 marks): - The forward reaction is exothermic, so lower temperature would give a higher yield (shifts equilibrium right) - However, lower temperature gives a rate that is too slow to be economically viable - Therefore a compromise temperature is used

(ii) Pressure > 7 MPa (2 marks): - There are 2 mol of gas on the left (reactants) and 1 mol on the right (products) - Increasing pressure shifts the equilibrium to the right (fewer gas moles on product side), giving a greater yield of ethanol

(iii) Why not exceed 7 MPa (1 mark): - Higher pressures require special/expensive plant or equipment - (Also accepted: much higher energy costs to generate and maintain high pressure)

(iv) More water — advantage and disadvantage (2 marks): - Advantage: Pushes equilibrium to the right (increases yield) — water is a reactant - Disadvantage: More water is condensed with the ethanol product, making separation more difficult (or: catalyst may be washed from its support)

Worked Example 5: Catalyst Sustainability Argument

Question (Jan 2023 Q22(d)(ii)):

Suggest why the use of a catalyst makes the industrial process more sustainable.

Full-mark answer (1 mark, choose ONE): - Less energy needed (because a lower temperature can be used while maintaining an acceptable rate) - Lower temperature required (reduces fuel consumption) - Less fuel burned (reduces CO₂ emissions) - Reduced carbon footprint

NOT accepted: - "The catalyst can be reused" — true but not a sustainability argument per se - "It lowers the activation energy" — this is the MECHANISM, not the sustainability benefit - "It increases the rate" — this is the FUNCTION, not the sustainability benefit

The key linkage: Lower Eₐ → lower T can be used → less energy/fuel → lower CO₂ emissions → more sustainable. You need the chain of reasoning.

Worked Example 6: Catalyst Explanation Using Maxwell–Boltzmann (3 Marks)

Question (Oct 2023 Q21(b)(i), Jan 2024 Q24(d)):

Explain how a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction, with reference to the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.

Full 3-mark answer:

Mark Statement
1 The catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy
2 A greater proportion of molecules now have energy ≥ Eₐ (the new, lower value)
3 Therefore there is a higher proportion of successful collisions per unit time, increasing the rate

Alternative wording for Mark 3 (accepted): - "More molecules have sufficient energy to react upon collision" - "The rate of successful collisions increases"

NOT accepted: - "The catalyst gives molecules more energy" — it doesn't - "The distribution shifts" — it doesn't - "The catalyst lowers the temperature needed" — while true in industry, this is not what the M–B diagram shows

Diagram that scores full marks (Jan 2024 Q24(d), 4 marks):

Mark Requirement
1 Labelled axes (energy on x, number/fraction of molecules on y)
2 Single Maxwell–Boltzmann curve starting at the origin
3 Catalyst provides lower Eₐ (shown as a vertical line moved to the left)
4 More particles have E ≥ Eₐ with catalyst (shaded area or clear annotation)

Worked Example 7: Dynamic Equilibrium Misconceptions

Question (Jan 2024 Q14):

Which statement about dynamic equilibrium is NOT true? A. A catalyst changes the position of equilibrium B. At equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products are constant C. At equilibrium, the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the backward reaction D. Equilibrium can only be reached in a closed system

Answer: A — A catalyst does NOT change the position of equilibrium. It speeds up both forward and backward reactions equally.

Why the other options ARE true: - B: Correct — concentrations are constant (but NOT necessarily equal) - C: Correct — this is the defining feature of dynamic equilibrium - D: Correct — equilibrium requires a closed system so matter cannot escape

Exam insight: This is a classic "identify the false statement" question. Students often select C or D (which are true) because they seem "too simple." Always read carefully — the question asks which is NOT true.

Worked Example 8: Carbon Dioxide and Ocean Acidification (Equilibrium Application)

Question (Oct 2023 Q20(c), 3 marks):

Explain why increasing the concentration of CO₂ in the atmosphere increases the acidity of seawater. Relevant equilibrium: CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq)

Full 3-mark answer:

Mark Statement
1 Increasing atmospheric CO₂ increases the concentration of dissolved CO₂ in seawater
2 This shifts the first equilibrium to the right, increasing [H₂CO₃]
3 The increased [H₂CO₃] shifts the second equilibrium to the right, increasing [H⁺], so the acidity increases

Note: Mark 3 depends on Mark 2 being correct (dependent marking).

Exam Technique Summary for Chapter 2.8

Le Chatelier — The 3-Step Justification Template

Whenever you are asked to "explain" the effect of a change on an equilibrium:

  1. State the change applied (e.g., "temperature is decreased")
  2. State the direction of shift (e.g., "equilibrium shifts to the left/right")
  3. Give the reason (e.g., "to oppose the change / because the system opposes the decrease in temperature / because there are fewer gas moles on the product side")

Maxwell–Boltzmann — Decision Tree

When asked what happens to the M–B distribution:

What changed?
├── Temperature ↑
│   → Peak: lower, right-shifted
│   → Area: same
│   → Eₐ: unchanged
│   → Proportion above Eₐ: increases
├── Catalyst added
│   → Curve: unchanged
│   → Area: same
│   → Eₐ: decreases (shifts left)
│   → Proportion above Eₐ: increases
├── Gas removed (pressure ↓ at const T)
│   → Peak: lower (fewer molecules at all energies)
│   → Area: decreases
│   → Eₐ: unchanged (same threshold)
│   → Shape (per molecule): same
└── Pressure ↑ (more gas in same volume)
    → Peak: higher (more molecules at all energies)
    → Area: increases
    → Eₐ: unchanged
    → Shape (per molecule): same

Catalyst — What to Say vs What NOT to Say

Context DO Say DO NOT Say
How a catalyst works "Provides alternative pathway with lower Eₐ" "Gives molecules more energy"
M–B explanation "Greater proportion of molecules have E ≥ Eₐ" "Distribution shifts to the right"
Sustainability "Less energy needed / lower temperature / less CO₂" "Catalyst is reused" or "Lowers Eₐ"
Equilibrium effect "No effect on equilibrium position" "Shifts equilibrium"

Rate vs Yield — Never Confuse Them

Question Type Think About Keywords
"What happens to the rate?" Kinetics / collision theory Faster collisions, more E ≥ Eₐ, more frequent collisions
"What happens to the yield?" Le Chatelier / equilibrium position Equilibrium shifts, more product favoured
"What is the compromise?" Both Rate too slow at low T, yield too low at high T

Full-Mark Answer Pattern: Le Chatelier Explanation (2–3 marks)

TWO-MARK STRUCTURE:
  1. IDENTIFY the change (e.g. "increasing temperature")
  2. STATE the shift (e.g. "equilibrium shifts to the LEFT/RIGHT")
  3. LINK to the effect (e.g. "yield of product INCREASES/DECREASES")
  4. (Optional) Give the OPPOSING effect on rate

THREE-MARK STRUCTURE (requires cause + reasoning):
  1. State the change
  2. State which direction is favoured and WHY (exothermic/endothermic, or gas moles)
  3. State the consequence for yield

TEMPERATURE:
  ↑T → favours ENDOTHERMIC direction → shifts away from heat
  ↓T → favours EXOTHERMIC direction → shifts toward heat

PRESSURE:
  ↑P → favours side with FEWER gas molecules
  ↓P → favours side with MORE gas molecules

CONCENTRATION:
  Adding reactant → shifts RIGHT (toward products)
  Removing product → shifts RIGHT (toward products)
  Adding product → shifts LEFT (toward reactants)

Jun 2024 Q15(c) Catalyst only changes Eₐ — distribution curve is UNCHANGED

Must memorise:

What a catalyst DOES:
  ✓ Provides alternative reaction pathway
  ✓ Lowers activation energy
  ✓ Increases rate of both forward AND backward reactions equally
  ✓ Does NOT get used up
  ✓ Can be recycled/reused

What a catalyst does NOT do:
  ✗ Change the position of equilibrium
  ✗ Change the equilibrium constant
  ✗ Change the yield of product
  ✗ Change the average kinetic energy of molecules
  ✗ Change the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curve shape
  ✗ Change ΔH of the reaction
