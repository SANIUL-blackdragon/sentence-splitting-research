6.1 Enthalpy Change and Standard Conditions

Spec ref: 6.1

Exam-Ready Definitions

Enthalpy change (ΔH): The heat energy change at constant pressure. It is the difference between the enthalpy of the products and the enthalpy of the reactants.

Standard conditions: - Pressure: 100 kPa (1 atmosphere) - Temperature: 298 K (25 °C) - Concentration: 1 mol dm⁻³ for solutions - All substances in their standard states under these conditions

Standard state: The physical state of a substance under standard conditions (e.g., H₂(g), H₂O(l), NaCl(s), Br₂(l)).

Exam Examples with MS-Acceptable Answers

Example 1 — State the standard conditions used for enthalpy changes. Source: Jun 2022 Q18(a)(i) — 1 mark

Acceptable Answers Rejected Answers
298 K (or 25 °C) and 100 kPa "Room temperature" alone
100 kPa and 298 K 298°K (incorrect notation)
"Normal conditions" or "STP"

Example 2 — Which equation represents the standard enthalpy change of formation of Al₂O₃? Source: Jun 2022 Q1 — 1 mark (MCQ)

Correct answer: 2Al(s) + 1½O₂(g) → Al₂O₃(s)

Requirement Why Other Options Are Wrong
Exactly 1 mol of product formed Options forming 2 mol Al₂O₃ are wrong
Elements in their standard states O₂(g) not O(g); Al(s) not Al(g)
Correctly balanced Fractional coefficients (1½) are accepted

Example 3 — Which equation represents the standard enthalpy change of formation? Source: Oct 2024 Q3 — 1 mark (MCQ)

Correct answer: Cu(s) + C(s) + 1½O₂(g) → CuCO₃(s)

Key acceptability criteria: all reactants are elements in standard states, exactly 1 mol of compound product, balanced.

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Confusing standard enthalpy of formation with combustion. Formation has elements as reactants; combustion has O₂ as a reactant and CO₂/H₂O as products.
  2. Using atoms instead of elements. O(g) is NOT the standard state of oxygen — O₂(g) is.
  3. Forming 2 mol of product. Standard enthalpy of formation is ALWAYS per mole of product.
  4. Giving "room temperature" instead of 298 K. Mark schemes require the numerical value.
  5. Giving "1 atm" instead of 100 kPa. Edexcel IAL uses 100 kPa (not 101.3 kPa or 1 atm).

6.2 Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

Spec ref: 6.2

Exam-Ready Definitions

Exothermic reaction: A reaction in which heat energy is transferred to the surroundings. The products have less enthalpy than the reactants. ΔH is negative.

Endothermic reaction: A reaction in which heat energy is absorbed from the surroundings. The products have more enthalpy than the reactants. ΔH is positive.

Exam Examples with MS-Acceptable Answers

Example 1 — Which process on the enthalpy level diagram represents an exothermic change? Source: Jun 2023 Q1 — 1 mark (MCQ)

Correct answer: 2Cl(g) → Cl₂(g)

Process Exothermic/Endothermic Reason
2Cl(g) → Cl₂(g) Exothermic Bond formation releases energy
Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g) Endothermic Bond breaking absorbs energy
Cl(g) → Cl⁺(g) + e⁻ Endothermic Ionisation energy (removing electron)
Cl₂(g) → 2Cl⁺(g) + 2e⁻ Endothermic Both ionisation and bond breaking

Example 2 — Identify the overall ΔH arrow on an enthalpy level diagram with an intermediate. Source: Jun 2024 Q1 — 1 mark (MCQ)

Key concept: The overall ΔH is the difference between the initial reactant level and the final product level — NOT the intermediate level, and NOT the activation energy.

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Getting the sign wrong for endothermic reactions. Endothermic = ΔH positive. Students often default to negative.
  2. Confusing activation energy with enthalpy change. Ea is the "hump" on a reaction profile; ΔH is the overall level difference between reactants and products.
  3. Assuming all bond-breaking is endothermic and all bond-forming is exothermic without being able to explain why. Bond breaking requires energy input (overcoming the electrostatic attraction); bond forming releases energy (new bonds form).

6.3 Enthalpy Level Diagrams

Spec ref: 6.3

Exam-Ready Definitions

Enthalpy level diagram: A diagram showing the relative enthalpy levels of reactants and products, with an arrow indicating the enthalpy change (ΔH). For exothermic reactions, products are at a lower level than reactants (arrow points down). For endothermic reactions, products are at a higher level (arrow points up).

Exam Examples with MS-Acceptable Answers

Example 1 — Draw an enthalpy level diagram for the combustion of octane. Source: Jun 2022 Q18(a)(iii) — 2 marks

Required elements: | Marking Point | MS Requirement | |--------------|----------------| | Y-axis label | Must be "enthalpy" (NOT "ΔH", NOT "energy change") | | Reactants level | Higher than products (exothermic) | | Products level | Lower than reactants | | Arrow | Single-headed arrow from reactants level to products level, labelled ΔH꜀ | | Labels | "Reactants" and "Products" on correct levels |

Example 2 — Identify the correct arrow on a complex enthalpy level diagram with an intermediate. Source: Jun 2024 Q1 — 1 mark (MCQ)

The overall ΔH arrow spans from the initial reactant level to the final product level, passing through (but not ending at) any intermediate.

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Labelling the y-axis as "ΔH" or "energy change" — must be "enthalpy". This is explicitly penalised in Jun 2022 MS.
  2. Drawing a double-headed arrow — ΔH is a single direction (reactants → products).
  3. Including activation energy on a simple level diagram. Unless the question asks for a reaction profile (with Ea), a level diagram only shows the start and end enthalpy levels.
  4. Labelling the arrow as "−ΔH" — the arrow itself represents ΔH; if the diagram correctly shows products lower than reactants, the value of ΔH is already negative.

6.4(i) Standard Enthalpy Change of Reaction (ΔH°r)

Spec ref: 6.4(i)

Exam-Ready Definition

Standard enthalpy change of reaction (ΔH°r): The enthalpy change when the molar quantities of reactants shown in the stoichiometric equation react under standard conditions (100 kPa, 298 K).

Note: The value depends on the stoichiometry of the equation as written. ΔH°r for 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O is twice ΔH°r for H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O.

6.4(ii) Standard Enthalpy Change of Formation (ΔH°f)

Spec ref: 6.4(ii)

Exam-Ready Definition

Standard enthalpy change of formation (ΔH°f): The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states under standard conditions (100 kPa, 298 K).

Exam Examples with MS-Acceptable Answers

Example 1 — State the meaning of the term standard enthalpy change of formation. Source: Jan 2024 Q22(a) — 2 marks

MS acceptable answer: 1 mol of substance formed from elements in standard states.

Both elements of the definition are required: | Mark | Requirement | |------|------------| | M1 | "1 mol" / "one mole" of compound | | M2 | "elements in standard states" / "elements in their standard states under standard conditions" |

Note: "Standard conditions" alone is NOT sufficient — the "elements" requirement is separate and must be stated.

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Forgetting "1 mole". The definition must specify exactly 1 mole of compound is formed.
  2. Saying "from its constituent elements" without "standard states". The standard states part is a separate mark.
  3. Confusing formation with atomisation. Formation produces a compound from elements; atomisation produces gaseous atoms from an element.

6.4(iii) Standard Enthalpy Change of Combustion (ΔH°c)

Spec ref: 6.4(iii)

Exam-Ready Definition

Standard enthalpy change of combustion (ΔH°c): The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a substance reacts completely with oxygen under standard conditions (100 kPa, 298 K), with all reactants and products in their standard states.

Exam Examples with MS-Acceptable Answers

Example 1 — Write an equation for the complete combustion of 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (isooctane). Source: Jun 2022 Q18(a)(ii) — 2 marks

Correct equation: C₈H₁₈ + 12½O₂ → 8CO₂ + 9H₂O

Marking Point Requirement
M1 Correct molecular formula C₈H₁₈ (from the name 2,2,4-trimethylpentane)
M2 Correctly balanced equation (C₈H₁₈ + 12½O₂ → 8CO₂ + 9H₂O)

State symbols are NOT required for this question. Fractional coefficients are accepted.

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Incomplete combustion — writing CO instead of CO₂, or not balancing fully.
  2. Not identifying the correct molecular formula from a structural/branched name.
  3. Assuming ΔH°c is always per mole of O₂ — it is per mole of the substance being combusted.

6.4(iv) Standard Enthalpy Change of Neutralisation (ΔH°neut)

Spec ref: 6.4(iv)

Exam-Ready Definition

Standard enthalpy change of neutralisation (ΔH°neut): The enthalpy change when 1 mole of water is formed from the reaction between an acid and an alkali under standard conditions (100 kPa, 298 K), with all solutions at 1 mol dm⁻³.

Exam Examples with MS-Acceptable Answers

Example 1 — Define the standard enthalpy change of neutralisation. Source: Oct 2023 Q18(a)(ii) — 2 marks

MS acceptable answer must include both elements: | Mark | Required Element | |------|-----------------| | M1 | Heat energy change / enthalpy change under standard conditions (298 K, 100 kPa) | | M2 | When 1 mol of water is formed / per mole of water produced |

Example 2 — Write the ionic equation for the neutralisation of HCl with NaOH. Source: Oct 2023 Q18(a)(i) — 1 mark

Correct answers: - H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O - H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O (also accepted)

State symbols NOT required.

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Defining per mole of acid or alkali — must be per mole of WATER produced.
  2. Omitting "standard conditions" — this is a "standard" enthalpy change.
  3. Confusing the ionic equation — the net ionic equation for any strong acid–strong base neutralisation is always H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

6.4(v) Standard Enthalpy Change of Atomisation (ΔH°at)

Spec ref: 6.4(v)

Exam-Ready Definition

Standard enthalpy change of atomisation (ΔH°at): The enthalpy change when 1 mole of gaseous atoms is formed from an element in its standard state under standard conditions (100 kPa, 298 K).

Exam Examples with MS-Acceptable Answers

Example 1 — Which equation represents the standard enthalpy change of atomisation of bromine? Source: Jan 2023 Q2 — 1 mark (MCQ)

Correct answer: ½Br₂(l) → Br(g)

Requirement Why Other Options Fail
1 mol of gaseous atoms produced Options producing 2 mol are wrong
Element in its standard state Br₂(l) is the standard state (not Br₂(g))
Gaseous atom as product Product must be Br(g)

Example 2 — Which process has an enthalpy change equal to 4 × 485 kJ mol⁻¹ (the C–F mean bond enthalpy)? Source: Oct 2023 Q1 — 1 mark (MCQ)

Correct answer: CF₄(g) → C(g) + 4F(g) (breaking all four C–F bonds)

This is NOT the enthalpy of atomisation of carbon or fluorine individually — it is the bond-breaking process for the compound.

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Using the wrong standard state. For bromine, the standard state is liquid (Br₂(l)), NOT gas. For mercury, it is liquid. For other elements, check the Data Booklet.
  2. Producing 2 mol of atoms instead of 1 mol. Atomisation is always per mole of gaseous atoms formed.
  3. Confusing atomisation with bond dissociation. Atomisation of a diatomic element (e.g., ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g)) equals the bond dissociation enthalpy. But atomisation of a compound involves breaking ALL bonds.

6.5(i) Energy Transferred: q = mcΔT

Spec ref: 6.5(i)

Exam-Ready Definition / Formula

Energy transferred: q = m × c × ΔT

Where: - q = energy transferred (in joules, J) - m = mass of the solution/substance being heated (in grams, g) - c = specific heat capacity (in J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ or J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹); for water, c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ - ΔT = temperature change (in °C or K)

Note: Use the mass of the solution (usually water), NOT the mass of the reactant.

Exam Examples with MS-Acceptable Answers

Example 1 — Calculate the enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol from calorimetry data. Source: Jun 2022 Q18(b)(i) — 4 marks

Given: 100.0 g water, ΔT = 13.2 °C, mass of ethanol burned = 0.305 g

Step Calculation Marks
Heat energy q = 100.0 × 4.18 × 13.2 = 5517.6 J 1 mark
Moles of ethanol n = 0.305 / 46.0 = 0.00663 mol 1 mark
Per mole 5517.6 / 0.00663 = 831,780 J mol⁻¹ 1 mark
Final answer −832 kJ mol⁻¹ (3 SF, negative sign, units) 1 mark

Acceptable range: −830 to −832 kJ mol⁻¹. Common error rejected: adding mass of ethanol (0.305 g) to water mass (100 g).

Example 2 — Calculate ΔH from thermometric titration data. Source: Jun 2024 Q17(c) — 5 marks

Given: 30.0 cm³ of 1.30 mol dm⁻³ NH₃(aq), 21.0 cm³ of HCl(aq) (end-point), max temperature 27.6 °C, initial temperature 20.7 °C

Step Calculation Marks
Mass of solution 30.0 + 21.0 = 51.0 g (assume density = 1.00 g cm⁻³) 1 mark
ΔT 27.6 − 20.7 = 6.9 °C 1 mark
q 51.0 × 4.18 × 6.9 = 1471 J 1 mark
n(NH₃) at end-point 1.30 × 30.0/1000 = 0.0390 mol 1 mark
ΔH −1471/0.0390 = −37.7 kJ mol⁻¹ 1 mark (sign + SF)

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Including the mass of the fuel/reactant in the "m" of q = mcΔT. Only the solution mass (usually water) is used.
  2. Forgetting to convert J to kJ before dividing by moles (or vice versa). Ensure consistent units.
  3. Forgetting the negative sign for exothermic reactions.
  4. Wrong specific heat capacity — water is 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹; some experiments use different solutions (e.g., Jun 2023 Q2a tests whether using 4.18 instead of 3.85 for an acid solution affects the result).
  5. Using ΔT in kelvin incorrectly. A change of 1 °C = a change of 1 K, so the numerical value of ΔT is the same in both units.

6.5(ii) Enthalpy Change of Reaction in kJ mol⁻¹

Spec ref: 6.5(ii)

Exam-Ready Definition

To convert the energy transferred (q) to an enthalpy change per mole (ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹):

ΔH = −q / n (for exothermic reactions; drop the negative for endothermic)

Where n = moles of the limiting reactant (or moles of water formed for neutralisation).

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Dividing by the wrong number of moles. For neutralisation, divide by moles of water formed. For combustion, divide by moles of fuel burned.
  2. Confusing "per mole of reaction" with "per mole of a particular substance". The definition depends on which enthalpy change is being calculated.

6.6(i) Hess's Law: Constructing Enthalpy Cycles

Spec ref: 6.6(i), 6.7

Exam-Ready Definition

Hess's Law: The total enthalpy change for a chemical reaction is independent of the route by which the chemical change occurs, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.

Exam Examples with MS-Acceptable Answers

Example 1 — Complete the enthalpy cycle for the formation of NH₄NO₃. Source: Jan 2023 Q22(g)(i) — 2 marks

Given a cycle with elements at top, NH₄NO₃(s) and its dissolution products at bottom: - Left arrow (reverse of ΔHf): −220.2 kJ mol⁻¹ (value is reversed from +220.2, so placed as the enthalpy change going from elements to NH₄NO₃(s)) - Right arrow (reverse of dissolution): +25.6 kJ mol⁻¹

MS note: The cycle requires careful attention to direction. When an arrow is reversed, the sign of ΔH flips.

Example 2 — Complete the Hess cycle for MgSO₄ hydration. Source: Oct 2024 Q16(b)(ii) — 2 marks

The cycle has: - Top row: MgSO₄(s) + 7H₂O(l) → MgSO₄·7H₂O(s) - Both arrows pointing downward to MgSO₄(aq) - The direct route (experimentally measured): ΔH₁ = −85.2 kJ mol⁻¹ - The two-step route: ΔH₂ (dissolution of anhydrous) and ΔH₃ (hydration)

MS requires: both arrows must point down to the aqueous species at the bottom; species must include state symbols.

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Arrows pointing in the wrong direction. The arrows on a Hess cycle must all flow in a consistent direction (usually from elements to products via the alternative route).
  2. Forgetting to reverse the sign when using a ΔH value in the opposite direction to how it was defined.
  3. Confusing which ΔH values go on which arrow. Formation enthalpies apply to the arrow from elements to compound.

6.6(ii) Hess's Law: Calculating Enthalpy Changes from Data

Spec ref: 6.6(ii)

Exam-Ready Approach

Using ΔHf values: ΔHr = ΣΔHf(products) − ΣΔHf(reactants)

Using ΔHc values: ΔHr = ΣΔHc(reactants) − ΣΔHc(products)

Exam Examples with MS-Acceptable Answers

Example 1 — Calculate ΔHr from a completed Hess cycle. Source: Jan 2023 Q22(g)(ii) — 1 mark

Given cycle values: ΔH(neutralisation) = −32.6, ΔHf(NH₄NO₃, s) = −365.6, reverse ΔHf = +220.2, reverse dissolution = +25.6

ΔHr = −(−32.6) − (−220.2) + (−365.6) + 25.6 = −87.2 kJ mol⁻¹

MS allows TE on cycle values from (g)(i).

Example 2 — Use Hess's Law to calculate an unknown enthalpy change. Source: Jan 2024 Q22(c) — 2 marks

Given: ΔHr₁ (reaction 1) = −462 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔHr₂ (from calorimetry) = −145 kJ mol⁻¹ (the −286 kJ mol⁻¹ value is ΔHf°(H₂O, l), not needed for direct Hess subtraction).

From the cycle: ΔH(target) = ΔHr₂ − ΔHr₁ ΔH(target) = (−145) − (−462) ΔH(target) = −145 + 462 ΔH(target) = +317 kJ mol⁻¹

MS requires: expression mark (showing the cycle relationship) + calculation mark. TE allowed from part (b).

Example 3 — Which expression gives ΔH for BaCO₃ decomposition using ΔHf values? Source: Oct 2023 Q2 — 1 mark (MCQ)

Correct answer: ΔHf(BaO) + ΔHf(CO₂) − ΔHf(BaCO₃)

= (−554) + (−394) − (−1216) = +268 kJ mol⁻¹

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Reversing the formula. ΔHr = ΣΔHf(products) − ΣΔHf(reactants). Students sometimes subtract products from reactants.
  2. Forgetting to multiply ΔHf by the stoichiometric coefficient. If the equation has 2 mol of a product, you need 2 × ΔHf.
  3. Sign errors in cycle arithmetic. With 3–4 terms, it is very easy to make a sign error. Write each term carefully.
  4. Reversing the ΔHc formula. For combustion data: ΔHr = ΣΔHc(reactants) − ΣΔHc(products) (the OPPOSITE of the formation formula).

6.9 Bond Enthalpy and Mean Bond Enhalpy

Spec ref: 6.9

Exam-Ready Definitions

Bond enthalpy (bond dissociation enthalpy): The enthalpy change when one mole of a specific covalent bond in a molecule in the gaseous state is broken.

Mean bond enthalpy: The average value of the bond enthalpy of a particular type of bond, averaged over a range of different compounds. It is found in the Data Booklet.

Key distinction: Bond enthalpy is for a specific bond in a specific molecule. Mean bond enthalpy is an average across many molecules.

Calculation method: ΔH = Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds formed)

6.11 Bond Enthalpy Data as Indicator of Reactivity

1. Enthalpy Level Diagrams

Key Concepts

An enthalpy level diagram is a visual representation of the enthalpy changes during a chemical reaction. It shows the relative enthalpy (energy content) of reactants and products as horizontal lines, with the vertical axis representing enthalpy.

Rules for drawing enthalpy level diagrams:

  1. Y-axis must be labelled "enthalpy" — not "ΔH", not "energy change", not "energy". This is strictly required by Edexcel mark schemes (penalised in Jun 2022 Q18(a)(iii)).

  2. For exothermic reactions: products line is BELOW reactants line. The arrow points downward. ΔH is negative.

  3. For endothermic reactions: products line is ABOVE reactants line. The arrow points upward. ΔH is positive.

  4. Arrow must be single-headed, going from reactant level to product level, labelled ΔH (with the appropriate subscript: ΔH꜀ for combustion, ΔHf for formation, etc.).

  5. Do NOT draw activation energy on a simple enthalpy level diagram unless the question specifically asks for a "reaction profile" or "activation energy diagram".

  6. When an intermediate is present (as in Jun 2024 Q1), the intermediate has its own horizontal line between reactants and products. The overall ΔH arrow still goes from the initial reactant level to the final product level — NOT to or from the intermediate.

Distinguishing diagram types:

Diagram Type Shows Key Features
Enthalpy level diagram Start and end enthalpy only Two horizontal lines, one arrow
Reaction profile Full energy pathway Includes activation energy "hump" and possibly an intermediate
Hess cycle Alternative routes to same products Triangle or other cycle shape with enthalpy values on arrows

Worked Example 1: Drawing a Combustion Enthalpy Level Diagram

Question (based on Jun 2022 Q18(a)(iii)): Draw an enthalpy level diagram for the complete combustion of octane (C₈H₁₈), which has ΔH꜀ = −5461 kJ mol⁻¹.

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Draw a vertical y-axis and label it "enthalpy" (not "energy" or "ΔH").

Step 2: Draw a horizontal line near the top of the diagram. Label it "Reactants" and write "C₈H₁₈(l) + 12½O₂(g)" next to it.

Step 3: Draw a horizontal line significantly lower than the reactants line. Label it "Products" and write "8CO₂(g) + 9H₂O(l)" next to it. The products line must be noticeably lower because the reaction is highly exothermic.

Step 4: Draw a single-headed vertical arrow from the reactants line down to the products line. Label this arrow "ΔH꜀ = −5461 kJ mol⁻¹".

Common pitfall: Do NOT draw a double-headed arrow. The arrow represents the direction of the enthalpy change (reactants → products), even though the value is negative.

Common Pitfalls for Enthalpy Level Diagrams

  1. Wrong y-axis label. Must be "enthalpy". Mark schemes explicitly reject "ΔH", "energy", "energy change", or "heat energy".
  2. Double-headed arrow. The arrow should be single-headed, showing the direction of the reaction.
  3. Labelling the arrow as "−ΔH". The arrow itself represents ΔH; if you draw it pointing down, the value of ΔH is negative. Writing "−ΔH" is a double-negative.
  4. Drawing Ea on a level diagram. Only include the activation energy hump if asked for a "reaction profile".

2. Calorimetry Calculations (q = mcΔT)

Key Concepts

Calorimetry is the experimental determination of enthalpy changes by measuring temperature changes in a known mass of solution.

The fundamental equation:

q = m × c × ΔT

Where: - q = heat energy transferred (J) - m = mass of solution being heated (g) - c = specific heat capacity (J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹); for dilute aqueous solutions, use 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ - ΔT = temperature change (°C)

Converting to enthalpy change per mole:

ΔH = −q / n (the negative sign is applied for exothermic reactions)

Where n = moles of the limiting reactant.

Important notes:

  1. Mass (m): Always use the mass of the solution being heated, NOT the mass of the solid or fuel being reacted/burned. For example, if you burn 0.305 g of ethanol in 100.0 g of water, m = 100.0 g.

  2. Assumption of density: For dilute aqueous solutions, assume the density is 1.00 g cm⁻³, so 1 cm³ = 1 g. Therefore, if you have 50.0 cm³ of solution, the mass is 50.0 g.

  3. Units: q is in joules (J). Convert to kJ before or after dividing by moles. Final ΔH should be in kJ mol⁻¹.

  4. Sign: Exothermic reactions have a negative ΔH. Endothermic reactions have a positive ΔH. For combustion and neutralisation, the answer is negative.

Worked Example 2: Calorimetry — Enthalpy of Combustion (Jun 2022 Q18(b)(i))

Question: In an experiment, 0.305 g of ethanol was burned and used to heat 100.0 g of water. The temperature of the water increased by 13.2 °C. Calculate the enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol. (Mₜ(C₂H₅OH) = 46.0 g mol⁻¹)

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Calculate the heat energy transferred to the water.

q = m × c × ΔT q = 100.0 × 4.18 × 13.2 q = 5517.6 J

Step 2: Calculate the moles of ethanol burned.

n = mass / Mₜ n = 0.305 / 46.0 n = 0.00663 mol

Step 3: Calculate the enthalpy change per mole.

ΔH = −q / n ΔH = −5517.6 / 0.00663 ΔH = −831,780 J mol⁻¹ ΔH = −832 kJ mol⁻¹ (to 3 SF)

Answer: −832 kJ mol⁻¹

Mark allocation: 1 mark for q, 1 mark for n, 1 mark for working per mole, 1 mark for final answer with correct sign, SF, and units.

Common pitfall: Adding the mass of ethanol (0.305 g) to the mass of water (100.0 g) to get m = 100.305 g. This is WRONG — the calorimeter measures the heat absorbed by the water only. The mark scheme explicitly rejects this.

Worked Example 3: Thermometric Titration — Enthalpy of Neutralisation (Jun 2024 Q17)

Question: 30.0 cm³ of 1.30 mol dm⁻³ ammonia solution (NH₃(aq)) was titrated with hydrochloric acid (HCl(aq)). The graph of temperature against volume of acid added was used to determine an end-point at 21.0 cm³ and a maximum temperature of 27.6 °C. The initial temperature was 20.7 °C. Calculate the enthalpy of neutralisation.

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Calculate the total mass of solution.

Total volume = 30.0 + 21.0 = 51.0 cm³ Assuming density = 1.00 g cm⁻³: mass = 51.0 g

Step 2: Calculate ΔT.

ΔT = 27.6 − 20.7 = 6.9 °C

Step 3: Calculate the heat energy transferred.

q = m × c × ΔT q = 51.0 × 4.18 × 6.9 q = 1471 J q = 1.471 kJ

Step 4: Calculate the moles of NH₃ neutralised at the end-point.

The 30.0 cm³ of 1.30 mol dm⁻³ NH₃ was the analyte (the solution being titrated). The 21.0 cm³ of HCl was the titrant (added from the burette). At the end-point, all the NH₃ has reacted, so moles of NH₃ neutralised = initial moles of NH₃ in the flask:

n(NH₃) = c × V = 1.30 × (30.0/1000) = 0.0390 mol

By 1:1 stoichiometry (NH₃ + HCl → NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻), this equals the moles of HCl added and the moles of NH₄⁺ formed.

(Note: this is a weak-base/strong-acid neutralisation, so the experimental ΔH is less exothermic than the standard ΔH°neut of strong acid + strong base, ≈ −57 kJ mol⁻¹. Also, no water is formed — the product is NH₄⁺(aq) — so strictly this is the enthalpy change per mole of NH₄⁺ formed, not per mole of water.)

Step 5: Calculate ΔH per mole of NH₃ neutralised.

ΔH = −q / n(NH₃) ΔH = −1.471 / 0.0390 ΔH = −37.7 kJ mol⁻¹

Answer: −37.7 kJ mol⁻¹

Note on graph reading: The maximum temperature on the graph is NOT used directly. Instead, you draw two lines of best fit (before and after the end-point) and extrapolate both to the time of mixing (120 s). The difference between the extrapolated lines at 120 s gives the true ΔT.

Worked Example 4: Enthalpy of Solution (Oct 2024 Q16(b)(i))

Question: 6.00 g of anhydrous magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄) was added to 100 cm³ of water. The temperature increased by 12.8 °C. Calculate the enthalpy change of solution of MgSO₄.

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Calculate heat energy transferred.

q = m × c × ΔT q = 100 × 4.18 × 12.8 q = 5350 J q = 5.35 kJ

(Note: mass of solution ≈ 100 g because 6.00 g of solid dissolves in 100 g of water, giving ≈106 g. However, the mark scheme accepts m = 100 g as the mass of water. Using 106 g gives a slightly different answer but is also accepted with TE.)

Step 2: Calculate moles of MgSO₄.

Mₜ(MgSO₄) = 24.3 + 32.1 + 4×16.0 = 120.4 g mol⁻¹ n = 6.00 / 120.4 = 0.0498 mol

Step 3: Calculate ΔH per mole.

ΔH = −5.35 / 0.0498 ΔH = −107 kJ mol⁻¹ (exothermic)

Answer: −107 kJ mol⁻¹

The MS requires: 2–3 SF, negative sign, units (kJ mol⁻¹).

Common Pitfalls for Calorimetry

  1. Wrong mass. Use the solution mass (usually water), NOT the mass of the solid/fuel being reacted.
  2. Missing the negative sign. Exothermic = negative ΔH. This is penalised.
  3. Not dividing by moles. q = mcΔT gives the total heat for the experiment. You must divide by the number of moles of the limiting reactant to get kJ mol⁻¹.
  4. Reading ΔT from the peak instead of extrapolating. In thermometric titrations, the graph shows continued heat loss during the experiment. You must extrapolate both lines to the mixing point.
  5. Significant figures. 1 SF is penalised. 2–3 SF is expected. The mark scheme allows TE on intermediate rounding.

3. Hess's Law Cycles

Key Concepts

Hess's Law: The total enthalpy change for a chemical reaction is independent of the route by which the chemical change occurs, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.

This means you can calculate an enthalpy change that cannot be measured directly by using alternative routes with known enthalpy changes.

Two main approaches:

Approach A — Using ΔHf values (formation data):

         Elements (in standard states)
         /                          \
   ΔHf(R1) + ΔHf(R2) + ...    ΔHf(P1) + ΔHf(P2) + ...
       /                              \
  Reactants  ────── ΔHr ──────→  Products

Formula: ΔHr = ΣΔHf(products) − ΣΔHf(reactants)

Approach B — Using ΔHc values (combustion data):

  Reactants                           Products
     |                                  |
   ΔHc(R1) + ΔHc(R2) + ...       ΔHc(P1) + ΔHc(P2) + ...
     |                                  |
     └────→  Combustion products  ←─────┘

Formula: ΔHr = ΣΔHc(reactants) − ΣΔHc(products)

Note the REVERSAL compared to the formation formula. This is because combustion products are on the "other side" of the cycle relative to the reaction.

Key rules for constructing cycles:

  1. Elements always go at the top of the cycle.
  2. All arrows must flow in a consistent direction.
  3. If you reverse a process, reverse the sign of ΔH.
  4. If you multiply a process by a factor, multiply ΔH by the same factor.
  5. The sum of ΔH values along any route from start to finish must be the same.

Worked Example 5: Hess's Law Using ΔHf Values (Oct 2023 Q2)

Question (MCQ): Which expression gives ΔH for the thermal decomposition of BaCO₃?

BaCO₃(s) → BaO(s) + CO₂(g)

Given: ΔHf(BaCO₃) = −1216 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔHf(BaO) = −554 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔHf(CO₂) = −394 kJ mol⁻¹

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Identify the formula.

ΔHr = ΣΔHf(products) − ΣΔHf(reactants)

Step 2: Apply to this reaction.

Products: BaO(s) and CO₂(g) Reactants: BaCO₃(s)

ΔHr = [ΔHf(BaO) + ΔHf(CO₂)] − [ΔHf(BaCO₃)] ΔHr = [(−554) + (−394)] − [(−1216)] ΔHr = (−948) − (−1216) ΔHr = −948 + 1216 ΔHr = +268 kJ mol⁻¹

Answer: +268 kJ mol⁻¹ (endothermic, as expected for thermal decomposition)

The MCQ answer was option A: ΔHf(BaO) + ΔHf(CO₂) − ΔHf(BaCO₃)

Worked Example 6: Hess's Law Cycle Completion and Calculation (Jan 2024 Q22)

Question: The enthalpy change for the reaction of calcium oxide with hydrochloric acid was determined experimentally as ΔHr₂ = −145 kJ mol⁻¹.

The reaction is: CaO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ΔHr₂ = −145 kJ mol⁻¹

Another reaction has: Ca(OH)₂(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l) ΔHr₁ = −462 kJ mol⁻¹

Use Hess's Law to calculate the enthalpy change for: CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(s)

(Note: the value −286 kJ mol⁻¹ is ΔHf°(H₂O, l) — the standard enthalpy of formation of water — NOT the enthalpy of neutralisation. The enthalpy of neutralisation of a strong acid with a strong base is ≈ −57 kJ mol⁻¹. ΔHf°(H₂O, l) = −286 kJ mol⁻¹ appears in some exam mark schemes as a separate cycle term, but is not needed for this direct Hess subtraction.)

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Construct the Hess cycle.

  CaO(s) + H₂O(l)  ──ΔHtarget──→  Ca(OH)₂(s)
      |                                  |
      | ΔHr₂ = −145                      | reverse ΔHr₁ = +462
      v                                  v
  CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)  ←────────  CaCl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
  (via CaO + 2HCl)                  (via Ca(OH)₂ + 2HCl)

Both indirect routes pass through CaCl₂(aq) as a common intermediate. The 2 mol HCl(aq) cancels on each side. One mole of H₂O(l) also cancels.

Step 2: Apply Hess's Law (direct subtraction).

ΔH(target) = ΔHr₂ − ΔHr₁ ΔH(target) = (−145) − (−462) ΔH(target) = −145 + 462 ΔH(target) = +317 kJ mol⁻¹ (endothermic)

Answer: +317 kJ mol⁻¹

(Note: this endothermic value reflects the fabricated data values in this question — the actual slaking of lime is exothermic at ≈ −65 kJ mol⁻¹. The exam-style data here is chosen to test the Hess subtraction method, not to reflect real thermodynamic data.)

Mark allocation: 1 mark for the expression (showing the cycle relationship ΔHtarget = ΔHr₂ − ΔHr₁), 1 mark for the calculation (+317 kJ mol⁻¹). TE allowed from part (b).

Worked Example 7: Hess's Law for Hydration (Oct 2024 Q16(b)(ii)–(iii))

Question: The enthalpy change of solution of anhydrous MgSO₄ was found experimentally to be −85.2 kJ mol⁻¹. The enthalpy change of solution of MgSO₄·7H₂O is +15.8 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the enthalpy change of hydration of MgSO₄.

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Construct the Hess cycle.

  MgSO₄(s) + 7H₂O(l)  ──ΔH(hydration)──→  MgSO₄·7H₂O(s)
        |                                            |
        | ΔH(soln, anhydrous) = −85.2                 | ΔH(soln, hydrated) = +15.8
        |                                            |
        v                                            v
                  MgSO₄(aq)  +  7H₂O(l)

Step 2: Apply Hess's Law.

Going from MgSO₄(s) + 7H₂O(l) to MgSO₄(aq) + 7H₂O(l):

Via hydration route: ΔH(hydration) + ΔH(soln, hydrated) = ΔH(hydration) + 15.8 Via direct route: ΔH(soln, anhydrous) = −85.2

These must be equal: ΔH(hydration) + 15.8 = −85.2 ΔH(hydration) = −85.2 − 15.8 ΔH(hydration) = −101 kJ mol⁻¹

Answer: −101 kJ mol⁻¹

Mark allocation: 2 marks for the cycle completion (both arrows pointing down, correct species), 2 marks for the calculation.

Common Pitfalls for Hess's Law

  1. Sign errors. This is the most common error. When an arrow is reversed, the sign of ΔH must flip. Write each term carefully and check signs.
  2. Using the wrong formula. Remember: ΔHr = ΣΔHf(products) − ΣΔHf(reactants) but ΔHr = ΣΔHc(reactants) − ΣΔHc(products). The combustion formula is REVERSED.
  3. Not multiplying ΔHf by the stoichiometric coefficient. If the equation has 2CO₂, you need 2 × ΔHf(CO₂).
  4. Forgetting that the cycle must form a closed loop. The sum of ΔH values going one way must equal the sum going the other way.

4. Bond Enthalpy Calculations

Key Concepts

Bond enthalpy (bond dissociation enthalpy): The enthalpy change when one mole of a specific covalent bond in a gaseous molecule is broken.

Mean bond enthalpy: The average enthalpy change for breaking one mole of a particular type of bond, averaged over many different compounds. These are the values found in the Data Booklet.

The bond enthalpy calculation formula:

ΔH = Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds formed)

This formula works because: - Breaking bonds is ENDOTHERMIC (absorbs energy, positive contribution) - Forming bonds is EXOTHERMIC (releases energy, negative contribution) - Net ΔH = energy in − energy out

Steps for a bond enthalpy calculation:

  1. Write the balanced equation with all species as gases (if possible).
  2. Count the number and type of each bond in the reactant molecules.
  3. Count the number and type of each bond in the product molecules.
  4. Calculate Σ(bonds broken) = Σ(nᵢ × bond enthalpy of bond i) for reactants.
  5. Calculate Σ(bonds formed) = Σ(nⱼ × bond enthalpy of bond j) for products.
  6. ΔH = Σ(broken) − Σ(formed).

Counting bonds in a molecule:

For a hydrocarbon CₐH_b: - Number of C–C bonds = a − 1 (for a straight chain) - Number of C–H bonds = b

For molecules with multiple bond types, draw the displayed formula or use systematic counting.

Worked Example 8: Bond Enthalpy Calculation — Combustion of Octane (Jun 2023 Q20(a)(i))

Question: Calculate the enthalpy change of combustion of octane (C₈H₁₈) using mean bond enthalpies.

Bond enthalpies (kJ mol⁻¹): C–C = 347, C–H = 413, O=O = 498, C=O = 805, O–H = 464

Equation: C₈H₁₈(l) + 12½O₂(g) → 8CO₂(g) + 9H₂O(l)

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Count bonds in reactants.

C₈H₁₈ (octane): - C–C bonds: For a straight chain of 8 carbons: 8 − 1 = 7 C–C bonds - C–H bonds: From the formula C₈H₁₈, there are 18 hydrogen atoms = 18 C–H bonds

O₂: - O=O bonds: 12½ O₂ molecules = 12.5 O=O bonds

Step 2: Calculate Σ(bonds broken).

Σ(broken) = (7 × 347) + (18 × 413) + (12.5 × 498) Σ(broken) = 2429 + 7434 + 6225 Σ(broken) = 16088 kJ mol⁻¹

Step 3: Count bonds in products.

CO₂: - Each CO₂ has 2 C=O bonds - 8 × CO₂ = 16 C=O bonds

H₂O: - Each H₂O has 2 O–H bonds - 9 × H₂O = 18 O–H bonds

Step 4: Calculate Σ(bonds formed).

Σ(formed) = (16 × 805) + (18 × 464) Σ(formed) = 12880 + 8352 Σ(formed) = 21232 kJ mol⁻¹

Step 5: Calculate ΔH.

ΔH = Σ(broken) − Σ(formed) ΔH = 16088 − 21232 ΔH = −5144 kJ mol⁻¹

Answer: −5144 kJ mol⁻¹

Note: The data book value for ΔH꜀ of octane is approximately −5470 kJ mol⁻¹. The bond enthalpy value differs because: 1. Mean bond enthalpies are averaged over many compounds, not specific to octane. 2. Octane is a liquid and water is a liquid, but bond enthalpies are for the gas phase. 3. The enthalpies of vaporisation of octane and water are not included.

Worked Example: Calculating an Unknown Bond Enthalpy from ΔH

Question (based on Jan 2024 Q24(a)(i)): The reaction S₈(s) + 24F₂(g) → 8SF₆(g) has ΔH = −9672 kJ mol⁻¹. Given that the S–S bond enthalpy in S₈ is 268 kJ mol⁻¹ and the F–F bond enthalpy is 151 kJ mol⁻¹, calculate the S–F bond enthalpy.

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Count bonds broken.

S₈ ring: 8 S–S bonds F₂: 24 F–F bonds (24 mol F₂ → 24 mol F–F bonds)

Σ(broken) = (8 × 268) + (24 × 151) Σ(broken) = 2144 + 3624 Σ(broken) = 5768 kJ mol⁻¹

Step 2: Relate to bonds formed using the ΔH equation.

ΔH = Σ(broken) − Σ(formed) −9672 = 5768 − Σ(formed) Σ(formed) = 5768 + 9672 Σ(formed) = 15440 kJ mol⁻¹

Step 3: Count S–F bonds formed.

8 SF₆ molecules, each with 6 S–F bonds = 48 S–F bonds total.

Step 4: Calculate S–F bond enthalpy.

S–F = 15440 / 48 S–F = 322 kJ mol⁻¹

Answer: 322 kJ mol⁻¹

Note on limitations (Jan 2024 Q24(a)(ii), 2 marks): 1. Bond enthalpy data is for the gas phase, but S₈ is a solid at room temperature — the enthalpy of sublimation of S₈ is not accounted for. 2. Bond enthalpy is an average (mean) value — the S–S bonds in the S₈ ring may differ from the "average" S–S bond enthalpy in the Data Booklet.

Common Pitfalls for Bond Enthalpy Calculations

  1. Incorrect bond counting. For C₈H₁₈: C–C bonds = 7 (not 8), C–H bonds = 18. Always verify by drawing the structure or using the formula.
  2. Using O–O single bond (146 kJ mol⁻¹) instead of O=O double bond (498 kJ mol⁻¹). Oxygen exists as O₂ with a double bond.
  3. Forgetting that bond enthalpy values are always positive (they represent energy required to BREAK a bond). The sign comes from the formula: broken (positive) minus formed (positive) gives the net ΔH.
  4. Not counting bonds in diatomic products correctly. Each CO₂ has TWO C=O bonds. Each H₂O has TWO O–H bonds.
  5. Confusing the direction of the formula. ΔH = Σ(broken) − Σ(formed). NOT Σ(formed) − Σ(broken).

5. Calculating Mean Bond Enthalpies from Atomisation Data

Key Concepts

When atomisation enthalpies are given for several molecules, you can work backwards to determine individual bond enthalpies.

Approach: The atomisation enthalpy of a compound equals the total of all its bond enthalpies (since atomisation breaks ALL bonds to give individual gaseous atoms).

For a molecule with bonds of type A, B, C...: ΔHat(compound) = n_A × BE(A) + n_B × BE(B) + n_C × BE(C) + ...

By comparing atomisation enthalpies of related molecules, you can isolate individual bond enthalpies.

Worked Example: C–C Bond from Atomisation Data (Jun 2022 Q18(c))

Question: Use the following atomisation enthalpies to calculate the C–C bond enthalpy.

Species Atomisation Enthalpy (kJ mol⁻¹) Products
H₂O(l) 928 2H(g) + O(g)
CH₄(g) 1740 C(g) + 4H(g)
CH₃OH(g) 2105 C(g) + 4H(g) + O(g)
C₂H₅OH(g) 3322 2C(g) + 6H(g) + O(g)

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Find the O–H bond enthalpy from H₂O.

H₂O → 2H(g) + O(g) This breaks 2 O–H bonds. ΔHat(H₂O) = 2 × BE(O–H) 928 = 2 × BE(O–H) BE(O–H) = 928 / 2 = 464 kJ mol⁻¹

Step 2: Find the C–H bond enthalpy from CH₄.

CH₄ → C(g) + 4H(g) This breaks 4 C–H bonds. ΔHat(CH₄) = 4 × BE(C–H) 1740 = 4 × BE(C–H) BE(C–H) = 1740 / 4 = 435 kJ mol⁻¹

Step 3: Find the C–O bond enthalpy from CH₃OH.

CH₃OH → C(g) + 4H(g) + O(g) This breaks: 3 C–H bonds + 1 C–O bond + 1 O–H bond. ΔHat(CH₃OH) = 3 × BE(C–H) + BE(C–O) + BE(O–H) 2105 = (3 × 435) + BE(C–O) + 464 2105 = 1305 + BE(C–O) + 464 2105 = 1769 + BE(C–O) BE(C–O) = 2105 − 1769 = 336 kJ mol⁻¹

Step 4: Find the C–C bond enthalpy from C₂H₅OH.

C₂H₅OH → 2C(g) + 6H(g) + O(g) This breaks: 1 C–C bond + 5 C–H bonds + 1 C–O bond + 1 O–H bond. ΔHat(C₂H₅OH) = BE(C–C) + 5 × BE(C–H) + BE(C–O) + BE(O–H) 3322 = BE(C–C) + (5 × 435) + 336 + 464 3322 = BE(C–C) + 2175 + 336 + 464 3322 = BE(C–C) + 2975 BE(C–C) = 3322 − 2975 = 347 kJ mol⁻¹

Answer: C–C bond enthalpy = 347 kJ mol⁻¹

Alternative shortcut method: Compare C₂H₅OH and CH₃OH directly.

C₂H₅OH has 1 more C atom than CH₃OH, which means: 1 extra C–C bond + 2 extra C–H bonds.

Difference in atomisation = 3322 − 2105 = 1217 kJ mol⁻¹ BE(C–C) + 2 × BE(C–H) = 1217 BE(C–C) + 2(435) = 1217 BE(C–C) = 1217 − 870 = 347 kJ mol⁻¹ ✓

Common Pitfalls for Atomisation Calculations

  1. Not identifying which bonds are broken. Write out the full structural formula and count every bond systematically.
  2. Working in the wrong order. Start with the simplest molecule (H₂O for O–H, CH₄ for C–H) and build up.
  3. Arithmetic errors. These multi-step calculations have many opportunities for arithmetic mistakes. The mark scheme allows TE at each stage.

6. Experimental Error and Uncertainty Evaluation

Key Concepts

Percentage uncertainty in a calculated result:

For calorimetry, the main source of uncertainty is usually the temperature measurement.

If ΔT = T_final − T_initial, and each reading has uncertainty ±δ:

Total uncertainty in ΔT = ±(δ + δ) = ±2δ

% uncertainty in ΔT = (2δ / ΔT) × 100%

If the calculation involves multiplying or dividing quantities, add the percentage uncertainties:

% uncertainty in q = % uncertainty in m + % uncertainty in c + % uncertainty in ΔT (Usually only ΔT has significant uncertainty; m and c are precise.)

Percentage error vs data book value:

% error = |experimental value − data book value| / |data book value| × 100%

Note: The data book value is ALWAYS the denominator.

Key Sources of Error in Calorimetry

Source of Error Effect on ΔH (combustion) Why
Heat loss to surroundings Less exothermic Some heat from the reaction does not reach the water
Incomplete combustion Less exothermic Not all fuel burns completely; less energy released per mole burned
Evaporation of fuel Less exothermic Some fuel evaporates without burning; the "mass burned" includes unburned fuel
Heat capacity of calorimeter Less exothermic The calorimeter absorbs some heat; q calculated from water only is too low
Not all fuel burned N/A (not an error) If you use the mass actually burned in the calculation, this is not an error
Using wrong specific heat capacity Either direction Using 4.18 instead of 3.85 for acid solution gives larger

Evaluating the Effect of Changes

Proportional reasoning (Jan 2023 Q1(b)):

If both the mass of fuel and the volume of water increase by the same factor (e.g., ×1.5), the temperature change ΔT remains THE SAME.

Reasoning: - More fuel → more energy released (q increases by ×1.5) - More water → more mass to heat (q = mcΔT; m increases by ×1.5) - Since q and m both increase by ×1.5, and c is constant, ΔT stays the same.

Effect of calorimeter heat capacity (Oct 2023 Q18(b)(iii)):

If the calorimeter absorbs some heat, the total energy released by the reaction is: q_total = q_water + q_calorimeter

If you only measure q_water, you underestimate the total energy released. For an exothermic reaction: the true ΔH is MORE negative (more exothermic) than calculated.

Common Pitfalls for Error and Uncertainty

  1. Halving instead of doubling uncertainty for ΔT. ΔT uses TWO readings (initial and final), so the uncertainty is 2 × single reading uncertainty.
  2. Using experimental value as denominator in % error. Always use the data book / accepted value.
  3. Not explaining the direction of the error. "Heat loss" alone may score 1 mark. "Heat loss to surroundings makes ΔH less exothermic" scores 2 marks.
  4. Suggesting irrelevant errors. "Not all fuel burned" is not an error if the mass actually burned was used. "No lid" and "not stirred" are often too vague to score.

7. Bond Enthalpy as an Indicator of Reactivity

Key Concepts

Principle: A weaker bond has a lower bond enthalpy and is easier to break. In reactions where the rate-determining step involves bond breaking, a weaker bond generally leads to a faster reaction.

Application to halogenoalkane hydrolysis:

C–X Bond Bond Enthalpy (kJ mol⁻¹) Relative Reactivity
C–F 485 Slowest
C–Cl 340 Slow
C–Br 280 Fast
C–I 240 Fastest

The trend in reactivity follows the INVERSE of the bond enthalpy trend: weaker bond → faster reaction.

However, bond enthalpy is not the only factor. The structure of the halogenoalkane also matters:

Structure Mechanism Relative Rate
Tertiary SN1 (carbocation intermediate) Fast
Secondary SN1/SN2 (mixed) Medium
Primary SN2 (back-side attack) Slow

When comparing halogenoalkanes with different structures AND different halogens, both factors must be considered.

In Jun 2022 Q17(b), comparing 2-chloro-2-methylpropane (tertiary C–Cl) with 1-iodobutane (primary C–I): - Structure favours the tertiary one (SN1, fast) - Bond enthalpy favours the iodo one (C–I weaker, fast) - The two factors OPPOSE each other → relative rate cannot be predicted

In Jun 2024 Q16(b)(iii), comparing 2-iodo-2-methylpropane (tertiary C–I) with 1-chlorobutane (primary C–Cl): - Structure favours the tertiary one (SN1) - Bond enthalpy favours the iodo one (C–I weaker) - Both factors agree → 2-iodo-2-methylpropane is definitely faster

Worked Example: Evaluating Competing Factors

Question: Can you predict which reacts faster with aqueous silver nitrate: 1-chlorobutane or 2-iodo-2-methylpropane? Explain.

Answer:

Yes, 2-iodo-2-methylpropane reacts faster, for TWO reasons:

  1. Bond enthalpy: The C–I bond (240 kJ mol⁻¹) is weaker than the C–Cl bond (340 kJ mol⁻¹). A weaker bond is broken more easily, leading to a lower activation energy and faster reaction.

  2. Structure: 2-iodo-2-methylpropane is a tertiary halogenoalkane, which reacts via the SN1 mechanism (involving a stable tertiary carbocation intermediate). 1-chlorobutane is primary and reacts via the slower SN2 mechanism.

Both factors (weaker bond AND more favourable mechanism) make 2-iodo-2-methylpropane the faster-reacting compound.

Contrast with the Jun 2022 example: When the structural factor and the bond enthalpy factor work in OPPOSITE directions (e.g., tertiary C–Cl vs primary C–I), you CANNOT predict which is faster without experimental data.

Limitations of Bond Enthalpy Data

When asked to evaluate the reliability of bond enthalpy calculations (Jun 2023 Q20(a)(ii), Jan 2024 Q24(a)(ii)):

  1. Bond enthalpies are mean/averaged values. The actual bond strength varies between molecules depending on the local chemical environment. For example, a C–H bond in methane may have a slightly different strength from a C–H bond in ethane.

  2. Bond enthalpy data is for the gas phase. If any reactant or product is a liquid or solid, the enthalpy change of vaporisation/sublimation must also be accounted for. Bond enthalpy calculations ignore this.

  3. Specific structural differences are not captured. In S₈, the S–S bonds are in an 8-membered ring, which may have different bond properties from S–S bonds in other molecules.

8. Enthalpy of Combustion Trend and Extrapolation

Key Concepts

For a homologous series of alkanes, the enthalpy of combustion becomes more negative (more exothermic) as the carbon chain length increases. This is because each additional CH₂ group contributes a roughly constant amount of additional energy release.

Approximate increment per CH₂ group: ~650–660 kJ mol⁻¹

This can be used to extrapolate to an unknown alkane.

Worked Example: Extrapolating Combustion Data (Oct 2023 Q3)

Question: Given the following standard enthalpy changes of combustion for alkanes:

Alkane ΔH꜀ (kJ mol⁻¹)
C₃H₈ −2219
C₄H₁₀ −2877
C₅H₁₂ −3509
C₆H₁₄ −4163
C₇H₁₆ −4817
C₈H₁₈ −5470

Which alkane is most likely to have ΔH꜀ = −6125 kJ mol⁻¹?

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Calculate the increment per CH₂ group.

From C₃ to C₈ (5 increments for 5 CH₂ groups added): Total change = −5470 − (−2219) = −3251 kJ mol⁻¹ Increment per CH₂ = −3251 / 5 = −650 kJ mol⁻¹

(Alternatively, check individual increments: 2877−2219 = 658; 3509−2877 = 632; 4163−3509 = 654; 4817−4163 = 654; 5470−4817 = 653. Average ≈ 630.)

Step 2: Calculate how many CH₂ increments from a known value.

From C₈H₁₈ (ΔH = −5470) to −6125: Difference = −6125 − (−5470) = −655 kJ mol⁻¹ Number of CH₂ increments = 655 / 650 ≈ 1.01

This is approximately 1 extra CH₂ group → C₉H₂₀ (nonane).

Check from C₇: From C₇H₁₆ (ΔH = −4817): Difference = −6125 − (−4817) = −1308 Increments = 1308 / 630 ≈ 2.08 → about 2 CH₂ groups → C₉H₂₀ ✓

Answer: C₉H₂₀ (nonane)

This tests pattern recognition rather than recall — a higher-order MCQ skill.

9. Percentage Efficiency Calculation

Key Concepts

In some contexts (e.g., engine efficiency), the useful energy obtained from a fuel is less than the theoretical maximum from its enthalpy of combustion.

Percentage efficiency = (useful energy output / total energy input) × 100

Where: - Useful energy output = energy actually used for work - Total energy input = n(fuel) × |ΔH꜀|

Worked Example: Engine Efficiency (Jun 2023 Q20(a)(iii))

Question: 11.0 kJ of useful energy is obtained from burning 1.00 g of octane (C₈H₁₈, Mᵣ = 114). The standard enthalpy change of combustion of octane is −5470 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the percentage efficiency.

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1: Calculate moles of octane burned.

n = mass / Mᵣ = 1.00 / 114 = 8.77 × 10⁻³ mol

Step 2: Calculate total energy available from combustion.

Total energy = n × |ΔH꜀| = 8.77 × 10⁻³ × 5470 = 48.0 kJ

Step 3: Calculate percentage efficiency.

From the mark scheme: % efficiency = 100 × 11 / (1.00 × 5470 / 114) = 100 × 11 × 114 / (1.00 × 5470) = 125,400 / 5,470 = 22.9% (or 23%)

Answer: 22.9% (or 23%)

Common Pitfalls for Efficiency

  1. Forgetting to convert mass to moles before multiplying by |ΔH꜀|.
  2. Forgetting absolute value. ΔH꜀ is negative, but the energy INPUT is positive (you release 5470 kJ per mole).
  3. Confusing efficiency with % error. Efficiency = useful/total; error = |experimental − true|/true.

Full-Mark Answer Pattern: Calorimetry Calculation (4–5 marks)

Step 1: Heat energy transferred
  q = m × c × ΔT = [mass of solution in g] × 4.18 × [temperature change in °C]
  (Units: J — leave as J initially)

Step 2: Moles of substance that reacted
  n = [mass in g] / Mᵣ
  OR n = c × V (from titration context)

Step 3: Enthalpy change per mole
  ΔH = q / n
  Convert J → kJ by dividing by 1000

Step 4: Sign and units
  Exothermic = NEGATIVE sign
  Units = kJ mol⁻¹
  2–3 significant figures (1 SF is penalised)

Full-Mark Answer Pattern: Hess's Law Calculation

Method A (using formation data):
  ΔHr = ΣΔHf(products) − ΣΔHf(reactants)

Method B (using combustion data):
  ΔHr = ΣΔHc(reactants) − ΣΔHc(products)
  (Note: reversed compared to formation method)

Method C (cycle completion):
  1. Draw the cycle with arrows
  2. Assign signs to each arrow (same direction = same sign as given; reversed = opposite sign)
  3. Sum all arrows around the cycle = 0
  4. Solve for unknown

KEY TRAP: When an arrow is reversed, the sign FLIPS.
  e.g. ΔHf = −365.6 → arrow pointing AWAY from NH₄NO₃ = +365.6

5.1 Enthalpy Level Diagram (2–3 marks)

Papers: Jun 2022 Q18(a)(iii), Jan 2024 Q22(a)

Requirements for full marks:

3. Downward arrow between them labelled ΔH
4. Optional: "reactants" and "products" labels on each line
5. NO activation energy hump (unless specifically asked for reaction profile)

5.2 Percentage Uncertainty (1 mark)

Papers: Jun 2022 Q18(b)(ii), Jan 2023 Q1(a), Jun 2024 Q11(c)

% uncertainty = (total uncertainty in measurement / measured value) × 100

For temperature change (ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial):
  Total uncertainty = uncertainty in Tfinal + uncertainty in Tinitial
  (BOTH readings contribute to uncertainty)

Example: ±0.05 °C each → total = ±0.10 °C
  % uncertainty = (0.10 / ΔT) × 100

5.3 Experimental Error Explanation (2 marks)

Papers: Jun 2022 Q18(b)(iii), Jun 2023 Q20(a)(iv), Oct 2023 Q18(b)(iii)

When experimental ΔH is LESS EXOTHERMIC than data book value:
  ✓ Heat loss to surroundings
  ✓ Incomplete combustion
  ✓ Evaporation of fuel (e.g. ethanol)
  ✓ Heat absorbed by calorimeter/beaker (not accounted for)

NOT accepted:
  ✗ "Not all fuel burned" (mass used, not theoretical, is in calculation)
  ✗ "No lid on beaker" (too vague)
  ✗ "Water not stirred" (insufficient)

5.4 Thermometric Titration Analysis (3–6 marks)

Papers: Oct 2023 Q18(b)(i), Jun 2024 Q17(a–c)

Step 1: Draw two lines of best fit on the graph
  - Pre-endpoint: extrapolate the initial temperature rise
  - Post-endpoint: extrapolate the temperature plateau/decline
  - Find the intersection = endpoint volume and extrapolated ΔT

Step 2: Read values from the graph
  - End-point volume (±0.5 cm³ tolerance typical)
  - Maximum extrapolated temperature (±0.1 °C tolerance)

Step 3: Calculate moles from the endpoint
  n(acid or base at endpoint) = c × V

Step 4: Calculate ΔH
  q = [total mass of both solutions] × 4.18 × ΔT
  ΔH = −q / n (negative for exothermic neutralisation)

9. Types of Energetics Questions by Section

Section A (MCQ) — Average 3–4 marks per paper

Energetics MCQs are focused on: - Definition recall: ΔHf, atomisation, standard conditions (1 mark each) - Calculation-based reasoning: % uncertainty, proportional ΔT, wrong molar mass effect - Hess's Law logic: sign changes when reversing equations, multiplying coefficients

MCQ Strategy: - For definition questions, check THREE things: (1) 1 mol, (2) elements in standard states, (3) correct physical states - For uncertainty questions, ALWAYS double the individual uncertainty for ΔT (two readings) - For proportional reasoning, track how BOTH variables change simultaneously

This is where the main calorimetry and Hess's Law questions appear: - 4–5 mark calorimetry calculation (appears in 6/7 papers) - Hess's Law cycle completion/calculation (appears in 5/7 papers) - Bond enthalpy calculation (occasional, 3–4 marks) - Experimental error/uncertainty (1–2 marks)

10. Mark Scheme Carry-Forward (Error Carried Forward) Rules

From analysis of all 7 mark schemes:

Where TE is applied: - Multi-step calculations: if q is wrong but ΔH = q/n is correct using that q → full marks for later steps - Hess's Law cycles: if a value placed in the cycle is wrong but the calculation using it is correct → score calculation marks - Bond enthalpy: if one bond energy is wrong but the total Σ is calculated correctly from it → score for the total

Where TE is NOT applied: - Signs (+/−): wrong sign is penalised independently - Significant figures: only 1 SF is penalised; otherwise any reasonable SF is accepted - Units: penalised once per paper (not per question)
