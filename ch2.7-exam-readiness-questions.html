Intermolecular Forces, Redox Chemistry, and Groups 1, 2 and 7

Q1 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Describe how London forces arise between molecules.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Describe

Answer:

An instantaneous/temporary dipole arises from the asymmetrical distribution of electrons in a molecule. This induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule. The attraction between the instantaneous dipole and the induced dipole is a London force.

Q2 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Explain why a straight-chain alkane has stronger London forces than a branched isomer with the same molecular formula.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

The straight-chain isomer has greater surface area, allowing more extensive London force interactions between molecules.

Q3 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Explain, in terms of electrons, why larger halogen molecules (e.g. I₂) have higher boiling points than smaller ones (e.g. Cl₂).

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Larger halogen molecules have more electrons → larger electron cloud → more easily polarised → stronger London forces → more energy needed to overcome intermolecular forces → higher boiling point.

Q4 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Draw two adjacent molecules to show how an instantaneous dipole induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule. Label the instantaneous dipole and the induced dipole, and show the partial charges (δ⁺ and δ⁻).

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Draw

Answer:

Two molecules drawn adjacent. First molecule labelled "instantaneous (temporary) dipole" with δ⁺ on one end and δ⁻ on the other (due to asymmetric electron distribution). Second molecule labelled "induced dipole" with δ⁺ facing the δ⁻ of the first molecule and δ⁻ facing the δ⁺ of the first molecule. An arrow or label showing "attraction" between the opposite partial charges.

Q5 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

Explain why saying "more electrons = stronger forces" alone is insufficient for explaining London force trends, and state what additional concept is required.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

"More electrons" alone is insufficient because the mark scheme also requires the concept of polarisability. The full chain is: more electrons → larger electron cloud → more easily polarised → stronger London forces. Without "more easily polarised", the explanation is incomplete.

Q6 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

Which of the following compounds has ONLY London forces between its molecules: HF, OF₂, CF₄, PF₃?

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Name

Answer:

CF₄. CF₄ is tetrahedral and symmetrical → bond dipoles cancel → no permanent dipole → London forces only.

Q7 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Explain the difference between BF₃ and NH₃ in terms of the intermolecular forces they experience.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

BF₃ is trigonal planar and symmetrical, so the bond dipoles cancel, making it non-polar — it has only London forces. NH₃ is trigonal pyramidal (due to the lone pair), so it is polar — it has London forces, permanent dipole–dipole forces, and hydrogen bonding.

Q8 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Explain why propanal has a higher boiling point than butane, even though butane has more electrons.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Propanal has a C=O bond that creates permanent dipole–dipole forces, which are stronger than the London forces in butane. The additional permanent dipole–dipole forces in propanal outweigh the slightly stronger London forces in butane.

Q9 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

Define the term hydrogen bonding.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Define

Answer:

Hydrogen bonding is a strong type of intermolecular force that occurs when a hydrogen atom is covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (F, O, or N) and is attracted to a lone pair of electrons on a highly electronegative atom (F, O, or N) in a different molecule.

Q10 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Explain why (CH₃)₃COH can form hydrogen bonds but CH₃COCH₃ (propanone) cannot, despite both containing oxygen.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

(CH₃)₃COH has an O–H bond where H is covalently bonded to the electronegative O atom, allowing hydrogen bonding to a lone pair on O of a different molecule. CH₃COCH₃ has a C=O bond but no O–H bond; the H atoms are bonded to C, which is not electronegative enough for hydrogen bonding.

Q11 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Describe what a correct diagram showing a hydrogen bond between a methanol molecule and a water molecule should include.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Draw

Answer:

The diagram must show: (1) The O—H bond of one molecule (H bonded to O, NOT to C); (2) A dashed line from H to the O atom of the other molecule, representing the hydrogen bond; (3) The O—H⋯O angle approximately 180°; (4) The lone pair on the O atom accepting the hydrogen bond should be indicated; (5) Partial charges δ⁺ on H and δ⁻ on O.

Q12 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

State the two elements that must be present in a molecule for hydrogen bonding to occur.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

State

Answer:

Hydrogen bonded to fluorine, oxygen, or nitrogen (any two of F, O, N).

Q13 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

State whether a molecule with a C–H bond can participate in hydrogen bonding.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

State

Answer:

No. Carbon is not electronegative enough; hydrogen bonding requires H bonded to F, O or N.

Q14 QFR 6 marks · ~12 min

Compare the intermolecular forces in H₂ and NH₃. In your answer, you should include the types of forces present, how they arise, and explain which substance liquefies more easily.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Compare

Answer:

(1) Both form London forces. (2) Temporary dipole induces dipole in adjacent molecule. (3) Weaker attraction in H₂ (electron cloud less easily polarised). (4) NH₃ forms hydrogen bonds (N very electronegative). (5) Hydrogen bond between N lone pair and δ⁺H of different molecule. (6) NH₃ liquefies more easily (due to stronger hydrogen bonding compared to London forces in H₂).

Q15 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

State which compound has the higher boiling point: butan-1-ol or butane-1,4-diol. Give a reason.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

State

Answer:

Butane-1,4-diol — two hydrogen-bonding sites per molecule (more hydrogen bonding).

Q16 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Explain why propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) is viscous at room temperature.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Three OH groups → can form up to 3 hydrogen bonds per molecule → strong intermolecular forces → high viscosity.

Q17 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Pentan-1-ol (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂OH) has a higher boiling point than 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol ((CH₃)₃CCH₂OH). Explain why.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Both compounds have hydrogen bonding (both have one –OH group). The difference is due to London forces. Pentan-1-ol has a straight chain (greater surface area) and therefore stronger London forces than the branched 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol.

Q18 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

Arrange HF, HCl, HBr, and HI in order of increasing boiling temperature. Explain your answer.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

HCl < HBr < HI < HF. All hydrogen halides have London forces. London force strength increases with increasing number of electrons from HCl to HI. Only HF has hydrogen bonding. Hydrogen bonding is much stronger than London forces, so HF has the highest boiling point despite having the fewest electrons.

Q19 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

A student claims that HBr must have a higher boiling point than HF because it has more electrons and therefore stronger intermolecular forces. Evaluate this claim with reference to the types and strengths of intermolecular forces involved.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Justify

Answer:

The student's claim is incorrect. While it is true that HBr has more electrons than HF and therefore stronger London forces, HF also has hydrogen bonding (H bonded to F, the most electronegative element). Hydrogen bonding is much stronger than London forces. Therefore HF has a higher boiling point than HBr despite having fewer electrons. The student failed to consider that different TYPES of intermolecular force can override electron count comparisons.

Q20 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

For a 2-mark boiling point explanation question, why is writing "stronger intermolecular forces" alone NOT sufficient for full marks?

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Write

Answer:

Because the mark scheme explicitly requires specifying WHICH type of intermolecular force (e.g. "stronger London forces" not just "stronger intermolecular forces") AND the reason (e.g. "due to greater surface area" or "more electrons"). Writing "stronger IMF" without naming the type is penalised.

Q21 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Compare the intermolecular forces in hexane with those in water.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Compare

Answer:

Hexane has only London forces between its molecules (non-polar molecule). Water has hydrogen bonding between its molecules (H bonded to O), which is the strongest type of intermolecular force. Water also has London forces and permanent dipole–dipole forces, but hydrogen bonding is the strongest and most significant.

Q22 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

Explain why an ionic compound such as NaCl dissolves readily in water but not in hexane. Refer to the intermolecular forces involved.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Water is polar and forms ion-dipole interactions with Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, which are strong enough to separate the ions from the ionic lattice. Hexane is non-polar and can only form London forces, which are too weak to overcome the strong ionic bonds in NaCl.

Q23 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

Draw a labelled diagram showing the ion-dipole interactions between Mg²⁺, SO₄²⁻ and water molecules when MgSO₄ dissolves.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Draw

Answer:
  • For Mg²⁺ (positive ion): the δ⁻ oxygen atom of water molecules points TOWARDS the Mg²⁺ ion; draw 4–6 water molecules surrounding Mg²⁺ with O atoms nearest.
  • For SO₄²⁻ (negative ion): the δ⁺ hydrogen atoms of water molecules point TOWARDS the SO₄²⁻ ion; draw water molecules with H atoms nearest the SO₄²⁻.
  • Must show: δ⁺ on H atoms and δ⁻ on O atom (at least once); correct orientation (O of H₂O near cation, H of H₂O near anion). Full charges (+/−) are NOT correct — these are partial charges.
Q24 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

State which end of a water molecule (H or O) is attracted to a negative ion (anion) when an ionic compound dissolves.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

State

Answer:

The δ⁺ hydrogen atoms of water are attracted to negative ions (anions).

Q25 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

For an ion-dipole diagram question, what must you show and why?

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

N

Answer:

You must show δ⁺ and δ⁻ (partial charges) on water molecules at least once, because full positive/negative charges are NOT correct — ion-dipole interactions involve partial charges on the polar water molecule. Marks are deducted if full charges are shown.

Q26 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Define the term oxidising agent.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Define

Answer:

An oxidising agent is a species that causes oxidation by gaining electrons (it is itself reduced). Its oxidation number decreases.

Q27 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

In the reaction Ca + Cl₂ → CaCl₂, explain why this is a redox reaction. Include the oxidation number changes.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Ca is oxidised (0 → +2) AND Cl₂ is reduced (0 → −1). Both oxidation number changes must be shown to demonstrate that both oxidation and reduction occur.

Q28 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Explain the difference between the species being oxidised and the oxidising agent.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

The species being oxidised LOSES electrons and has its oxidation number INCREASED. The oxidising agent is the species that CAUSES oxidation by GAINING electrons; it is itself REDUCED. They are different species — the oxidising agent is NOT the species being oxidised.

Q29 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

State the oxidation number of oxygen in hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

State

Answer:

−1

Q30 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

State the oxidation number of hydrogen in sodium hydride, NaH.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

State

Answer:

−1

Q31 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

State the oxidation number of fluorine in any compound.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

State

Answer:

−1 (always −1 in all compounds).

Q32 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Calculate the oxidation number of vanadium in the ion VO²⁺.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Calculate

Answer:

O is −2. Let V = x. x + (−2) = +2, therefore x = +4. V = +4.

Q33 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Determine the oxidation number of Cl in ClO₂.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Determine

Answer:

O is −2. Let Cl = x. x + 2(−2) = 0, therefore x = +4. Cl = +4.

Q34 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Compare the oxidation numbers of chromium in K₂Cr₂O₇ and K₂CrO₄. Show your working.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Compare

Answer:

In K₂Cr₂O₇: 2(+1) + 2x + 7(−2) = 0 → 2x = +12 → Cr = +6. In K₂CrO₄: 2(+1) + x + 4(−2) = 0 → x = +6. Both have Cr = +6.

Q35 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

A student is given four compounds: HF, NaH, H₂O₂, and CH₄. (a) Deduce which compound does NOT contain any element with an oxidation number of −1. (b) For each of the remaining three compounds, identify the element with oxidation number −1 and explain why.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Deduce

Answer:

(a) CH₄ (C = −4, H = +1; no element at −1). (b) HF: F has oxidation number −1 (fluorine is always −1). NaH: H has oxidation number −1 (NaH is a metal hydride; Na = +1, so H = −1). H₂O₂: O has oxidation number −1 (H₂O₂ is a peroxide; H = +1, so O = −1).

Q36 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Work out the formula of potassium manganate(VI).

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Work

Answer:

Mn(VI) indicates Mn = +6. In the manganate ion: x + 4(−2) = −2 → x = +6. K is +1, so K₂MnO₄.

Q37 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Determine the formula of barium(II) ferrate(VI).

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Determine

Answer:

BaFeO₄. Ba²⁺ and FeO₄²⁻ with Fe = +6.

Q38 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

The Roman numerals in chlorate(V) and chlorate(VII) refer to the oxidation number of chlorine. Deduce the oxidation number of chlorine in NaClO₃ (chlorate(V)) and NaClO₄ (chlorate(VII)), showing your working.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Deduce

Answer:

In NaClO₃: +1 + x + 3(−2) = 0 → x = +5. Cl = +5 in chlorate(V). In NaClO₄: +1 + x + 4(−2) = 0 → x = +7. Cl = +7 in chlorate(VII). The Roman numerals indicate the oxidation number of chlorine (+5 and +7).

Q39 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

Define the term disproportionation as applied to a redox reaction.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Define

Answer:

Disproportionation is a redox reaction in which the same element is simultaneously oxidised and reduced. The oxidation numbers of this element in the products are both higher and lower than its oxidation number in the reactant.

Q40 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

For a 3-mark disproportionation explanation, what phrase is absolutely required that will NOT be accepted if you just write "redox"?

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Give

Answer:

Write "disproportionation" and state "the SAME element is both oxidised AND reduced". Writing just "redox" is explicitly ignored/penalised in mark schemes — the specific term "disproportionation" is required.

Q41 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

Explain why the reaction Cl₂ + H₂O → HClO + HCl is a disproportionation reaction.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

The same element (Cl) is both oxidised (0 → +1 in HClO) and reduced (0 → −1 in HCl). Since the same element undergoes both oxidation and reduction, this is disproportionation.

Q42 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Explain why the reaction IO₃⁻ + 5I⁻ + 6H⁺ → 3I₂ + 3H₂O is NOT a disproportionation reaction.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

I in IO₃⁻ = +5 (reduced to 0 in I₂); I in I⁻ = −1 (oxidised to 0 in I₂). Not disproportionation because two DIFFERENT species are oxidised and reduced — not the SAME species.

Q43 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

Chlorine dioxide, ClO₂, contains chlorine with an oxidation number of +4. (a) Write a balanced equation for the disproportionation of ClO₂ in alkaline solution to give ClO₂⁻ and ClO₃⁻. (b) Explain why this is a disproportionation reaction.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Deduce

Answer:

(a) 2ClO₂ + 2OH⁻ → ClO₂⁻ + ClO₃⁻ + H₂O (b) Cl is +4 in ClO₂; it is reduced to +3 in ClO₂⁻ AND oxidised to +5 in ClO₃⁻. The same element (Cl) is both oxidised and reduced.

Q44 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

In the reaction between chlorine and cold dilute sodium hydroxide: Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O, deduce which species is oxidised, which is reduced, and identify the oxidising agent and reducing agent.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Deduce

Answer:

Cl in Cl₂ (0) is oxidised to Cl in NaClO (+1) — oxidation number increases. Cl in Cl₂ (0) is reduced to Cl in NaCl (−1) — oxidation number decreases. Cl₂ is both the oxidising agent (gains electrons to become Cl⁻) and the reducing agent (loses electrons to become ClO⁻). This is a disproportionation reaction.

Q45 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

Show that MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + Cl₂ + 2H₂O is a redox reaction by assigning oxidation numbers and identifying what is oxidised and what is reduced.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Show

Answer:

Mn in MnO₂: +4; Mn in MnCl₂: +2 (reduced, gains 2 electrons). Cl in HCl: −1; Cl in Cl₂: 0 (oxidised, loses 1 electron per Cl atom). Since oxidation numbers change, this is a redox reaction. Manganese is reduced and chlorine is oxidised.

Q46 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Write the two half-equations for the reaction between MnO₂ and HCl, given that the products are Mn²⁺, Cl₂, and H₂O.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Write

Answer:

Reduction: MnO₂ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 2H₂O; Oxidation: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.

Q47 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

A student writes the following half-equation for the reduction of MnO₂ in acidic conditions: MnO₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + H₂O. Identify the error in this half-equation and write the correct balanced version.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

The equation is not balanced for oxygen or hydrogen. There are 2 oxygen atoms on the left but only 1 on the right. The correct balanced half-equation is: MnO₂ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 2H₂O (2 H₂O needed to balance oxygen, 4H⁺ needed to balance hydrogen).

Q48 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

Sodium chlorate(I) solution is left to stand and slowly disproportionates. (a) Write the oxidation and reduction half-equations for this disproportionation. (b) By multiplying and combining the half-equations, deduce the full ionic equation.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Deduce

Answer:

(a) Reduction (+1 → −1): ClO⁻ + H₂O + 2e⁻ → Cl⁻ + 2OH⁻. Oxidation (+1 → +5): ClO⁻ + 4OH⁻ → ClO₃⁻ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻. (b) Multiply the reduction half-equation by 2 (to give 4e⁻): 2ClO⁻ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + 4OH⁻. Add to the oxidation half-equation and cancel: 3ClO⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻.

Q49 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

State how the reactivity of Group 2 metals changes down the group.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

State

Answer:

Reactivity increases down the group.

Q50 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

Explain why calcium reacts more vigorously with water than magnesium. In your answer, refer to electronic structure.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Ca has a larger atomic radius and the outer electron is more effectively shielded by more inner electron shells. This means less energy is needed to remove the outer electron (lower first ionisation energy), so calcium reacts more vigorously than magnesium.

Q51 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

Explain why Na⁺ forms a stable peroxide (Na₂O₂) but Li⁺ does not.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Larger ionic radius of Na⁺ → lower charge density → polarises O₂²⁻ less → stabilises the peroxide ion. Li⁺ has too high charge density and distorts the peroxide ion excessively, making it unstable.

Q52 QFR 1 mark · ~2 min

State how the thermal stability of Group 2 carbonates changes down the group.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

State

Answer:

Thermal stability increases down the group.

Q53 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

Explain why Mg(NO₃)₂ is LESS thermally stable than Ca(NO₃)₂.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Mg²⁺ is smaller → higher charge density → more polarising → N–O bonds weakened more → less energy to break → decomposes more easily.

Q54 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

For a QWC question on thermal decomposition trends, what is the full polarising power explanation chain you must include?

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Write

Answer:

Write the chain in order: (1) M²⁺ ionic radius increases down Group 2; (2) Charge density (charge/size ratio) decreases; (3) Polarising power decreases; (4) The cation distorts/polarises the anion less; (5) The bonds within the anion are weakened less; (6) More energy is required to decompose; (7) Thermal stability increases.

Q55 QFR 6 marks · ~12 min

Evaluate the diagonal relationship between Li and Mg by comparing the thermal decomposition of their nitrates with that of NaNO₃.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Evaluate

Answer:

4LiNO₃ → 2Li₂O + 4NO₂ + O₂ and 2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂ + O₂ — both give the oxide, NO₂, and O₂. NaNO₃ → NaNO₂ + ½O₂ — gives the nitrite and O₂ (no NO₂). Li behaves like Group 2 because Li⁺/Mg²⁺ are smaller than Na⁺ → greater charge density → polarise nitrate more → weaken N–O bond → decompose to give NO₂.

Q56 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

Explain why the thermal decomposition of Group 2 nitrates produces the oxide, NO₂, and O₂, whereas Group 1 nitrates (except lithium) produce the nitrite and O₂.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Group 2 cations (M²⁺) have a higher charge than Group 1 cations (M⁺). This gives Group 2 cations a greater charge density and greater polarising power. The greater polarising power more strongly distorts the nitrate anion, weakening the N–O bonds more. This allows the nitrate to fully decompose (breaking N–O bonds completely to give the oxide and NO₂). Group 1 cations (except Li⁺) have lower charge density, so they polarise the nitrate less and only the N–O bond to oxygen is weakened enough for oxygen loss (giving the nitrite).

Q57 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

When 2.97 g of Mg(NO₃)₂ is thermally decomposed, 1200 cm³ of gas is collected at room temperature and pressure. Use this information to show that the molar gas volume is 24 000 cm³ mol⁻¹. (Molar mass of Mg(NO₃)₂ = 148.3 g mol⁻¹)

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Calculate

Answer:

2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂ + O₂ (5 mol gas per 2 mol nitrate). n(Mg(NO₃)₂) = 2.97/148.3 = 0.02003 mol. n(gas) = 5/2 × 0.02003 = 0.05008 mol. Molar volume = 1200/0.05008 = 23 962 ≈ 24 000 cm³ mol⁻¹.

Q64 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

25.0 cm³ of a saturated solution of Ca(OH)₂ required 18.95 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ HCl for neutralisation. Calculate the solubility of Ca(OH)₂ in g dm⁻³. (Molar mass of Ca(OH)₂ = 74.1 g mol⁻¹)

Q65 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Command Word

BaCO₃ dissolves in acid, whereas BaSO₄ is insoluble in acid. This ensures that any white precipitate observed is BaSO₄, confirming the presence of sulfate ions.

Explain why the boiling temperature increases down Group 7 from Cl₂ to I₂.

More electrons → larger electron cloud → more easily polarised → stronger London forces. (Not "stronger covalent bonds" — boiling involves overcoming intermolecular forces, not breaking covalent bonds.)

Q73 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Atomic radius increases down Group 7, shielding increases, and the ability to attract electrons (electronegativity) decreases. This means the halogen molecules become less able to gain an electron and act as oxidising agents.

Q74 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Answer:

Group 7 reactivity (as oxidising agents) DECREASES down the group. Group 2 reactivity INCREASES down the group. These are opposite trends: halogens get less reactive (less able to gain electrons) while Group 2 metals get more reactive (more easily lose electrons).

Q75 QFR 3 marks · ~6 min

Concentrated sulfuric acid is added to solid sodium bromide. Explain the redox reaction that occurs, identifying what is oxidised and what is reduced.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

Initially, NaBr + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HBr (acid-base reaction). Then HBr reduces H₂SO₄: H₂SO₄ + 2HBr → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O. Br⁻ (in HBr) is oxidised from −1 to 0 (in Br₂). S in H₂SO₄ is reduced from +6 to +4 (in SO₂). Products include Br₂ (red-brown fumes), SO₂ (colourless acidic gas), and H₂O.

Q76 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

Explain the difference between the products formed when concentrated H₂SO₄ reacts with NaCl compared with NaBr.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Explain

Answer:

With NaCl: no redox occurs; only HCl gas (white fumes) is produced. Cl⁻ is too weak a reducing agent to reduce H₂SO₄. With NaBr: a redox reaction occurs; Br₂ (red-brown fumes) and SO₂ (colourless gas) are produced. Br⁻ is a moderate reducing agent and reduces H₂SO₄ from S(+6) to S(+4).

Q77 QFR 5 marks · ~10 min

Three test tubes contain solid NaCl, NaBr, and NaI respectively. Concentrated sulfuric acid is added to each. (a) State the products formed with each halide. (b) Deduce the order of reducing power of the halide ions from your observations.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Deduce

Answer:

(a) NaCl: HCl(g) only (white fumes, no redox). NaBr: HBr(g), Br₂ (red-brown fumes), SO₂(g) (colourless gas). NaI: HI(g), I₂ (black solid), S (yellow solid), H₂S(g), SO₂(g). (b) Reducing power: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻. Evidence: I⁻ reduces H₂SO₄ the most (to S/H₂S), Br⁻ reduces it less (to SO₂ only), and Cl⁻ does not reduce it at all. The stronger the reducing agent, the more H₂SO₄ is reduced.

Q78 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

A student claims that adding concentrated H₂SO₄ to sodium chloride produces chlorine gas. (a) Deduce whether this claim is correct. (b) The student then suggests that to produce chlorine from NaCl, they should use a stronger acid. Evaluate this suggestion.

Q85 QFR 2 marks · ~4 min

In a titration, 10.00 cm³ of solution is measured using a pipette (±0.04 cm³) and the titre is 25.00 cm³ using a burette (±0.05 cm³ per reading). Calculate the total percentage uncertainty in the concentration.

Q88 QFR 4 marks · ~8 min

Bleach contains NaOCl. A student claims that mixing bleach with acid produces chlorine gas because "the acid reacts with the bleach." Justify this claim by writing a two-step mechanism showing how the toxic gas is produced, naming the type of reaction occurring.

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Justify

Answer:

Step 1: Adding H⁺ shifts HClO ⇌ H⁺ + ClO⁻ to the LEFT, forming HClO. Step 2: HClO + Cl⁻ + H⁺ → Cl₂ + H₂O. This is a redox (comproportionation) reaction. The Cl in ClO⁻ (oxidation number +1) is reduced to Cl₂ (0), and the Cl⁻ (−1) is oxidised to Cl₂ (0). The net result is formation of toxic Cl₂(g), which is dangerous to inhale.

Q89 QFR 5 marks · ~10 min

Chlorine gas is used to produce calcium hypochlorite for swimming pool treatment via the reaction: 2Ca(OH)₂ + 2Cl₂ → Ca(ClO)₂ + CaCl₂ + 2H₂O. (a) Calculate the oxidation number of Cl in Ca(ClO)₂ and in CaCl₂. (b) Is this reaction an example of disproportionation? Justify your answer using oxidation numbers. (c) A pool requires 7.16 kg of Ca(ClO)₂. Calculate the volume of Cl₂ gas needed at rtp. (M(Ca(ClO)₂) = 143.1 g mol⁻¹; molar volume = 24000 cm³ mol⁻¹)

Answer & Marking Criteria
Command Word

Analyse

Answer:

(a) In Ca(ClO)₂: Ca = +2, O = −2, so Cl = +1. In CaCl₂: Ca = +2, Cl = −1. (b) Yes, this is disproportionation. Chlorine starts at 0 (in Cl₂) and ends up at +1 (in Ca(ClO)₂, oxidised) and −1 (in CaCl₂, reduced). The SAME element (chlorine) is both oxidised AND reduced. (c) n(Ca(ClO)₂) = 7160 / 143.1 = 50.0 mol. From the equation, 2 mol Cl₂ produces 1 mol Ca(ClO)₂. n(Cl₂) = 50.0 × 2 = 100 mol. V(Cl₂) = 100 × 24000 = 2,400,000 cm³ = 2400 dm³.
